# Part I: The Battery Model

## Chapter 1. You Are Not a Calorie Machine

The food I grew up on didn't come with a number on it.

The beef came from a steer we raised. The eggs came from chickens that walked around outside. The tomatoes came out of dirt I had my hands in. None of it arrived in a box, and none of it told me anything about itself. There was no panel on the side of a tomato.

My friends' food did. Theirs came in bright packages with a little black-and-white grid printed on the back, and on that grid was a number. Calories. I remember wanting that food badly, and I remember that the number meant nothing to me at the time. It was just part of the packaging, like the cartoon on the front.

It took me a long time to understand something about that number that still bothers me.

That number is the only thing most people have ever been told about their food. It has become the unit we use to think about the body itself. Calories in, calories out. Burn more than you eat. The body as a furnace with a door on the front.

It is not that the number is wrong. A calorie is a real, measurable thing. It is that the number answers a much smaller question than we think it does.

### What a calorie actually measures

A calorie is a unit of heat. That is its entire definition.

One food Calorie, the kind printed on the label, is the amount of energy it takes to raise the temperature of one kilogram of water by one degree Celsius. Nothing about that definition mentions biology. It is a physics measurement, and it comes from a physics instrument.

In the late 1800s and early 1900s, an American chemist named Wilbur Atwater worked out how to put food numbers on a scientific footing. His method, in simplified form, was this. Take a sample of food. Dry it. Put it in a sealed steel container called a bomb calorimeter. Fill the container with pure oxygen. Set it on fire with an electric spark. Surround the whole thing with a measured amount of water, and see how much the water heats up.

That is where calories come from. We set food on fire and measured the warmth.

Atwater then worked out average values, roughly four calories per gram of protein, four per gram of carbohydrate, and nine per gram of fat, and those averages are still the basis of nearly every nutrition label in the world. *(Evidence: well established. The method and its limitations are textbook material.)*

Now hold that image in your mind. A steel bomb. Pure oxygen. A spark. Ash at the bottom.

Does that sound like what happens when you eat dinner?

### The same energy, two completely different fates

Put a log on a fire and a piece of food in a body, and something very different happens in each case, even if the calorimeter reads the same number.

The log burns. The chemical bonds in the wood break apart all at once, in an uncontrolled rush, and essentially all of that energy leaves as heat and light. When it is over you have warmth that has already escaped up the chimney, and a pile of ash. Nothing was built. Nothing was stored. Nothing was organized.

Your cells do something almost unrecognizably different with the same chemistry.

They take the food apart slowly, in dozens of small, carefully controlled steps, each one run by its own enzyme. They strip electrons off the food molecules and hand those electrons down a chain of proteins, one careful handoff at a time. At each step they capture a little of the released energy and use it to push charged particles across a membrane, building up a difference in charge that can be held and spent later.

Only at the very end does what is left leave as heat.

&#91;image: Figure 1.1. The same 100 calories, two different fates. A fire converts fuel to heat in a single uncontrolled step. A cell takes the same energy apart slowly and stores it as a charge across a membrane, which it can then spend on work, signaling and repair.\]

Same calories. Completely different outcome.

This is the first thing I want you to take from this book. A calorie counts the fuel. It does not tell you what the fuel was turned into. It cannot tell you whether the energy was captured or wasted, whether it was stored as a usable charge or leaked away as warmth, whether it arrived at a time your body was ready for it, or whether your cells even had the machinery in working order to use it.

The calorie is an accounting unit. It is not an explanation.

And if you want to understand your own energy, the accounting is not where the answer lives.

### The question underneath

So let me ask a better question.

You are made of roughly thirty trillion cells. Each one is a fantastically organized structure. Molecules are sorted into compartments. Ions sit at very specific concentrations, different inside the cell from outside. Proteins are folded into exact shapes. Membranes hold their contents apart from everything else.

Here is the strange part. None of that arrangement is stable.

Left alone, all of it falls apart. Molecules spread out. Concentrated things become dilute. Sorted things become mixed. Structure becomes sameness. This is not a flaw in biology. It is one of the most reliable rules in the universe, and it has a name: the second law of thermodynamics.

The usual way to state it is that entropy, which you can loosely think of as disorder or sameness, always increases in an isolated system. Put a drop of ink in water and it spreads. It never gathers itself back into a drop. Pour hot coffee in a cold room and the coffee cools. The room never spontaneously heats the coffee back up.

Everything in nature runs downhill toward sameness. *(Evidence: well established. This is physics, not a health theory.)*

And yet here you are. Organized. Sorted. Compartmentalized. Different on the inside than the outside. Holding an arrangement that the universe is constantly trying to erase.

Which raises the question that this entire book is really about.

What is holding you together?

### Order costs energy, continuously

The answer is that you are not an isolated system, and you never rest.

A sealed jar is an isolated system. Whatever is inside it will drift toward sameness and stay there. You are the opposite. You are an open system, with energy and matter flowing through you constantly. Food goes in. Oxygen goes in. Light falls on you. Heat and waste go out. That flow never stops, not for one second, from before you were born until the moment you die.

That flow is what pays for your organization.

In 1944, the physicist Erwin Schrödinger published a short book called *What Is Life?*, based on lectures he had given in Dublin the year before. He was not a biologist, and he got some details wrong, but he put his finger on the central puzzle more clearly than almost anyone before him. Living things, he argued, survive by continuously drawing order from their surroundings. He called it feeding on "negative entropy." The phrase is clumsy, and he apologized for it himself, but the idea underneath it is exactly right.

You do not eat to get warm. You eat to stay organized.

Decades later, the chemist Ilya Prigogine won a Nobel Prize for formalizing this idea. Certain structures, he showed, can exist only while energy flows through them. Stop the flow and the structure does not slowly wind down. It collapses. He called them dissipative structures. A whirlpool is one. A flame is one.

You are one. *(Evidence: well established. Schrödinger's 1944 lectures and Prigogine's 1977 Nobel work are foundational physics and physical chemistry.)*

### What death actually looks like

If you want proof that holding your organization costs continuous energy, look at what happens when the energy stops.

There is a moment after death that most people have heard of but few have thought about carefully. Rigor mortis. The body stiffens.

Here is why. Inside a muscle, the proteins that produce movement grip each other and then release, over and over. The gripping is easy. The releasing is what costs energy. A molecule called ATP has to bind to the gripping protein to make it let go.

When the supply of ATP runs out, the grip never releases. Every muscle in the body locks in place. *(Evidence: well established.)*

This is the most literal demonstration I know of that a living body is not coasting. Even letting go costs energy. Even relaxation is an active, powered process. The moment the supply stops, the body does not gently settle. It seizes.

And underneath that, something quieter and more important is happening. The carefully maintained differences between the inside and the outside of every cell begin to collapse. Ions that were held apart start mixing. Charge that was held across membranes drains away. Compartments that were separate become continuous.

Everything evens out.

That, biologically, is what death is. Not the absence of energy in the body. A body at the moment of death still contains plenty of chemical energy. It is the loss of the difference. The loss of the gradient. The loss of the separation that made the whole thing work.

Life is the maintenance of difference. Death is equilibrium.

### You are more energetically intense than a star

People tend to think of the body as a low-energy object. An adult uses somewhere around two thousand calories a day, which works out to about one hundred watts. The same as an old incandescent bulb. That sounds modest.

Run the comparison a different way and it stops sounding modest.

Take the sun's total power output and divide it by the sun's mass, and you get roughly two ten-thousandths of a watt per kilogram. Take your hundred watts and divide by your body weight, and you get somewhere near one and a half watts per kilogram.

Gram for gram, you are running at something like seven thousand times the energy intensity of the sun.

The sun is enormous. You are dense with activity. Every one of those trillions of cells is running thousands of chemical reactions per second, pumping ions uphill, rebuilding its own parts, holding its own charge. The reason you do not glow is not that little is happening. It is that the energy is being used for organization instead of being thrown off as light.

And the scale of that work is hard to take in. Your body holds only a small amount of ATP at any given moment. In a muscle working at maximum effort, the stored ATP is enough for a couple of seconds. Not minutes. Seconds. Everything past that has to be made on the spot.

Over a full day, the best estimates are that you build and spend roughly your own body weight in ATP. You do not carry a reserve. You run a refinery. *(Evidence: the watt calculations are arithmetic from well established figures. Whole-body ATP turnover is an estimate, and published figures vary.)*

So when someone tells you the body is just a calorie furnace, understand what is being left out. The furnace framing describes the fuel going in and the heat coming out. It says nothing about the enormous, unceasing, exquisitely organized work happening in between, which is the only part that is actually you.

### Why I call it a battery

I've used the word battery for this since long before I wrote a word of this book, and I want to be precise about what I mean, because the metaphor can be taken badly.

A battery works by holding charge apart. Positive on one side, negative on the other, separated by a barrier. That separation is stored potential. When you connect the two sides through a device, charge flows, and that flow does work.

Your cells do the same thing, and they do it with real electrical charge across real membranes. This is not a poetic comparison. It is physically what is happening. Every cell in your body holds a measurable voltage across its outer membrane, and inside the cell, your mitochondria hold another one across their inner membrane. Two batteries. We will look closely at both in the next chapter.

That is the useful part of the metaphor. Here is where it goes wrong, and I want to plant this early because a lot of health writing gets it backward.

A battery in a drawer is best when it is fully charged. A cell is not.

Your cells are not trying to reach maximum voltage. They are trying to hold the right voltage for what they are, and to change it on command. A nerve cell has to be able to drop its voltage in a fraction of a millisecond, because that drop is the signal. A mitochondrion sitting at very high charge with nothing to do does not become healthier. It starts leaking damaging molecules.

And there is another layer, which may turn out to be the most important idea in this entire book. A single cell holding a perfect voltage in isolation is not health. Health is tissue. It is millions of cells holding voltages that are appropriate for each of them and coordinated with their neighbors. Coherence, not maximum.

So when I say human battery, I do not mean charge yourself up as high as you can. I mean something more demanding. I mean a system that can build charge, spend it on real work, contain the damage that work creates, repair what wears out, and do all of that again on the right schedule.

That is five separate jobs.

### The five jobs

&#91;image: Figure 1.2. The Human Battery cycle. Charge, convert, protect, repair, and repeat. These are not health tips. They are the minimum requirements for a system that holds itself together against physics.\]

**CHARGE.** Capture energy from the environment. Food, oxygen, and, as you will see, light, which does something entirely different from what most people assume.

**CONVERT.** Turn that stored charge into biological work. Pumping ions. Building proteins. Contracting muscle. Firing a nerve. Thinking a thought.

**PROTECT.** Doing work creates damage. Reactive molecules, misfolded proteins, wear on the machinery. A system that produces energy without containing its byproducts destroys itself.

**REPAIR.** Rebuild what wore out. When a component is too far gone, dismantle it and recycle the parts. When an entire cell is too far gone, remove it cleanly before it harms its neighbors.

**REPEAT.** Do all of it again, timed correctly. Not randomly. On a schedule that matches the day, and the season, and the world outside your skin.

That last one is the one almost everyone misses, and it is the one I now think about most. It is not enough to do the right things. In biology, the same action performed at the wrong time is a different action. Eating at midnight is not the same event as eating at noon. Bright light at seven in the morning and bright light at eleven at night are not the same input. They are closer to opposites.

Break any one of the five, and the other four start to fail.

**What determines the electrical state of a human cell, and what allows the body to maintain the gradients it needs to stay organized?**

That question sounds technical. It is not. It is the practical question underneath almost everything people actually want to know about their health. Why am I tired at two in the afternoon. Why did my bloodwork drift the wrong way. Why can't I sleep. Why did recovery get harder. Why does the same amount of food land differently than it did ten years ago.

Once you see it that way, you stop needing someone to hand you a list. You start being able to work it out yourself.

You picked up this book thinking something reasonable. *My body needs energy.*

By the end I want you thinking something much more precise. *My biology depends on maintaining carefully regulated gradients of energy and information, synchronized with my environment, so my cells can produce energy, communicate, repair themselves, and stay organized.*

## Chapter 2. The Two Batteries

I started college at fifteen, and I studied cell biology at UCSD.

I learned the material. I could draw the electron transport chain from memory. I could name the complexes, trace the pathways, and reproduce the diagrams on an exam. Mitochondria. ATP. Cellular respiration.

And I want to be honest about something. At the time, they were just terms and pathways.

I knew that mitochondria made ATP the way I knew that Sacramento is the capital of California. It was a fact I could produce on demand. It was not something I felt was about me. Nothing in those years made me look down at my own hand and think, *there are trillions of tiny electrical generators running in there right now, and if they stop, I stop.*

That came much later, and it came from a very different direction. But the material was already in my head, waiting.

So let me teach it the way I wish someone had taught it to me. Not as pathways to memorize. As machinery you own.

### The currency: electrons, protons, and ions

Everything in this chapter comes down to three kinds of moving parts.

**Electrons.** These are the tiny negatively charged particles that orbit the nucleus of every atom. They are what "energy in food" actually means. When you eat, you are not swallowing energy in some abstract sense. You are swallowing molecules whose electrons sit in high-energy arrangements, and your body's entire strategy is to move those electrons to a lower-energy arrangement and capture what is released along the way.

There is a word for moving electrons around. Chemists call it redox, short for reduction and oxidation. The naming is unfortunately backward and has confused students for a century, so here is the only version you need. **Losing electrons is oxidation. Gaining electrons is reduction.** That is the whole vocabulary.

Food is electron-rich. Oxygen is electron-hungry. Your metabolism is, at bottom, a very long, very careful transfer of electrons from food to oxygen.

If you let that transfer happen in one step, you get fire. Food plus oxygen goes to carbon dioxide, water, and heat. That is combustion, and it is exactly what happens in the bomb calorimeter from Chapter 1.

Your body performs the same overall reaction. Same starting materials. Same end products. It just refuses to do it in one step.

**Protons.** A proton is a hydrogen atom that has lost its electron. What is left is a bare positive charge. Protons are small, they are everywhere in the watery interior of a cell, and, as you will see, moving them across a barrier is how your mitochondria store energy.

**Ions.** These are atoms carrying a net electrical charge. Four of them matter most for the story in this book: sodium, potassium, calcium, and chloride. You know them from a nutrition label. They belong in a physics discussion.

Here is the sentence I want you to carry forward. **Charge, biologically, means a separation of these particles that the body actively maintains.** Not stored heat. Not calories. A difference, in space, held in place at a cost.

Which means something has to do the separating.

### The invention that made all of this possible

Before there was a brain, or a heart, or a spine, there was a membrane.

The membrane is the oldest invention in biology, and arguably the most important. It is a double sheet of fatty molecules, each one with a water-loving head and a water-fearing tail. Drop enough of them in water and they arrange themselves automatically, tails tucked inward, heads facing out, forming a continuous flexible sheet about five nanometers thick.

That sheet does something no other structure in chemistry does as well. It creates an inside.

And because the inside of the sheet is oily, and charged particles do not dissolve in oil, that sheet is an outstanding electrical insulator. An ion cannot simply drift across it. It has to go through a dedicated protein doorway, and the cell controls those doorways.

Which means a cell can pile up sodium on one side and keep it there. It can hold potassium on the other side and keep it there. It can hold a difference.

A thin insulating sheet with different charge on each side is not merely *like* a capacitor. In physics, that is what a capacitor is. Cell membranes have a measured electrical capacitance of roughly one microfarad per square centimeter, a value so consistent across cell types that physiologists treat it as a constant. *(Evidence: well established.)*

So the moment life invented the membrane, it invented the ability to store charge. Everything else in this book is built on that.

### Battery one: the cell membrane

&#91;image: Figure 2.1. The plasma membrane battery. The sodium-potassium pump spends one ATP per cycle to move three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in. That pumping builds the gradients; potassium then leaking back out through open channels is what generates most of the resting voltage.\]

Look at the numbers in that figure, because they are startling once you notice them.

Outside your cells, sodium sits at about 145 millimoles per liter, and potassium at about 4. Inside, it is nearly the reverse. Potassium around 140, sodium around 12.

That is not a small difference. Potassium is roughly thirty-five times more concentrated inside your cells than outside. Sodium is roughly twelve times more concentrated outside than in. And your cells hold that arrangement steady, all day, every day, for your entire life.

The protein doing most of that work is called the sodium-potassium pump. A Danish scientist named Jens Christian Skou discovered it in 1957, and he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it forty years later. Its operation is simple to state. It grabs three sodium ions from inside the cell, spends one molecule of ATP, changes shape, and throws them out. Then it grabs two potassium ions from outside and brings them in.

Three out, two in. Notice that the numbers do not match.

That mismatch means the pump is not electrically neutral. Every single cycle moves one net positive charge out of the cell. Run that in billions of pumps across trillions of cells and the inside of every cell becomes slightly negative relative to the outside.

Now I want to be precise about something, because this is where a lot of popular writing goes wrong.

The pump's direct contribution to your cells' voltage is real, but small. Only a few millivolts. The pump's main job is to build and maintain the gradients. The voltage itself comes mostly from what happens next.

Your cell membrane has potassium channels that sit open at rest. Potassium, crowded inside, drifts out through them, following its own concentration gradient. But potassium carries positive charge, so every ion that leaves makes the inside a little more negative. And that growing negativity pulls back on the next potassium ion trying to leave.

Eventually the two forces balance. The chemical push outward equals the electrical pull inward, and the system settles at a stable voltage. Walther Nernst worked out the equation for this in the 1880s, and for potassium at typical human concentrations it predicts somewhere near minus ninety millivolts. Real cells sit a bit above that, usually around minus seventy in a nerve cell, because a small amount of sodium leaks the other way. *(Evidence: well established. This is core physiology, not interpretation.)*

So the honest sentence is this. **The pump does not create the voltage directly. It builds and holds the gradients, and the voltage emerges from ions moving down those gradients through controlled openings.**

That distinction matters for the whole book, because it is the first hint of something I will keep returning to. The voltage is not a thing the body sets like a dial. It is the running result of a balance between pumping and leaking, and that balance can be tuned, moment to moment, by opening and closing doors.

### What holding that charge costs you

Now consider the bill.

Every pump cycle costs one ATP. The pumps never stop. Not while you sleep, not while you sit still, not for a single second of your life.

In your brain, the accounting has been worked out in detail. Careful analyses of the energy budget of gray matter have concluded that the large majority of the brain's energy use goes to restoring ion gradients after nerve cells signal. Your brain's enormous appetite for fuel is not mostly about thinking in some abstract sense. It is about resetting membrane voltage, over and over, at enormous speed. *(Evidence: well established. The detailed budget comes from Attwell and Laughlin, 2001, and subsequent work.)*

Across the whole body, estimates of what the sodium-potassium pump costs commonly land somewhere in the range of a fifth to nearly a third of your resting metabolism, with wide variation between tissues. *(Evidence: strong, but the specific fraction varies by tissue and by method. Treat the exact number with caution.)*

Stop and sit with that.

A meaningful share of everything you eat is spent on one job: keeping sodium outside your cells and potassium inside. Not on movement. Not on thought. On maintaining a difference.

This is Chapter 1's argument made concrete. You are not paying for heat. You are paying for separation.

### Battery two: the mitochondrion

Now go inside the cell, where there is a second battery, and it is stronger.

&#91;image: Figure 2.2. The two batteries. The cell holds roughly seventy millivolts across its outer membrane. Each mitochondrion inside holds 150 to 180 millivolts across an inner membrane only five nanometers thick, which works out to one of the most intense electrical fields in the natural world.\]

Mitochondria are strange, and their strangeness is the key to understanding them.

They have their own DNA, separate from the DNA in your cell's nucleus. They have a double membrane, which almost nothing else in the cell does. They divide on their own schedule, by splitting, rather than waiting for the cell to divide. And their internal machinery resembles bacterial machinery more than it resembles the rest of your cells.

The explanation, first argued seriously by Lynn Margulis in the 1960s against considerable resistance and now thoroughly accepted, is that mitochondria used to be free-living bacteria. At some point deep in the past, one cell engulfed another and, instead of digesting it, kept it. That partnership became permanent.

You are, quite literally, a collaboration. *(Evidence: well established.)*

The outer mitochondrial membrane is fairly porous. The inner membrane is where everything happens. It is folded into deep pleats, called cristae, which pack an enormous amount of surface area into a very small volume, and it is studded with the machinery that does the actual work of energy conversion.

Here is what that machinery does, in one sentence: it takes electrons from your food and uses the energy released as they move down a chain of proteins to pump protons from the inside of the mitochondrion to the outside of the inner membrane.

Protons pile up on one side. The inside becomes negatively charged relative to the outside.

And the resulting voltage is in the range of 150 to 180 millivolts.

That number by itself sounds trivial. A single AA battery is 1,500 millivolts. But voltage alone is the wrong way to think about it, because what matters to the molecules living inside that membrane is not the total voltage. It is how steep the drop is.

The inner mitochondrial membrane is about five nanometers thick. A nanometer is a billionth of a meter. Divide 150 millivolts by five nanometers and you get an electrical field of roughly thirty million volts per meter.

For comparison, air breaks down and conducts a lightning arc at around three million volts per meter.

The field inside your mitochondria is about ten times that. *(Evidence: well established. The voltage range and membrane thickness are standard measurements, and the field strength is arithmetic.)*

You have, running quietly inside every cell in your body, right now, some of the most intense electrical fields found anywhere in nature. Not as a metaphor. As a measurement.

### The man everybody laughed at

I want to tell you how we learned this, because it matters for how this book handles unconventional ideas.

For decades, biochemists assumed there had to be some mystery molecule that carried energy from food breakdown to ATP production. They searched for it for years and never found it.

In 1961, a British biochemist named Peter Mitchell proposed something that struck his colleagues as absurd. There is no mystery molecule, he said. The energy is stored as a proton gradient across a membrane. The link between burning food and making ATP is not chemical at all. It is electrical and physical.

The reaction was not polite. Mitchell's idea was dismissed and, at points, openly ridiculed. He eventually left his university post and set up a laboratory in a converted manor house in Cornwall, funded largely by himself, and kept working.

He was right. The evidence accumulated. In 1978, Peter Mitchell received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the chemiosmotic theory, which is now in every biochemistry textbook on earth. *(Evidence: well established.)*

I tell that story for two reasons.

The first is that the single most important idea in bioenergetics was, for years, a minority position held against the consensus. That is worth remembering when we reach the more contested territory later in this book.

The second reason is the harder one, and I want to be equally clear about it. Mitchell did not win because he was an outsider. He won because the experiments went his way. Being unconventional earns you a hearing. It does not earn you a conclusion.

That is the standard I am holding this book to.

### ATP: the currency, not the savings account

So the mitochondrion has built a charge. How does that become useful?

Protons want to flow back in. The membrane will not let them, except through one particular structure, which we will look at closely in Chapter 10. As they flow through, they turn a molecular rotor, and that rotation builds ATP.

ATP is a molecule with three phosphate groups in a row. Phosphates are negatively charged and dislike being crowded together, so holding three of them in a chain is like holding a compressed spring. Break off the last one and energy is released, right where it is needed.

And here is the crucial fact that reframes how most people think about energy.

ATP is not a fuel tank. It is not stored in any meaningful quantity. A muscle working at maximum effort exhausts its ATP in a couple of seconds. Everything beyond that has to be manufactured on the spot, continuously, at the rate it is being spent.

ATP is not your savings. It is the cash in your hand, and your mitochondria are the bank window, open twenty-four hours, with no vault behind it.

Over a day, as I mentioned in Chapter 1, you cycle roughly your own body weight through that window.

### Why two batteries, and why this matters to me

So you have two batteries, one nested inside the other.

The **mitochondrial battery** is the stronger one. It is where energy is captured and converted. It is an engine.

The **plasma membrane battery** is the one that carries information. It is what fires when a nerve signals, what changes when a muscle contracts, what shifts when a cell decides whether to divide. It is a communication line.

And they are joined at the hip. The mitochondrial battery makes the ATP. The ATP runs the pumps. The pumps maintain the plasma membrane battery. If the first one falters, the second one cannot be held.

I have spent my career working on the nervous system. In the Gonstead tradition I was raised in, the premise is that nerve function matters enormously to how the whole body works. I believed that long before I could have explained it at this level.

What I understand now is what a nerve signal physically is. It is a controlled, rapid, deliberate collapse and restoration of membrane voltage, traveling down a cell. That is all it is. Every thought you have ever had, every movement you have ever made, every sensation, was a wave of voltage change moving across membranes that something had to charge first.

So when a patient tells me they are exhausted, that their body will not recover, that their nervous system feels like it is running badly, I no longer hear a vague complaint. I hear a question about whether their cells can still build and hold the gradients that make signaling possible.

That is the question this book is chasing.

### What comes next

You now know that you hold real electrical charge, in two places, at continuous cost.

The obvious conclusion is that more of it must be better. Charge the batteries higher, get healthier.

That conclusion is wrong, and the next chapter is about why. It is the most important chapter in this book.

## Chapter 3. Coherence, Not Maximum Voltage

When I was a boy, my parents, some of their friends and I were attacked by killer bees at the ranch.

It ended the way those things end when they go badly. A hospital visit and an EpiPen.

I think about that day often now, because of what was actually happening inside the body of the person who needed that shot. The immune system was not failing. It was not broken. It was not underperforming.

It was working perfectly, at maximum output, and that is exactly what made it dangerous.

In anaphylaxis, immune cells release their chemical arsenal all at once. Blood vessels widen throughout the body, which is a useful local response to an injury and a catastrophic whole-body one, because blood pressure collapses. Airways tighten. The response that evolved to contain a threat starts shutting down the person it was protecting.

And the treatment is not more immune activity. It is epinephrine, which does the opposite. It tightens the vessels back up and opens the airway. It brings the system back toward the middle.

Nobody looks at anaphylaxis and concludes that the immune system should be turned up higher.

Hold onto that, because this chapter is about making the same mistake in a different system, and it is a mistake I see constantly in the world of health and longevity.

### The seductive wrong idea

You finished Chapter 2 knowing that your cells hold real electrical charge, and that holding it costs you a significant share of everything you eat.

The obvious next thought is the one almost everyone has.

*If voltage is life, then more voltage must be more life. Charge me up.*

You can find that idea everywhere. Raise your cellular voltage. Restore your charge. Devices, supplements, protocols, all promising to lift some number that is described as the measure of your vitality.

I understand the appeal. I would like it to be true. It is simple, it is measurable, and it gives you something to chase.

It is also wrong, and it is wrong in three separate ways. Getting this right is the difference between a book that sounds scientific and a book that is.

### Wrong the first way: the signal is the change

&#91;image: Figure 3.1. On the left, one nerve signal. The cell sits at rest, then rapidly depolarizes, then restores itself. On the right, a cell held further from threshold. It has more charge across its membrane, and it has nothing to say.\]

Here is what a thought physically is.

A nerve cell sits at rest, around minus seventy millivolts. Something nudges it. If that nudge pushes the voltage up to roughly minus fifty-five millivolts, a threshold is crossed, and gates in the membrane fly open. Sodium, which the cell has spent its whole life keeping outside, rushes in. The voltage shoots upward, past zero, to around plus forty millivolts.

Then the sodium gates slam shut, potassium gates open, potassium leaves, and the voltage falls back down. It briefly dips below where it started, then settles.

The whole event takes about a thousandth of a second. *(Evidence: well established. This is the Hodgkin and Huxley description of the action potential, Nobel Prize 1963.)*

Now notice what the message was.

The message was not the resting voltage. The message was the **collapse** of the resting voltage, and its restoration. The information is carried entirely in the change.

So consider a cell driven to a more negative voltage. Minus ninety instead of minus seventy. By the naive model, that cell is more charged, therefore healthier.

In reality, that cell is now further from threshold. It takes a bigger nudge to make it fire. Push it far enough and it stops firing at all.

It has more charge. It has less function.

This is the first crack in the whole idea, and it is not a small one. Your nervous system, your heart, your muscles, and much of your hormonal signaling all run on cells deliberately dropping and restoring their voltage on command. A cell that will not change is not a healthy cell. It is a silent one.

### Wrong the second way: charge with nowhere to go

Now look at the other battery.

&#91;image: Figure 3.2. Two mitochondria receiving the same fuel. On the left, energy is being spent, protons flow back through the ATP motor, and the electron chain keeps moving. On the right, nothing is being spent. The motor stalls, the voltage climbs, electrons back up in the chain, and reactive oxygen production rises.\]

Inside a mitochondrion, electrons move down a chain of proteins, and that movement pumps protons out across the inner membrane, building the voltage we met in Chapter 2.

Those protons want to come back in. When the cell needs energy, they do, flowing through the ATP motor and turning it. Protons return, voltage eases slightly, electrons keep moving down the chain, and the whole system flows.

Now take away the demand. The cell is not doing much. There is no call for ATP.

The protons have nowhere to go. The motor stops turning. The voltage climbs, and as it climbs, it becomes harder and harder for the chain to pump any more protons against it. Electrons stop moving forward. They sit on the chain, waiting.

And an electron sitting on a protein in an oxygen-rich environment will eventually fall off onto an oxygen molecule directly, producing a reactive oxygen species.

This is not a fringe claim. It is one of the better-characterized findings in mitochondrial bioenergetics. Reactive oxygen production rises steeply as membrane potential rises, particularly once the voltage is high and ATP demand is low. A landmark 1997 paper by Korshunov, Skulachev and Starkov made the relationship explicit, and the finding has been confirmed and refined many times since. *(Evidence: well established in isolated mitochondria; the picture in intact tissue is more complex.)*

There is an elegant corollary, and I love it because it is so counterintuitive.

Mitochondria have proteins that deliberately let protons leak back across the membrane without making any ATP. From a pure efficiency standpoint that looks like a design flaw. You built a beautiful gradient and then poked a hole in it.

But that controlled leak lowers the voltage slightly, which keeps electrons moving, which reduces reactive oxygen production. It is a pressure relief valve. Skulachev called the principle mild uncoupling, and it is why your brown fat can turn the entire gradient into heat on purpose when you are cold. *(Evidence: well established for brown fat and UCP1; the broader protective role of mild uncoupling across tissues is strong but still developing.)*

So here is the second crack. **A mitochondrion at maximum charge with nothing to spend it on is not a well-charged battery. It is a stalled one, and stalled is where the damage starts.**

If you remember one idea from this book when you are deciding what to do on a Tuesday afternoon, make it this one. This single mechanism is most of the explanation for Chapter 17, where we look at what constant food and no movement actually does to you.

### Wrong the third way: there is no single right number

You already saw a hint of this in Chapter 2. A nerve cell rests near minus seventy millivolts. A skeletal muscle cell rests near minus ninety. A fat cell sits much closer to zero.

These are not degrees of health. A fat cell is not a failed neuron. Each tissue holds the voltage that its particular job requires.

It goes further. The same cell holds different voltages at different points in its own life.

This is where the work of Michael Levin and others becomes interesting, and I want to be careful about how I present it, because it is the first place in this book where we move from settled physiology into active research.

Across a range of organisms, cells that are actively dividing and cells that are undifferentiated tend to sit at more depolarized voltages, closer to zero. Cells that have matured into a stable, specialized job tend to sit more polarized. And the relationship is not merely descriptive. In laboratory work, artificially changing a cell's membrane voltage can influence whether stem cells differentiate and what they differentiate into. *(Evidence: strong in cell culture and in model organisms; the practical significance in adult human tissue is still emerging.)*

There is a related observation about cancer. Many tumor cells are measurably depolarized compared to the normal tissue around them, an observation going back to work by Binggeli and Weinstein in the 1980s and pursued seriously since. What that means is genuinely open. Depolarization may be a consequence of rapid growth, a contributor to it, or both. *(Evidence: emerging. The association is real. The direction of causation is not settled, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.)*

So the third crack is this. **Voltage is not a score. It is a setting, and the correct setting depends on the cell, the tissue, and what that cell is doing right now.**

### What health actually looks like

If it is not a level, what is it?

It is range. It is the ability to move.

And you can see this beautifully in a measurement you may already own, because your watch probably takes it.

Your heart does not beat like a metronome. The interval between beats varies, constantly, from one beat to the next. That variation is called heart rate variability, and for decades it was treated as noise in the signal.

It is not noise. It is the signature of a system with two opposing controls, one that speeds the heart and one that slows it, both live at the same time, constantly making small corrections.

And the finding is consistent. Reduced heart rate variability, a heart that has become more metronomic, is associated with worse outcomes across a wide range of conditions. In one of the early landmark studies, low variability after a heart attack predicted mortality independently of how much heart muscle had been damaged. *(Evidence: well established as an association. Whether improving variability improves outcomes, or simply reflects that something else improved, is a separate and less settled question.)*

Sit with the shape of that finding, because it is the whole chapter in one sentence.

**A heart that has lost its variability has become more regular, and more regular is worse.**

Nobody looks at that data and concludes we should aim for a perfectly steady pulse. We understand immediately that a living system needs to be able to move.

The same is true of voltage. What you want is not a high number. What you want is a cell that can sit where it belongs, change quickly when called on, and come back. Range. Responsiveness. Recovery.

### The word I keep coming back to: coherence

There is one more layer, and it is the one I find most interesting.

Everything so far has been about single cells. But you are not a bag of independent cells. You are tissue.

Many of your cells are physically connected to their neighbors by small protein channels called gap junctions, which let ions and small molecules pass directly from one cell into the next. Cells that are coupled this way are, electrically, partly one thing. When one changes, its neighbors feel it. *(Evidence: well established.)*

This is how your heart works as a pump rather than as several billion cells twitching on their own schedules. It is how a wave of contraction moves through your gut. Coordination is not a bonus feature. In a heart, loss of coordination is called fibrillation, and it kills you in minutes even though every individual cell is still alive and still electrically active.

A fibrillating heart has no shortage of electrical activity. It has lost agreement.

Beyond the heart, the picture becomes more speculative and more fascinating. Levin's group has shown, in flatworms and frog embryos, that patterns of voltage across a whole tissue carry information about what that tissue should become. Change the pattern and you change the anatomy that develops, without touching a single gene. *(Evidence: strong and reproducible in those model organisms. Whether anything comparable can be harnessed in adult humans is, at this point, a hypothesis.)*

So here is the sentence that I think is the most important scientific claim in this entire book, and I want to flag clearly what kind of claim it is.

**Health is not maximum charge in any single cell. It is tissue-appropriate voltage, the ability to change it on demand, and coordination between neighboring cells. Coherence, not maximum.**

The physiology underneath that sentence, the tissue-specific voltages, the necessity of dynamic change, the electrical coupling of cells, is well established. The framing of coherence as an organizing principle for human health is my working model. It is built on real research, and it is the lens I use, and it has not been tested as a unified theory. You should hold it the way I do: seriously, and provisionally. This is coherence across space, between neighboring cells. By the end of the book it will also mean coherence across time, and the Coda states the full version, including the parts I have not shown you yet.

### Not a thermostat, a negotiation

There is a word most people learn in school for this: homeostasis. The body keeps things steady.

It is a useful word and a slightly misleading one, because it suggests a thermostat holding a fixed number.

In the 1980s, Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer proposed a better one: allostasis, stability through change. The idea is that the body does not defend a fixed set point. It predicts what is coming and shifts its settings in advance.

Your cortisol starts rising before you wake up, not after. Your heart rate begins climbing before you start running, not once you are already oxygen-starved. Your metabolism shifts across the day in anticipation of when food usually arrives.

The body is not holding a number. It is running a forecast. *(Evidence: the specific anticipatory responses are well established. Allostasis as an overall framework is a widely used and productive model rather than a settled law.)*

Which raises the obvious question, and it is the question that opens the next part of this book.

If your body is predicting what is coming, what is it using to make the prediction?

# Part II: Light and Time

## Chapter 4. Light Is Information, and a Little Chemistry

I grew up outside.

Not in the way people mean when they say a child played outdoors. I mean that the work was outside, so I was outside. Animals need feeding before the day starts. Fences need mending. Gardens need tending. A ranch does not schedule itself around anyone's comfort.

So I was in the sun at dawn, and in it through the middle of the day, and still in it when the light went orange and long in the evening. Not because anyone told me it was healthy. Because that was where the work was.

I want to be honest about what I understood at the time. Nothing. I thought sunlight was for vitamin D, and I thought that only because someone had told me so.

What I know now is that I was receiving, every single day, the most important environmental signal a human body gets, at intensities most adults today go months without seeing.

### An old book got to the order before I did

I want to put something here that is not evidence, and I want to say so before I say it.

Open Genesis and look at the order of events.

Light comes first. Let there be light. Then it is divided from the darkness. Then both halves are named, Day and Night. And then the sentence that sets the pattern for everything after it: the evening and the morning were the first day.

Four things, in sequence, and not one living creature has appeared yet. Light. Then the separation of light from dark. Then the naming of both. Then the rhythm.

Plants arrive on day three. The sun and the moon do not show up until day four, which readers have argued about for a very long time, and the argument is beside my point. What matters to me is what the sun and moon are given for when they finally arrive. Not warmth. Not growth. Not energy. They are made "for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and years." The word sitting behind "seasons" is mo'adim, appointed times, the same word used later for festivals. They are installed as clocks.

Animals come on days five and six. Life arrives last, into a world where the light, the darkness and the calendar are already running.

Now, what I am not doing with that.

I am not offering it as support for anything in this chapter. It cannot do scientific work, and if I leaned on it that way you would be right to close the book. The argument here stands on the retina, on melanopsin and on the phase response curve, and it stands or falls there, with or without Genesis.

What I am doing is pointing at something I find hard to walk past. Whatever you believe about where that text came from, somebody writing thousands of years before anyone had heard of a photoreceptor put light before life, put the dividing of light from darkness before life, and said plainly that the purpose of the sun and the moon was to mark time.

That is this entire chapter, written down a very long time before I got to it.

So here is the claim, and then I will spend the rest of the chapter defending it with instruments rather than scripture. Light reaches you first as information, and the first thing that information tells you is what time it is.

And I want to be equally clear about what that signal is not.

### What light does, and what it does not

There are two wrong answers in circulation, and I want to clear both out of the way before we go any further, because this book gets misread if I do not.

The first wrong answer is the one you will find in wellness circles. Sunlight charges your body the way a charger fills a phone. Photons flow in, your cells fill up with energy.

The second wrong answer is the one you will find in a great deal of mainstream medicine. Light makes vitamin D, and is otherwise a dermatological hazard with no other role in physiology.

Both are wrong, and the truth sits between them in a way that is considerably more interesting than either.

**Light does two things to you. It carries information, and it does a small amount of specific chemical work.** The first is the main event and the subject of this entire part of the book. The second is real, much smaller, and consistently misdescribed in both directions.

### First, and mainly, light is information

The energy delivered by the light entering your eyes is, in metabolic terms, negligible. It is a rounding error against what a resting body burns in a minute. Yet that trivial amount of energy will reorganize the timing of metabolism in nearly every tissue you own.

That combination, almost no energy and an enormous effect, is the signature of information rather than power. A key weighs almost nothing and does not push the door open. It tells a lock what to do.

When it comes to your eyes and your clock, light is a key.

### Second, light is a minor energy input

Now the part I want to be precise about, because people get it wrong in both directions.

Your skin absorbs an enormous amount of solar energy. Stand outside on a bright day and your body is absorbing radiant energy on the order of several hundred watts. That is more raw power than your entire resting metabolism uses.

So the problem with "your body is a solar panel" is not that the energy is too small. The energy is large.

The problem is what happens to it. Almost all of it becomes heat. A stone in a wall absorbs sunlight in the same way and warms up. **Absorbing radiant energy and converting it into biological work are two completely different things.**

A solar panel is defined by the conversion. Photons in, electrical current out, powering the device. Your skin has no machinery that does that. You have no chlorophyll, no reaction center, and no pathway that carries a photon-excited electron into a mitochondrion.

That is the real difference between you and a leaf. Not that the leaf absorbs light and you do not. You both do. The leaf can convert it.

**But a fraction of that absorbed energy does real chemical work, and that fraction deserves far more respect than it usually gets.**

Specific molecules in your tissue absorb specific wavelengths, and when they do, they physically change shape. That shape change is not heat. It is the first step of a chemical reaction or a signaling cascade.

Molecules that do this are called chromophores, and you are full of them. A cholesterol precursor in your skin absorbs UVB and becomes vitamin D. Nitrogen compounds in your skin release nitric oxide under UVA. Melanin, hemoglobin, urocanic acid, flavins, porphyrins, bilirubin and your DNA itself all absorb light at characteristic wavelengths, and several of them do something as a result.

And then there is the finding that genuinely surprised me the first time I read it. **Human skin expresses opsins**, the same family of light-detecting proteins your eyes use for vision. Chapter 7 goes through what they appear to be doing there. Your skin is not only absorbing light. It is detecting it, with dedicated receptors.

### So here is the framing for the rest of this book

**Light is information, and light is a minor energy input.**

The information is the main event. It sets the clock, and the clock schedules almost everything in Parts III and IV.

The energy input is real, it is wavelength-specific, and it is small. It produces signaling molecules rather than fuel. Vitamin D is not an energy source. Nitric oxide is not an energy source. They are messages, manufactured by photochemistry.

And the one thing light does not do, which is where the popular version goes wrong, is supply the electrons in your electron transport chain. Those come from food, and Chapter 15 traces exactly where they came from before that.

Hold both halves. A book that says light is only information underdescribes your skin. A book that says light charges you is selling something. The accurate version is that you are lit, you are timed by it, and a small amount of what lands on you is turned into chemistry.

### Your eyes are lying to you about brightness

Before we get to the biology, look at the numbers, because they are genuinely shocking and almost nobody knows them.

&#91;image: Figure 4.1. Illuminance on a logarithmic scale, where each gridline is ten times the one before it. The gap between a bright indoor room and an ordinary overcast day outside is larger than most people's intuition allows for.\]

Brightness is measured in lux. Here is what matters.

A well-lit kitchen or office sits at somewhere around 300 to 500 lux. It feels bright. You can read comfortably. Nothing about it seems dim.

Step outside on a gray, unremarkable, overcast day, and you are standing in something between 1,000 and 10,000 lux.

Step into direct midday sun and you are in the neighborhood of 100,000 lux.

So the ordinary indoor day most adults live in is not slightly dimmer than outdoors. Depending on the day, it can be ten times dimmer, fifty times dimmer, two hundred times dimmer.

Why does nobody notice?

Because your visual system is extraordinarily good at hiding it from you. Your pupils adjust. Your retina adjusts its sensitivity. Your brain performs the equivalent of automatic exposure correction. The entire system is built to give you usable vision across an enormous range, which means it deliberately conceals the absolute brightness from your conscious experience.

Your eyes tell you the room is bright. They are answering a different question than the one your body is asking.

Because the part of your eye that reports to your clock does not care whether you can see. It cares how much light is actually arriving. *(Evidence: well established. The lux figures are standard photometric values and the mechanisms of visual light adaptation are textbook.)*

### The third kind of eye cell

For almost the entire history of biology, we believed the eye had two kinds of light-detecting cells. Rods, for dim light and motion. Cones, for color and detail. That was the complete list, and it was in every textbook.

Then some results came in that did not fit.

Researchers found mice that had been genetically engineered to lack functioning rods and cones. These animals were, for all visual purposes, blind. They could not see.

And their body clocks still reset perfectly to the light and dark cycle. *(Freedman and colleagues, 1999.)*

Around the same time, work with a small number of blind human patients found something parallel. Some people with no conscious light perception still showed normal melatonin suppression when exposed to bright light. Their brains knew the light was there. They did not. *(Czeisler and colleagues, 1995.)*

Something in the eye was detecting light through a completely separate channel.

In 1998, Ignacio Provencio identified a new light-sensitive pigment, which he named melanopsin. Within a few years, the cells carrying it were pinned down: a small population of retinal ganglion cells, a fraction of a percent of the cells in your retina, that are directly light-sensitive on their own. They are called intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells. *(Berson, Dunn and Takao, and Hattar and colleagues, both 2002.)*

These cells are built for a completely different job than vision, and every feature reflects it.

They respond **slowly**. Where a cone reports in milliseconds so you can track a moving object, these cells integrate light over many minutes. They are not asking what just happened. They are asking what kind of light this has been for a while.

They are **brightness meters**, not image formers. They do not contribute to the picture you see. They report a quantity.

They are **tuned to blue**, peaking in sensitivity around 480 nanometers, which is roughly the wavelength of a clear sky overhead.

And they wire directly to the clock. A dedicated nerve bundle called the retinohypothalamic tract runs from these cells to a small structure in the hypothalamus. *(Evidence: all of the above is well established, and was recognized as a major discovery in sensory biology.)*

&#91;image: Figure 4.2. The pathway, and the two jobs. Daylight is detected by melanopsin cells in the retina, which report brightness to the master clock, which synchronizes clocks throughout the body. Alongside that, chromophores in skin absorb specific wavelengths and do a small amount of real chemical work.\]

Think about what this means.

You have a sense organ you have never noticed using. It has no conscious sensation attached to it. You cannot feel it working. Its entire output is an answer to one question, asked continuously: *how bright is it, and what color, and for how long?*

And the answer to that question is used to set the time in every tissue you have.

### What the signal actually contains

It is not a single number. Your body reads at least four things in the light.

**How bright.** This is the strongest component, and it is why the numbers in Figure 4.1 matter so much. The clock-setting response is much stronger at outdoor intensities than indoor ones.

**What color.** Morning and evening sunlight, passing through more atmosphere at a low angle, is redder. Midday sun, and the blue sky overhead, is richer in the short wavelengths these cells prefer. The spectrum is itself a clue about the hour.

**For how long.** These cells add light up over time. A few seconds is nothing. Twenty minutes is a statement.

**And above all, when.** This is the one that changes everything, and the next chapter explains the clock it acts on. The same light delivers opposite instructions depending on the time of day it arrives. Bright light in the morning pulls your clock earlier. The identical light late at night pushes it later. Not more of the same effect. The opposite effect. *(Evidence: well established. This is the phase response curve, one of the most reproducible findings in circadian biology.)*

I want to sit on that last point, because it is the single most practical idea in this part of the book.

There is no such thing as a good amount of light. There is only light at a time. The same photons that anchor your entire day when they arrive at seven in the morning will unpick it when they arrive at eleven at night.

This is why I am not going to give you a rule like "get more light." That rule is incomplete to the point of being useless. What I will give you, by the end of Part II, is an understanding of what light means at each hour, so you can work out the rule yourself.

### Why the modern eye is starved

Put the pieces together and the modern problem draws itself.

We evolved under a light environment with enormous contrast. Days spent in thousands to tens of thousands of lux. Nights, before fire, at a fraction of one lux.

A typical modern day looks nothing like that. Hundreds of lux indoors during the day, a fraction of what the outdoor world delivers. And then, at night, hundreds of lux again, delivered by screens and ceiling lights at a time when our ancestors had starlight.

The days are dim and the nights are bright. The contrast has been flattened from both ends.

And a clock that runs on contrast, given a day that barely differs from a night, does exactly what you would expect. It drifts, it weakens, and the tissues that depend on it fall out of step with each other.

That is Chapter 16. Everything between here and there is about understanding the machinery well enough that the failure mode becomes obvious.

### Back to the ranch

I said I did not understand any of this as a kid, and I did not. But I want to close by naming what was actually happening to me on those mornings.

When I walked out at dawn to feed animals, a set of cells in my retina I had never heard of were counting photons and sending a signal down a nerve bundle to a cluster of neurons the size of a grain of rice. That signal was telling the clock in my brain that the day had begun.

That clock was then sending that time signal outward, to my liver, my gut, my muscles, my immune cells, and telling them all to start the day together.

I did not feel any of it. It has no sensation. It is one of the most consequential things that happens to a human body and it is completely silent.

Most people reading this book have not given their clock a clear morning signal in years.

## Chapter 5. The Master Clock and Your Hundred Clocks

For the better part of five years I went back and forth between California and Abu Dhabi.

That is a long way. Depending on the route, you cross eleven or twelve time zones, and you land into a day that has nothing to do with the one your body thinks it is in.

Everybody knows what that feels like. What interests me now is what it proves.

When you land in a new time zone, your body does not simply accept the local time. If it did, jet lag would not exist. You would step off the plane, see the sun, and be on local time immediately.

Instead, for days, you are wrong. Wide awake at three in the morning. Falling asleep at dinner. Hungry at the wrong hours. Your digestion behaving as though it is still somewhere over the Atlantic.

That gap between where you are and how you feel is the clearest evidence there is of something most people never think about.

**You are not responding to the world. You are running an internal clock, and it is currently wrong.**

### The clock keeps running in the dark

This was proven the hard way, by people who went underground.

In 1938, a sleep researcher named Nathaniel Kleitman and his student Bruce Richardson spent over a month living deep inside Mammoth Cave in Kentucky, out of reach of daylight and any clue about the time.

In the 1960s and 1970s, a French geologist named Michel Siffre went further, spending months at a time alone in caves with no clock, no daylight, and no contact with anyone who knew the date. He slept when he felt like sleeping and ate when he felt hungry.

And in both cases, the rhythm did not disappear.

The people in these experiments kept a regular cycle of sleep, waking, temperature and hormone release. They simply drifted, because their internal cycle was not exactly twenty-four hours long. Siffre, famously, lost track so thoroughly that when his team came to retrieve him he believed it was nearly a month earlier than it actually was. He had been living long days without noticing.

Modern work under carefully controlled conditions has pinned the human free-running period down to just over twenty-four hours, averaging around twenty-four hours and eleven minutes. *(Czeisler and colleagues, 1999.)*

That small discrepancy matters enormously, and it explains why light is so important. If your internal clock ran exactly twenty-four hours, you could ignore the sun entirely. Because it runs slightly long, you drift a little later every day, and something has to nudge you back.

That nudge is morning light. Every single day, for your whole life. *(Evidence: well established.)*

### The clock is a chemical loop

So what is this clock made of? Not neurons firing in a circle. Something smaller and stranger.

&#91;image: Figure 5.1. The core clock loop. Two proteins switch on the genes for two other proteins, which accumulate through the day, re-enter the nucleus, and switch the first pair off. Then they break down, the brake lifts, and the cycle begins again.\]

It works like this.

Two proteins, called CLOCK and BMAL1, join together and act as a switch. They turn on a set of genes, among them two called *Period* and *Cryptochrome*.

Those genes get read, and the cell begins manufacturing PER and CRY proteins. This takes hours. The proteins accumulate through the day.

Eventually there are enough of them that they travel back into the nucleus, find CLOCK and BMAL1, and shut them off. Production stops.

With production stopped, the existing PER and CRY proteins gradually break down overnight. As they disappear, the brake comes off CLOCK and BMAL1, which switch back on, and the whole cycle starts over.

One complete lap takes roughly twenty-four hours.

That is the clock. It is a chemical loop with a built-in delay, and the delay is the timekeeping.

This mechanism was worked out in fruit flies by Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young, and they were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2017 for it. The same machinery, with the same logic, runs in human cells. *(Evidence: well established.)*

I find this genuinely beautiful. There is no pendulum, no quartz crystal, no counting. There is a protein that switches on the gene that builds the protein that switches it off, and the time it takes to do that is a day.

### The master clock, and the billions of others

Here is the part that changes how you should think about your health.

That loop is not running in one special place. It is running in nearly every cell in your body.

Your liver cells are running it. Your fat cells. Your muscle. Your gut lining. Your skin. Your immune cells. Each one has its own copy of the clock, ticking away independently.

When researchers have looked at how many genes are under circadian control, the answers have been startling. In mice, work by Zhang and colleagues in 2014 found that a large fraction of all protein-coding genes are expressed rhythmically in at least one tissue, with different tissues running different sets. A later study in baboons, published in 2018, found rhythmic expression in an even larger share of genes across the tissues examined.

The details differ between studies and species. The headline does not. **A very large portion of your biology is not on all the time. It is scheduled.** *(Evidence: well established that widespread rhythmic gene expression exists; the exact percentages depend on tissue, species and method.)*

Enzymes that break down fat run at certain hours. Repair machinery runs at certain hours. Immune activity has a daily profile. Even the sensitivity of your cells to insulin changes across the day.

Which raises an obvious problem. If you have billions of independent clocks, what stops them from drifting apart?

### The conductor

Sitting in your hypothalamus, just above the point where your optic nerves cross, are two small clusters of about ten thousand neurons each. This is the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the SCN.

This is the master clock, and it has one advantage the others do not. It gets a direct line from the eye, via the melanopsin cells from Chapter 4.

The SCN knows what time it actually is outside. Your liver does not.

So the SCN broadcasts. Through nerve signals, hormone release, and body temperature rhythms, it sends timing information outward to the rest of the body, keeping the peripheral clocks in step with the world and with each other.

The orchestra analogy is nearly perfect here and I will use it, with one qualification. Every musician can already play the piece from memory, at roughly the right tempo, on their own. They do not need the conductor to know the notes. What the conductor provides is agreement about when to start and how fast to go.

Remove the conductor and you do not get silence. You get a slow, ugly drift into a performance where every section is playing the right music at slightly the wrong time.

That is exactly what internal desynchrony looks like, and it is one of the central ideas of this book. *(Evidence: well established.)*

### What the schedule actually looks like

The clock is not abstract. You can watch it run.

&#91;image: Figure 5.2. Three of the body's daily rhythms. Cortisol rises sharply around waking and falls across the day. Melatonin is released only in darkness. Core body temperature swings by close to a degree, bottoming out in the last hours of sleep.\]

**Cortisol** has a reputation as the stress hormone, and that framing does it a disservice. Its daily pattern is at least as important as its stress response. Cortisol begins climbing in the hours before you wake, spikes sharply in the first thirty to forty-five minutes after waking, and then falls away across the day to a low point around midnight.

That morning spike is called the cortisol awakening response, and it is not a sign of stress. It is a scheduled mobilization. It is your body releasing fuel and raising alertness because it has predicted, correctly, that you are about to need both.

**Melatonin** is the opposite. It is released by the pineal gland, and only in darkness. It begins rising roughly two hours before your usual bedtime, a point researchers call dim light melatonin onset, peaks in the small hours, and clears by morning.

Melatonin is not primarily a sedative. It is a signal, and the message is simply: *it is night now*. Every tissue with a clock can read it.

**Core body temperature** swings by something close to a full degree Celsius across twenty-four hours. It bottoms out in the last hours of sleep, typically around four or five in the morning, and peaks in the early evening. That falling temperature in the evening is part of what makes sleep possible, and it is why a warm room makes falling asleep harder.

*(Evidence: all three rhythms are well established and are standard clinical measurements.)*

Notice that all three are anticipatory. Cortisol rises *before* you wake. Temperature falls *before* you sleep. This is the allostasis idea from Chapter 3, made visible. Your body is not reacting. It is forecasting.

### The part almost nobody knows: your liver listens to food

Now for the finding that reorganizes everything, and that sets up Part V of this book.

The SCN takes its cue from light. That much we have covered.

But the peripheral clocks are not purely obedient. They have their own inputs, and for several of them, particularly the liver and the digestive system, the strongest input is not light at all.

It is food.

In a landmark set of experiments published in 2000, Damiola and colleagues fed mice only during the hours when they would normally be asleep. Everything else was held constant, including the light cycle.

The clock in the brain stayed locked to the light, exactly as expected.

The clocks in the liver and other peripheral organs abandoned it and reset to the food.

Within days, the master clock and the liver clock were running on two different schedules inside the same animal. *(Evidence: well established in animal models, and repeatedly replicated. Human food-timing studies are more limited but point the same direction.)*

Sit with what that means for a person.

If you get your light signal from a bright morning and your food signal from eleven at night, you are not simply having a late snack. You are sending two contradictory time-of-day messages into the same body, and different organs are going to believe different ones.

Your brain says it is Tuesday morning. Your liver says it is something else entirely.

That is internal desynchrony, and it is a genuinely different concept from being tired or getting too little sleep. Every organ can be working correctly, every clock can be ticking normally, and the system can still be failing, because they no longer agree on what time it is.

I think this is the most underappreciated idea in modern health. We have spent decades asking what people eat. We have spent almost no time asking when, and the when may be carrying more weight than we assumed.

### Back on the plane

So let me go back to those flights.

What was actually happening to me when I landed in Abu Dhabi was not simply fatigue. My SCN was receiving a new light signal and beginning to shift. My liver, my gut and my muscles were shifting too, but at their own rates, driven partly by light and partly by when I happened to eat.

For several days, my organs were not just on the wrong time. They were on different times from each other.

And that resolves on its own after a long flight, which is why we treat jet lag as an inconvenience rather than a health problem.

The question this book asks is what happens when the same condition is produced not by a flight, but by an ordinary modern life. Dim days. Bright nights. Food at all hours. Sleep at shifting times.

What happens when the jet lag never resolves, because you never landed?

## Chapter 6. The Spine: NAD+, the Clock, and Sirtuins

After Kynlee, I spent thirteen years reading.

Not casually. Not the way you read a health article. I mean that I went back to primary literature, followed citations, read papers I only half understood, and read them again.

The first book was John Ott's *Health and Light*. Ott was not a physician. He was a time-lapse photographer who noticed that his plants behaved differently under different kinds of light, spent decades following that observation, and ended up arguing that light is a biological input rather than simply illumination.

Some of what Ott wrote has held up well and some of it has not, and I would not hand it to someone today as a source. What it did for me was break an assumption I did not know I was carrying: that light is scenery. Once that assumption goes, you start noticing that half of physiology is scheduled, and then you cannot stop pulling the thread.

After that it was everything. Mitochondrial biology. Circadian science. Biophysics. The quantum biologists. Doug Wallace. Gilbert Ling. Gerald Pollack. Fritz-Albert Popp and Roeland Van Wijk. Michael Crawford.

I was looking for one thing the whole time, though it took me years to be able to say it this plainly.

I wanted to know where these fields touch.

Because they are usually taught as separate subjects. Energy is biochemistry. Timing is neuroscience. Repair is molecular biology. Different departments, different journals, different conferences.

And then I found a molecule that does not respect any of those boundaries.

This chapter is about that molecule, and it is the spine of this book.

### NAD+, and why it is not a supplement

Its full name is nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. Nobody says that. It is NAD+, and it is built from vitamin B3.

You may have seen it on a shelf. Set that aside completely for now. I will deal with supplements honestly at the end of this chapter, and the honest answer is more complicated than either side of that argument admits.

What matters first is what the molecule does, because it does two jobs that have almost nothing in common, and that double role is the entire point.

&#91;image: Figure 6.1. NAD+ has two separate jobs. As an electron shuttle it is recycled endlessly and never used up. As a substrate for sirtuins, PARPs and CD38 it is broken apart and must be rebuilt.\]

### Job one: the shuttle

Go back to Chapter 2, where I said that metabolism is the movement of electrons from food to oxygen.

Something has to carry those electrons. That is NAD+.

When your cells break down food, NAD+ is right there to collect the electrons that come off. Loaded, it becomes NADH. NADH then travels to the mitochondrial membrane, hands its electrons into the electron transport chain, and becomes NAD+ again, empty and ready.

This happens constantly, at enormous scale. The same molecules cycle round thousands of times a day. In this role NAD+ is not consumed at all. It is a delivery van, not a parcel.

And this gives it a second property, which is quieter and more important.

Because NAD+ and NADH are the same molecule in two states, the **ratio between them tells you the redox state of the cell**. Lots of NAD+ relative to NADH means the electron traffic is flowing and there is capacity to accept more. Lots of NADH relative to NAD+ means electrons are backing up and the system is congested.

In the cytoplasm of a healthy cell, NAD+ outnumbers NADH by a very wide margin, on the order of hundreds to one. Inside the mitochondria the ratio is far tighter, because that is where the electrons are being handed off.

That ratio is a gauge. Enzymes throughout the cell read it. Your metabolism makes decisions based on it. *(Evidence: well established.)*

Remember this gauge. It returns in Chapter 17, when we look at what happens to it when food never stops arriving.

### Job two: the fuel

Here is where it gets strange, and where most explanations stop.

A second, completely separate set of enzymes uses NAD+ in a way that **destroys it**. They break the molecule apart to power their own chemistry. Every reaction costs one NAD+, permanently.

Three families matter.

**Sirtuins** are a family of seven enzymes in humans, and they are involved in a remarkable range of things: DNA repair, mitochondrial biogenesis, inflammation control, metabolic regulation, and, as you will see in a moment, the clock itself. They are absolutely dependent on NAD+. No NAD+, no sirtuin activity, regardless of how much sirtuin protein is present.

**PARPs** are your DNA damage repair crews. When DNA is damaged, PARP enzymes rush to the break and begin repair work, and they consume NAD+ heavily to do it. Serious damage can drain a cell's NAD+ substantially.

**CD38** is an enzyme involved in immune signaling, and it is a significant NAD+ consumer. It becomes more abundant with age, which makes it part of the story of why NAD+ tends to fall as people get older. *(Camacho-Pereira and colleagues, 2016.)*

*(Evidence: all well established.)*

Now put the two jobs side by side.

The same molecule that carries the electrons from your food is the molecule your repair enzymes burn. Energy production and repair are drinking from the same well.

That has a consequence people rarely state out loud. **When a cell is under heavy damage load, repair enzymes consume NAD+, and the supply available for everything else drops.** A cell dealing with a lot of damage is a cell with less to work with.

And since NAD+ is constantly being destroyed, it has to be constantly rebuilt. Your cells run a recycling pathway called the salvage pathway, and its rate-limiting step, the bottleneck that determines how fast NAD+ can be regenerated, is an enzyme called NAMPT.

Hold that name. It is about to connect everything.

### The loop that ties the book together

Between 2008 and 2013, a series of papers established something that, when I first read them, rearranged how I thought about health.

Here it is.

&#91;image: Figure 6.2. The NAD+, clock and sirtuin loop. The clock drives production of the enzyme that rebuilds NAD+. NAD+ levels rise and fall daily as a result. Sirtuins follow NAD+ because they cannot work without it. And sirtuins then chemically modify the clock proteins, feeding back into the loop that started it.\]

**Step one.** NAMPT, the bottleneck enzyme for rebuilding NAD+, is under clock control. CLOCK and BMAL1, the same two proteins from Chapter 5, switch on the NAMPT gene. *(Ramsey and colleagues, 2009; Nakahata and colleagues, 2009.)*

**Step two.** Because production of the rebuilding enzyme is rhythmic, **NAD+ levels themselves rise and fall on a daily cycle.** NAD+ is not a level. It is a rhythm.

**Step three.** Sirtuins depend completely on NAD+. So sirtuin activity rises and falls with it. Repair, metabolic regulation and mitochondrial maintenance are therefore scheduled, not continuous.

**Step four, and this is the part that closes the circle.** SIRT1, the best-studied sirtuin, chemically modifies BMAL1 and PER2, the clock proteins themselves. It tunes the clock that created it. *(Asher and colleagues, 2008; Nakahata and colleagues, 2008.)*

And there is a further layer. Work published in 2013 by Peek and colleagues showed that the clock, working through NAD+, controls mitochondrial oxidative capacity. Your mitochondria are not equally capable at all hours. Their capacity is scheduled too.

*(Evidence: well established in mice, replicated across multiple laboratories. Direct human evidence for the full loop is much thinner, largely because you cannot biopsy people's livers every four hours. I hold the loop as solid in mammalian biology and provisional in the specifics of human application.)*

I want to state plainly why this matters so much to the argument of this book.

Before this work, you could hold energy, timing and repair as three separate subjects. After it, you cannot. The clock builds the molecule. The molecule powers the repair enzyme. The repair enzyme tunes the clock.

**It is one system, seen from three sides.**

That is why this book is organized the way it is, and why I will not let you treat sleep, food timing, light and exercise as items on a list. They are inputs to the same loop.

### What drains it

If NAD+ sits at the center, then the useful question is what pulls it down.

**Age.** NAD+ levels decline with age across multiple tissues. This is one of the more reproducible findings in the field. How much, and in which tissues, varies considerably between studies. *(Evidence: strong but still developing. Treat specific percentage figures with suspicion.)*

**Damage load.** Heavy PARP activity from DNA damage consumes NAD+ directly.

**Inflammation and rising CD38.** More of this enzyme with age means more NAD+ destroyed.

**Metabolic overload.** A constant flood of fuel keeps NADH high relative to NAD+, which means the gauge reads congested. This is Chapter 17's whole mechanism.

**A broken clock.** If NAMPT production is rhythmic and driven by the clock, then a disrupted clock means disrupted NAD+ rhythm. In animals with damaged clock genes, the NAD+ rhythm flattens.

That last point is the one I want you to carry forward, because it runs against intuition.

If you disrupt your circadian rhythm badly enough, you are not only sleeping worse. You may be interfering with the daily rebuilding of the molecule that your repair enzymes run on.

Poor circadian health is not only a sleep problem. It is potentially a repair problem.

### The supplement question, answered honestly

Now I will deal with the bottle on the shelf, because you have been waiting for it and because how I answer it tells you what kind of book this is.

The logic is appealing. NAD+ falls with age. Sirtuins need NAD+. Therefore raise NAD+ and restore function.

The two main precursors sold are nicotinamide riboside, NR, and nicotinamide mononucleotide, NMN.

Here is what the evidence actually shows.

**They work as chemistry.** Human trials consistently show that oral NR raises NAD+ levels in blood. That part is not in dispute. The compounds do what they claim to do biochemically. *(Evidence: well established.)*

**The clinical results are modest and inconsistent.** Human trials have been mostly small, mostly short, and have produced mixed findings. Some show improvements in specific markers. Many show no meaningful effect on the outcomes people actually care about. *(Evidence: emerging, and genuinely unresolved.)*

**Tissue delivery is an open question.** Raising NAD+ in blood is not the same as raising it in muscle, brain, or liver, and measuring it in those tissues in living humans is hard.

**Rhythm may matter as much as level.** This is my own reasoning rather than an established finding, and I want it labeled as such. If the biology depends on NAD+ *cycling* daily, then flooding the system with a constant supply is not obviously the same intervention as restoring a rhythm. It might even work against it. I am not aware of human trials designed to test that question, and I would like to see them. *(Evidence: hypothesis. Mine.)*

So here is my position, stated plainly.

I am not against these compounds and I am not going to tell you they are useless. I am telling you that the evidence today supports the chemistry far better than it supports the outcomes.

And I will say something that will cost me, in a market where the supplement is the product.

**The things in this book that most reliably support NAD+ are not things you buy.** Circadian regularity supports the rhythm that builds it. Exercise raises it. Fasting periods raise it. Reducing chronic inflammation reduces what destroys it.

Those are Chapters 17, 18, 19 and 20. They are free, they have better evidence behind them, and I take no commission on any of them.

## Chapter 7. The Full Spectrum

In 1996 I was living in Rio de Janeiro, training at Gracie Barra in Barra da Tijuca for the first Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu World Championship. It was a school then. It is an academy franchise now.

When we were not on the mats, we were on the beach or in the mountains. Hours in the sun, day after day, month after month. I got darker than I have ever been in my life.

That same period, Rio was dealing with a dengue outbreak. On a training run in the hills above the city I was bitten, and I came down with it. There is a reason people call dengue breakbone fever.

Within about ten days I was back training.

For years I told that story a particular way. I said my vitamin D was so high from all that sun that I threw it off fast.

I want to take that story apart in front of you, because how I handle it is the whole point of this chapter.

Here is what is actually true. Most healthy young adults recover from a first dengue infection in roughly one to two weeks. I was twenty-something, extremely fit, and training full time. That alone predicts what happened to me.

Researchers have looked at vitamin D and dengue. Laboratory work suggests vitamin D influences how immune cells respond to the virus, and some observational studies have linked vitamin D status to how severe the illness becomes. The results are mixed, and no trial has shown that vitamin D prevents dengue or shortens it.

So my recovery is an experience. It is not evidence. I still suspect the sun helped. I cannot show you that it did, and a book that blurs that line is not worth reading.

What I can tell you, with real confidence, is what that sun was doing to my skin, because that part is not in doubt.

### Several jobs, several very different levels of proof

We say "sunlight" as though it were one thing. It is not. It is a broad band of wavelengths, and different parts of it do completely unrelated things when they hit you.

&#91;image: Figure 7.1. The bands of sunlight and what each one does, plus the detection layer running underneath them. The evidence behind these claims is not equally strong, and this book will not pretend otherwise.\]

Chapters 4 and 5 covered the middle of that chart, visible light and the clock. This chapter is about the two ends.

### UVB: the one that makes a vitamin out of you

Start with the part that is beyond argument.

Sitting in your skin is a molecule called 7-dehydrocholesterol, a relative of cholesterol. When a UVB photon hits it, it breaks a specific bond and rearranges the molecule. Body heat then completes a second rearrangement, and the result is vitamin D3.

This is not a metaphor or a signaling pathway. It is photochemistry. Light energy is doing chemical work directly in your tissue. *(Evidence: well established.)*

And the system has an elegant safety feature. If you stay in the sun, the intermediate compound does not keep piling up into vitamin D forever. Continued UVB exposure converts the excess into inactive products instead.

This is why you cannot overdose on vitamin D from sunlight, no matter how long you stay out, while you absolutely can from high-dose supplements. The body built a limiter into the sun pathway and there is none in a capsule. *(Evidence: well established.)*

How much UVB you actually get depends on several things, and none of them are about willpower.

**Latitude and season.** The further from the equator you live, the lower the winter sun sits, and the more atmosphere the light passes through. Above roughly thirty-five degrees of latitude, there are months where very little usable UVB reaches the ground at all, no matter how bright the day looks or how cold and clear the sky is.

**Time of day.** UVB is concentrated around the middle of the day. Early morning and late afternoon sun is comparatively poor in it, which is one of the few cases in this book where midday is the answer.

**Skin pigmentation.** Melanin absorbs UV. That is its job, and it is protective. It also means that darker skin requires substantially longer exposure to make the same amount of vitamin D. A recommendation written for pale skin at high latitude is not a recommendation for everyone, and a great deal of health writing ignores this.

**Age.** The precursor in skin declines with age, so older skin produces less from the same exposure.

**Glass.** This one surprises people.

&#91;image: Figure 7.2. How far each band penetrates, and why the window matters. Ordinary window glass blocks most UVB while letting UVA through.\]

Sitting in a sunny window is not sun exposure in the sense that matters for vitamin D. The glass removes almost all the UVB. You get the wavelengths that tan and age skin, and essentially none of the wavelength that makes vitamin D. *(Evidence: well established.)*

### Where the vitamin D story gets uncomfortable

Now I have to tell you the part that most books in this genre leave out.

The observational data on vitamin D is spectacular. People with low blood levels have higher rates of nearly everything bad: cardiovascular disease, cancer, autoimmune conditions, infections, all-cause mortality. Study after study, population after population.

So the obvious move was to run large randomized trials and give people vitamin D.

Those trials have largely been disappointing.

The biggest of them, VITAL, randomized more than 25,000 American adults to vitamin D or placebo and followed them for years. It did not find a significant reduction in invasive cancer or major cardiovascular events. Several other large trials have reached broadly similar conclusions on their primary outcomes. *(Manson and colleagues, 2019. Evidence: well established as a result.)*

That is a serious finding and I am not going to talk my way around it.

There are a few honest readings.

One is that vitamin D supplementation simply does not deliver what the observational data suggested. The trials may be right.

A second is that low vitamin D is largely a **marker** rather than a cause. Who has low vitamin D? People who are indoors, sedentary, obese, chronically ill, or elderly. A blood level may be reporting a whole way of living, and a capsule replaces the number without replacing the life.

A third possibility, and the one I find most interesting, is that the trials tested the wrong thing. They tested a molecule. They did not test sunlight. If sunlight does several things, only one of which is vitamin D, then giving people the vitamin in isolation would be expected to underperform going outside.

There is observational work pointing that way. A large Swedish cohort study following nearly thirty thousand women for two decades found that sun avoidance was associated with higher all-cause mortality, with the authors noting the effect size was on a scale comparable to smoking. *(Lindqvist and colleagues, 2016.)*

I want to be careful with that study, because it is exactly the kind of finding people misuse. It is observational. Women who spend more time in the sun differ in many other ways, including wealth, exercise and general health. Confounding is a real concern and the authors said so. It is a provocative result, not a proof. *(Evidence: strong association, unresolved causation.)*

My own position, stated as what it is: **I think sunlight is doing more than making vitamin D, and I think the vitamin D trials are evidence for that rather than against the sun.** That is my working model. The trials are established fact. My interpretation of them is not.

### UVA: the wavelength that talks to your blood vessels

UVA goes deeper than UVB, past the outer layer of skin and into the dermis, where your small blood vessels are.

Work led by Richard Weller and colleagues in Edinburgh found something unexpected. Human skin holds a reservoir of nitrogen compounds that can be converted into nitric oxide, a molecule that relaxes blood vessels. UVA light appears to release nitric oxide from those stores, and does so without needing the usual enzyme that normally produces it.

In controlled studies, UVA exposure of the skin produced a measurable drop in blood pressure. The effect was modest, a few millimeters of mercury, and it was temporary. *(Liu and colleagues, 2014. Evidence: strong but still developing. Whether this produces long-term cardiovascular benefit has not been shown.)*

This matters for the argument of this chapter more than its size suggests, because it is a second, completely separate mechanism by which sunlight affects the body, with nothing to do with vitamin D and nothing to do with the clock.

One thing does not follow. UVA also drives skin aging and contributes to skin cancer risk. Nothing in this section is an argument for a tanning bed, which delivers UVA in concentrations sunlight does not.

### Red and near-infrared: where I have to be most careful

At the far end of the chart is the territory with the loudest claims and the thinnest evidence, and it happens to be the territory closest to the themes of this book. That is exactly when I need to be most disciplined.

The hypothesis goes like this. Cytochrome c oxidase, the final enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain from Chapter 2, absorbs light in the red and near-infrared range. In certain conditions, it can be blocked by nitric oxide sitting where oxygen should bind. Red and near-infrared light, the argument goes, displaces that nitric oxide, unblocks the enzyme, and lets electron flow resume.

If that were straightforwardly true, it would be remarkable. Light acting directly on the mitochondrial battery.

Here is the honest state of it.

**What supports it.** Cytochrome c oxidase does absorb in these wavelengths. Red and near-infrared do penetrate tissue further than other wavelengths. There is a substantial body of laboratory and animal work, and a growing number of small human trials in specific applications such as wound healing, skin conditions, and muscle recovery, some with positive results.

**What challenges it.** The mechanism is genuinely contested among people who study it. Competing explanations include effects on water structure near membranes, on light-sensitive ion channels, and simple local heating. Human trials are mostly small and short. Dosing is inconsistent between studies, and there is evidence that the response is not linear, meaning more is not necessarily better and may be worse. Publication bias in a field with a commercial device market is a real concern.

**What would settle it.** Larger, longer, properly blinded human trials with pre-registered outcomes and standardized dosing, testing whether the proposed mechanism is actually the one operating.

*(Evidence: emerging. The absorption chemistry is real. The clinical claims run well ahead of the data.)*

I am genuinely interested in this area. I also am not going to build a chapter of a book on it, and if you see someone presenting red light therapy as settled mitochondrial medicine, they are ahead of the evidence.

### The layer underneath all of it: your skin is a sense organ

Everything above treats your skin as a surface that light happens to. A place where reactions occur.

That turns out to be an undersell, and the finding that changed my mind about it is recent enough that most clinicians have not encountered it.

In 2015, Haltaufderhyde and Oancea reported that human epidermal skin expresses **opsins**. Not one. Several, including rhodopsin, the pigment your rod cells use to see in dim light, along with opsin 3 and opsin 5. They were found in keratinocytes and melanocytes, the ordinary working cells of your skin.

Since then the functional work has started to arrive. Opsin 3 has been shown to act as a key regulator of UVA-induced photoaging in human dermal fibroblasts. Opsin 5 has been shown to regulate ultraviolet-induced melanin production in human epidermal melanocytes. *(Evidence: the expression is well documented. What each one does is strong and still being worked out.)*

Sit with what that means.

Your skin is not only absorbing light. It is **detecting** it, using the same protein family your eyes use for vision, wired into signaling cascades that change what the cell does.

And the mechanism is the one from Chapter 4. A photon arrives. A chromophore absorbs it and physically changes shape. The protein holding that chromophore changes conformation. A cascade fires. Nothing about that requires energy to be stored anywhere. The photon's job is to flip a switch, and the switch is a protein.

This is the strongest version of the argument that light does more than make vitamin D, and it does not depend on any of the contested claims elsewhere in this book.

### The one published claim of light as an actual energy source

Now the outer edge, because I said this book would name its limits.

Is there any evidence that light adds to the body's energy budget rather than only to its signaling? There is one line of work, and I want to present it accurately rather than either dismissing it or overselling it.

In 2014, Xu and colleagues published a paper in *Journal of Cell Science* with a title that states the claim plainly: light-harvesting chlorophyll pigments enable mammalian mitochondria to capture photonic energy and produce ATP. The argument is that chlorophyll metabolites from dietary plants accumulate in mitochondria, and that in the presence of light those pigments can raise ATP production. A follow-up in *Scientific Reports* in 2016 extended the idea to mitochondrial light sensing.

Here is my honest assessment.

**What supports it.** These are real papers in real journals. The proposed mechanism is not absurd: chlorophyll derivatives are light-absorbing molecules, they are present in plant food, and they do end up in tissue.

**What challenges it.** The work has not been widely replicated. The effect requires dietary plant pigments, so it is not a property of human biology on its own. The magnitude of any contribution to a whole-body energy budget has not been established, and it is almost certainly very small. And light has to reach the mitochondria in question, which limits it to superficial tissue.

**What would settle it.** Independent replication in mammals, with a measured contribution to ATP production in living tissue, and a comparison against animals not fed chlorophyll metabolites.

*(Evidence: emerging. I include it because it is the only published mechanism I know of by which light could add energy rather than only information, and because a book that labels its own speculations should not quietly omit other people's.)*

### So where does that leave the framing

**Light is information, and light is a minor energy input.**

The information is overwhelming in importance. It sets the clock, and the clock schedules the rest of this book.

The energy input is real and small. UVB does genuine photochemistry and makes vitamin D. UVA releases nitric oxide. Red and near-infrared may act on the last enzyme in the electron transport chain. Skin opsins detect light and trigger cascades. And there is one contested claim that dietary chlorophyll metabolites let mitochondria capture a little light directly.

What none of that amounts to is a solar panel. The electrons that power you come from food. But the older framing, that light is purely a timing signal with a side order of vitamin D, does not survive contact with the last fifteen years of photobiology either.

You are lit, you are timed by it, and a small, specific fraction of what lands on you is turned into chemistry.

### The bill

Everything above comes with a cost, and I am not going to bury it.

Ultraviolet radiation damages DNA. It is classified as a known human carcinogen. It causes the common skin cancers and contributes to melanoma, and the damage accumulates across a lifetime. Severe sunburns, especially in childhood, raise melanoma risk. *(Evidence: well established. Not in dispute anywhere.)*

I got very dark in Rio and in Abu Dhabi. I was in my twenties, I was outdoors constantly, and I was not thinking about any of this. I will not tell you that was free.

So here is how I hold it, and this is a judgment call rather than a finding.

The evidence supports **regular, moderate, unburnt** sun exposure. It does not support burning, and it does not support the idea that more is always better. Burning is the thing most clearly linked to the worst outcome.

And the calculation is personal in a way that general advice cannot capture. Skin type, family history of skin cancer, latitude, season, occupation and medications all move it. If you have a personal or family history of skin cancer, this is a conversation with a dermatologist, not a chapter in a book.

What I will say is that "avoid the sun entirely" and "take a vitamin D capsule instead" is a trade that the evidence does not clearly support, and that the capsule has not performed the way the observational data promised.

Chapter 18 is where I get specific about timing and practice. For now I want you holding the mechanisms, and holding them with the right amount of confidence in each one.

### What comes next

Everything in this chapter has been about light arriving.

The next chapter is about light not arriving, and about the fact that its absence is not an empty space in the day. Darkness is its own signal, it carries its own instructions, and almost nobody alive today has experienced the real thing.

## Chapter 8. The Power of Darkness

In the White Mountains of Arizona, near the New Mexico border, I learned what darkness actually is.

I was thirteen. My father and I were on a bear hunt on horseback, following the hounds. Somewhere in the chase the day got away from us, the sun went down, and the two of us were out in the forest.

Pitch black. Not the dark of a bedroom with the light switched off. No streetlights. No glow on the horizon. Nothing.

I was scared. So we did the only sensible thing. We held onto our horses and let them find the way.

And they did. Step by step, through a forest we could not see, back to camp.

What I remember most is not the fear. It is the silence, and the strange beauty of being somewhere with no light in it at all.

The horses could manage because their eyes are built for it. Horses have far more of the light-sensitive rod cells than we do, plus a reflective layer behind the retina that gives every photon a second chance to be caught. Our eyes adapt too, but slowly. It takes somewhere around twenty to thirty minutes in real darkness for human night vision to come fully online, and most people never stay in the dark long enough to find out.

I have thought about that night many times since, and here is what I have come to believe about it.

That forest was not an absence of anything. It was a signal my body had been built to receive, and which I have almost never received since.

### Darkness is not the absence of a signal

Here is the mistake almost everyone makes, and I made it for years.

We treat light as the input and darkness as what is left over when the input stops. Light does things. Dark is the gap in between.

That is backwards.

Your body does not merely stop responding at night. It actively runs a completely different program, and that program begins only when darkness is detected. Hormones are released. Repair processes start. Metabolism shifts. Temperature falls. A whole shift of work begins that cannot begin while the lights are on.

Darkness is not the pause between the signals. Darkness is one of the two signals.

And notice that the oldest creation account we have says exactly that, in its first few lines. Light is made, and the very next act is to divide it from the darkness, and then both are given names. Not light created with darkness left over as the gap. Two things, separated on purpose, each one named.

I am not building anything on that, for the reasons I gave in Chapter 4. I only think it is worth noticing that treating darkness as a thing in its own right, rather than as the absence of a thing, is a very old idea, and that we may be the first people in history to have mislaid it.

And the clock does not measure either one on its own. **It measures the difference between them.** That is why this chapter matters so much, and why both halves of the modern problem are equally serious.

### Melatonin, and what it is actually for

The messenger for the night program is melatonin.

It is made in the pineal gland, a structure about the size of a grain of rice near the center of your brain. It is manufactured from serotonin, and its release is controlled by the master clock from Chapter 5, working through a pathway that ends at the pineal.

The critical feature is this. **The pathway is actively shut off by light.** Not dimmed. Shut off.

Melatonin is not primarily a sedative, and this is the most common misunderstanding about it. It is a chemical announcement. It circulates through your body and every tissue with a clock can read it, and the message it carries is simply: *it is night now*.

Nocturnal animals, which are asleep during the day and active at night, release melatonin at night too, exactly as we do. If melatonin were a sleep hormone, that would make no sense. As a darkness signal, it makes perfect sense. *(Evidence: well established.)*

Melatonin appears to do more than announce the time, and this is where the evidence becomes more interesting and less settled. It is a capable antioxidant, and there is a body of work, much of it from Russel Reiter, Dun-Xian Tan and colleagues, arguing that melatonin is also produced inside mitochondria, where it would be positioned to deal with reactive oxygen species at the source.

If that turns out to be right, it would connect the night signal directly to the mitochondrial protection theme of this book. I find it a genuinely appealing idea, and I am going to hold it loosely. *(Evidence: emerging. The antioxidant chemistry is established. Mitochondrial synthesis and its physiological importance in humans are not settled.)*

### What an ordinary lamp does

Most people believe this is a problem about phones.

It is bigger than phones, and the research that showed it used nothing more exotic than a lit room.

&#91;image: Figure 8.1. The effect of ordinary evening room lighting on melatonin, drawn from the pattern reported by Gooley and colleagues in 2011. Not a screen. Not a bright light. A normally lit living room.\]

In 2011, Gooley and colleagues at Harvard did something very simple. They exposed people to ordinary room light in the hours before bed, at levels below two hundred lux, which is dimmer than most kitchens, and compared them to people kept in dim light.

Room light delayed melatonin onset in almost every person tested, and shortened the duration of elevated melatonin by roughly ninety minutes. Light during the sleep period suppressed melatonin substantially further. *(Evidence: well established, and replicated.)*

So an ordinary evening, in an ordinary house, with the ceiling light on, measurably shortens your biological night.

And screens make it worse rather than being the whole story. Reading on a light-emitting tablet before bed, compared to a printed book, has been shown to delay melatonin release, delay sleep onset, and leave people less alert the following morning. *(Chang and colleagues, 2015.)*

Two details about evening light matter practically, and I want them in your head before Chapter 18.

**Wavelength.** The melanopsin cells from Chapter 4 peak in sensitivity near 480 nanometers, in the blue. Long wavelength light, red and amber, produces a much weaker response at the same intensity. This is the real reason behind red bulbs and warm incandescent lighting in the evening. It is not that red is magic. It is that red is comparatively invisible to the cell that reports to your clock. *(Evidence: well established in principle.)*

**Intensity and duration.** These cells add light up over time. A brief trip to a bright bathroom is not the same event as three hours under a ceiling light.

### The contrast, from both ends

Now put this chapter together with Chapter 4, because the two halves of the problem are one problem.

&#91;image: Figure 8.2. Light reaching the eye across twenty-four hours, on a logarithmic scale. The environment human biology was built in, against a typical modern day. The days have collapsed downward and the nights have risen up.\]

Look at what has happened to the gap.

The world we evolved in delivered tens of thousands of lux during the day and something close to absolute darkness at night. The difference between day and night was a factor of millions.

A typical modern day delivers a few hundred lux indoors and a few hundred lux in the evening. The difference between day and night, for a person who works indoors and watches television at night, can be smaller than the difference between two rooms in their own house.

The clock is not being given a weak signal. It is being given an **ambiguous** one.

And this is now the normal human condition rather than an unusual one. Satellite mapping of artificial light at night found that the large majority of the world's population, and close to everyone in the United States and Europe, lives under light-polluted skies. Most people alive have never seen the sky my father and I sat under in Arizona. *(Falchi and colleagues, 2016. Evidence: well established.)*

### What happens when night work becomes the job

The hardest evidence on all of this comes from people who live it at the extreme.

In 2007, the International Agency for Research on Cancer reviewed the evidence on shift work involving circadian disruption and classified it as probably carcinogenic to humans. The classification was re-examined and maintained in a later review.

That is not a claim about a light bulb in a bedroom, and I am not going to stretch it into one. Shift work involves disrupted sleep, disrupted eating, disrupted social life and light at the wrong times, all together, for years. Separating those factors is genuinely hard and the research community says so.

But it tells you the system is not indifferent to this. When circadian disruption is severe and sustained, the consequences are serious enough that a major cancer agency has taken a position on it. *(Evidence: well established as a classification; the specific mechanism and the size of the risk remain under study.)*

### The season you no longer have

One more thing gets lost when nights stop being dark, and it operates on a longer timescale.

Day length changes across the year, and that change is information. Photoperiod drives reproduction, migration, hibernation, coat changes and metabolic shifts across an enormous range of species. It is one of the most reliable environmental signals there is, because unlike temperature or food supply, the calendar never lies.

Humans still carry seasonal biology. A study published in 2015 examining gene expression across populations found that a substantial fraction of genes showed seasonal variation, including genes involved in immune function and inflammation, with opposite patterns in the northern and southern hemispheres. *(Dopico and colleagues, 2015. Evidence: strong but still developing.)*

We are not seasonal animals in the way a bear is. We are also not the flat, identical-every-day creatures our indoor lives imply.

A house held at the same temperature, lit to the same brightness, stocked with the same food, in December and in June, is sending a body built for seasons a year with no seasons in it. What that costs us, honestly, is not well quantified. I flag it as a real question rather than a settled harm.

### What I actually think about that night in Arizona

I was a kid, I was frightened, and I could not see my own hands.

And biologically, that was one of the more complete nights I have ever had. My melatonin was doing exactly what it evolved to do, uninterrupted, because there was not a single photon anywhere in that forest to stop it.

I have spent most of my life since then in places where that is impossible.

Which is why, when I get to the practical chapters, darkness is going to get the same weight as sunlight. Not as an afterthought. Not as "and also sleep in a dark room." As one half of the signal your entire biology is timed by.

The day tells your body when to work. The night is when the work gets done.

### What comes next

I have said several times now that repair happens at night.

I have not yet said what that repair actually is, why the pressure to sleep builds the way it does, or what it has to do with the energy you spent during the day.

## Chapter 9. Sleep: The Night Shift

For about nine years I was in the restaurant business.

If you have never worked in restaurants, here is the thing to understand about them. The business does not end when the last guest leaves. It ends some time after that, once the kitchen is broken down, the books are reconciled, and somebody has dealt with whatever went wrong that evening.

And I was not only doing that. Those years overlapped with running a company, and with everything else I had going. I have almost always had more than one business at a time.

I want to be careful here, because I am about to write a chapter telling you that sleep is not optional, and I was not a model of it.

What I can tell you honestly is that I understood sleep the way most driven people understand it. As the thing that happens when the work is finished. As the least defended block in the day, and therefore the one that gets taken when something else needs the hours.

What I did not understand, at all, is that sleep is not the absence of activity.

It is a shift. It is when a specific set of jobs gets done. And the pressure that makes you want it is, quite literally, a receipt.

### Two forces, not one

Start with the question people rarely ask. Why do you get sleepy when you do?

The answer, worked out by Alexander Borbély and refined over forty years, is that two separate systems are running at once.

&#91;image: Figure 9.1. The two-process model. Sleep pressure climbs with every waking hour and discharges during sleep. The clock's alerting signal runs on its own schedule, set by light. Sleep goes well when the two line up.\]

**The first is sleep pressure.** It builds from the moment you wake and keeps building the longer you stay up. It has nothing to do with the time of day. It is a running total of how long you have been awake and, as you will see, how hard you have been working.

**The second is the clock.** This is Chapter 5's suprachiasmatic nucleus, set by light, producing an alerting signal that rises and falls on a twenty-four-hour schedule regardless of when you last slept.

You fall asleep easily when sleep pressure is high **and** the clock's alerting signal is low. When those two line up, sleep is effortless.

When they do not, sleep is a fight. *(Evidence: well established.)*

This is why so much advice about sleep fails. People try to fix sleep by addressing only one of these.

Staying up later builds more pressure, but if your clock is telling your body it is still afternoon, more pressure does not solve it. That is jet lag, and that is also what a chaotic schedule produces without any traveling.

And this is the honest reason a long afternoon nap can wreck a night. It discharges pressure you were going to need later.

### Sleep pressure is made of spent energy

Now the connection that made me rewrite how I think about sleep, and the most literal link in this entire book between the battery and how you actually feel.

What is sleep pressure physically made of?

&#91;image: Figure 9.2. Where sleep pressure comes from. ATP is spent doing work, losing phosphates until what remains is adenosine. Adenosine accumulates through the day and binds receptors in the brain, and that binding is what sleepiness feels like.\]

Go back to Chapter 2. ATP is the cell's working currency, and it carries three phosphate groups. When a cell does work, it breaks one off, leaving ADP. Break another and you have AMP.

Strip the phosphates entirely and what remains is a molecule called **adenosine**.

Adenosine accumulates in the brain across the waking day. It binds to specific receptors, and that binding is a major part of what sleepiness is.

Stop and look at what that means.

The feeling of needing sleep is, in significant part, **your brain detecting the accumulated residue of energy you have already spent.** It is not a vague sense of tiredness. It is a chemical record of consumption. A receipt.

And during sleep, that adenosine is cleared. The pressure genuinely discharges.

Which also explains caffeine, and explains it more precisely than most people realize. Caffeine does not give you energy. It has no calories and it does not make ATP. What it does is occupy the adenosine receptors, so the adenosine that is already there cannot deliver its message.

The debt is not paid. The bill is simply covered up, and when the caffeine clears, the adenosine is still there, along with everything that accumulated in the meantime. *(Evidence: well established.)*

I am not telling anyone to stop drinking coffee. I am telling you that what you are doing is hiding a measurement, not changing the thing being measured.

### What actually happens while you are out

Sleep is not one state. It cycles, roughly every ninety minutes, through stages that do different work.

**Deep slow-wave sleep** dominates the first part of the night. Brain activity slows into large synchronized waves. Your largest pulse of growth hormone is released here. This is the repair-heavy portion of the night, and it is the part you lose first when your sleep is cut short.

**REM sleep** dominates the second half. The brain becomes highly active, eyes move rapidly, the body is largely paralyzed, and most vivid dreaming happens. REM appears to be important for emotional processing and for certain kinds of memory.

Both matter, and here is the practical consequence. They are not evenly distributed. If you get six hours instead of eight, you do not lose a quarter of each. You lose a disproportionate share of the REM sleep that was scheduled for the end. *(Evidence: well established.)*

Across both stages, sleep is doing work that is genuinely hard to do while awake: consolidating memory, clearing metabolic products, rebalancing hormones, and carrying out repair.

### How fast the damage shows up

The number that changed my mind about this is from 1999.

Spiegel, Leproult and Van Cauter restricted healthy young men to four hours in bed for six nights. Six nights. Not months.

Their glucose tolerance deteriorated substantially. The researchers described the metabolic profile as resembling something closer to early impairment than to healthy young adults. It reversed with recovery sleep. *(Evidence: well established, and replicated many times since.)*

Six nights. Healthy young men. Nothing else changed.

If you have ever wondered whether sleep is really a metabolic issue or just a comfort issue, that study is the answer. You can degrade someone's glucose handling in under a week with nothing but a shortened night.

### Sleep and the battery, in the research

There is a newer line of work that sits right on the theme of this book, and it comes from fruit flies.

In 2020, Vaccaro and colleagues published a study asking a blunt question: what actually kills an animal deprived of sleep? They found that reactive oxygen species accumulated, strikingly, in the gut. And when they gave the flies antioxidants, sleep-deprived flies survived far longer than they otherwise would.

A year earlier, Kempf and colleagues in Gero Miesenböck's laboratory had reported something even more directly on this book's theme, under a title that could have been written for it: a potassium channel subunit that couples mitochondrial electron transport to sleep. The cells that decide when the animal sleeps appear to be reading the state of the electron transport chain itself. The same group has continued to push into what they describe as the mitochondrial origins of sleep pressure.

If that generalizes, and I want to be very clear that generalizing from flies to humans is exactly the move that gets health writers into trouble, it would suggest that sleep pressure is not only about adenosine accumulating. It may also be a response to the oxidative cost of having been awake and working.

Which would fit this book's framework almost too neatly, and that is precisely why I am flagging it rather than building on it. *(Evidence: emerging, in invertebrate models. I find it compelling. It is not established in humans.)*

### A live scientific argument, shown honestly

There is one more sleep finding you have probably heard, and it is currently in dispute. I am including it because I think showing you an unresolved argument is more useful than pretending science speaks with one voice.

In 2013, Xie and colleagues published work in mice describing a system, later called the glymphatic system, in which spaces between brain cells expand during sleep and fluid flushes through, clearing metabolic waste. It was an elegant finding and it spread widely. The brain cleans itself at night.

In 2024, a study using a different method reported broadly the opposite: reduced brain clearance during sleep and anesthesia.

So where does that leave us? With an open question. The idea that sleep supports some form of brain maintenance remains plausible and is supported by other lines of evidence. The specific glymphatic mechanism, and whether flow increases or decreases during sleep, is genuinely contested right now. *(Evidence: contested. Watch this space.)*

I would rather tell you that than repeat a tidy story that may be revised.

### The finding that changed what I tell people

For years, the advice was about duration. Get eight hours.

More recent work suggests that may be the wrong emphasis.

Using accelerometer data from a very large cohort, Windred and colleagues reported in 2024 that **sleep regularity**, meaning how consistent your sleep and wake times are from day to day, predicted mortality risk more strongly than sleep duration did.

Think about why that would make sense given everything in Part II. Regularity is what keeps the clock anchored. An irregular schedule is the jet lag from Chapter 5, generated at home, every week, without going anywhere. *(Evidence: strong observational finding. It does not prove that fixing regularity fixes outcomes, and a trial would be needed to show that.)*

That finding is why, when we get to Chapter 18, consistency is going to get more emphasis than hours.

### The temperature detail almost nobody uses

One practical thing follows directly from Chapter 5, and it is the single most underused sleep lever I know.

Your core body temperature must **fall** for sleep to begin properly. That fall is part of the signal.

The body does it by opening blood vessels in the hands and feet, dumping heat outward. That is why warm hands and feet at bedtime are a sign that the process is working, and why a bedroom that is too warm makes it harder to fall asleep.

It is also the counterintuitive reason a warm bath or shower before bed can help. The warmth draws blood to the surface, and when you get out, the heat loss accelerates the core temperature drop. You are not warming yourself to sleep. You are using warmth to cool faster. *(Evidence: well established.)*

### What I would tell my younger self

Those restaurant years, I treated sleep as the flexible item in the budget.

What I understand now is that I was not borrowing time. I was skipping a shift. The adenosine still accumulated. The repair window still closed. The glucose handling still changed. None of that was waiting politely for me to get around to it.

The work does not disappear when you skip it. It just does not get done.

# Part III: The Engine

## Chapter 10. Inside the Mitochondria

Between the ages of fourteen and seventeen I raced bicycles.

If you have ever raced, you know a sensation that is hard to describe to anyone who has not. There is a point on a long climb where your legs are still turning, your lungs are working as hard as they can, and something underneath both of those has simply run out. It is not pain exactly. It is a ceiling.

I did not have language for it then. I do now.

What I was running into was the maximum rate at which my mitochondria could move electrons from food to oxygen. That is what endurance is. Not willpower, not lung size. The rate at which a chain of proteins in your cells can process electrons.

This chapter is about that chain. We are going inside the engine and watching it run, one step at a time, because everything in the rest of this book, the reactive oxygen of Chapter 11, the overfeeding of Chapter 17, the exercise of Chapter 19, is a variation on what happens in here.

### The problem the cell had to solve

Remember the fire from Chapter 1.

Burn sugar in one step and you get carbon dioxide, water, and a lot of heat. The chemistry is the same as what your cells do. The difference is entirely in how it is staged.

Because if you released all that energy at once inside a cell, you would cook the cell. And you would capture none of it.

So the cell does something clever. It moves the electrons downhill in small steps, and at several of those steps it takes some of the released energy and does work with it.

&#91;image: Figure 10.1. The electron transport chain. Electrons enter from food and pass through a series of protein complexes, falling to lower energy at each handoff. At three of those steps, the released energy is used to pump protons out across the inner membrane.\]

### Step by step

**Electrons arrive.** NADH, the loaded shuttle from Chapter 6, docks at **Complex I** and hands over its electrons. A second entry point, **Complex II**, accepts electrons from a different carrier called FADH2. Notice in the figure that Complex II does not pump any protons. It is a doorway rather than a pump, and that is why the fuels that enter there yield slightly less.

**The electrons travel.** They move to a small mobile carrier called coenzyme Q, which ferries them through the fatty interior of the membrane to **Complex III**. From there another mobile carrier, cytochrome c, takes them to **Complex IV**.

**Oxygen catches them.** At Complex IV, the electrons are handed to oxygen, along with protons, and the product is water.

One detail I am going to leave sitting here and pick up properly in Chapter 14. When structural biologists finally resolved Complex I finely enough to see individual water molecules inside it, they found that the proton pathway through the membrane is partly built out of ordered water. The pumping you just read about does not simply happen in water. Part of it happens through water, along chains of water molecules that have to be lined up correctly for a proton to cross.

This is why you breathe. Not to inflate your lungs. Oxygen's job is to stand at the end of that chain and accept electrons. Without an acceptor, the whole chain backs up within seconds, which is why a few minutes without oxygen is fatal in a way that a few days without food is not.

The water produced there is real water, and it adds up to a meaningful quantity across a day. Your mitochondria are, quietly, a water source. Chapter 14 comes back to that.

**And at three of those steps, protons get pumped.** Complexes I, III and IV each use the energy released as electrons fall to push protons from the matrix out across the inner membrane, against the gradient.

*(Evidence: all of the above is well established. This is textbook biochemistry, worked out over decades and confirmed structurally.)*

### The river with three dams

The analogy I keep coming back to is a river.

Water flows downhill. That is what it does, and it will do it whether or not anything is there to catch it.

Now put three dams along the river. At each one, some of the falling water's energy is captured and used to pump water up into a reservoir above.

The river still ends up at the bottom. Nothing has violated anything. But along the way, a store of potential energy has been built up in the reservoir, and that store can be released later, on demand, to do whatever you want.

The electrons are the river. Oxygen is the sea. The reservoir is the proton gradient across the inner membrane, and the voltage we measured in Chapter 2, those 150 to 180 millivolts, is how full the reservoir is.

There is an important detail in the analogy. The dams only pump when the water is flowing. If the river stops, the pumping stops. If the reservoir gets too full, it becomes harder to pump against it, and the flow slows.

Hold that thought. It is the whole of Chapter 11.

### Two forms of the same stored energy

One refinement, because it matters later.

When protons are pumped out, two things change at once. There are more protons on one side, which is a concentration difference. And those protons carry positive charge, which is an electrical difference.

Biochemists call the combination the proton-motive force, and it has two components: the voltage, and the pH difference. In mitochondria, most of it is carried as voltage.

This matters because the two are interchangeable. A cell can shift the balance between them. It is one more example of the theme from Chapter 3: what looks like a single number is actually a system with room to move. *(Evidence: well established.)*

### The turbine

So the reservoir is full. Now what?

&#91;image: Figure 10.2. ATP synthase. Protons flowing back across the membrane turn a ring, which turns a driveshaft, which drives conformational changes in a three-part head where ADP and phosphate are pressed together into ATP.\]

Protons want to come back. They are crowded on one side and the other side is negatively charged, so both forces push the same way. But the membrane will not let them through.

Except in one place.

Sitting in the membrane is ATP synthase, and it is the strangest machine in your body. I said in Chapter 1 that I would not let an analogy replace the biology. Here the analogy *is* the biology.

Protons flow through a channel and into a ring of protein subunits embedded in the membrane. As each proton passes, it causes the ring to rotate one notch. The ring is attached to a central shaft, and the shaft runs down into a larger head sitting in the matrix.

The head has three sites where ADP and phosphate can bind. As the asymmetric shaft turns inside it, it deforms each site in turn, and that mechanical deformation squeezes ADP and phosphate together into ATP.

This is a rotary motor. It was not named one by analogy. Researchers have attached fluorescent markers to the shaft and **watched it spin under a microscope**, which is one of the more satisfying experiments in the history of biology.

It turns on the order of a hundred times a second, producing three ATP per revolution. Paul Boyer and John Walker received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1997 for working out this mechanism. *(Evidence: well established, including direct observation of rotation.)*

There are thousands of these in a single mitochondrion. Hundreds of mitochondria in many cells. Trillions of cells. All of them spinning, right now, as you read this.

### The leak, and why it is not a flaw

Now the detail that turns this from a description of machinery into an argument.

The system is not perfectly sealed. Protons leak back across the inner membrane without passing through ATP synthase and without producing any ATP at all. That energy comes out as heat.

From an engineering standpoint this looks like a defect. You built a beautiful gradient and it leaks.

A substantial fraction of your resting metabolic rate is accounted for by this leak. It is not a rounding error. It is a design feature, and here is why.

First, it produces heat, and you are a warm-blooded animal who needs to produce heat continuously. In brown fat, this is taken to an extreme. A protein called UCP1 opens a dedicated proton channel that makes no ATP whatsoever, converting the entire gradient directly into warmth. That is how a newborn, who cannot shiver effectively, stays warm, and it is what cold exposure activates in adults. *(Evidence: well established.)*

Second, and this is the part that matters most for this book, the leak **keeps the flow moving**.

Go back to the dams. If the reservoir becomes completely full and nothing is drawing from it, the pumps stall and the river backs up. The controlled leak is a spillway. It keeps the voltage from climbing too high, which keeps electrons moving down the chain, which prevents them from sitting on the proteins and falling off onto oxygen the wrong way.

That is the preview of the next chapter, and it is why Chapter 3 insisted that maximum charge is not the goal.

**Perfect efficiency, in this system, would be dangerous.**

### What I was actually hitting on that climb

Back to the bicycle.

What I ran into on those climbs was the ceiling of this whole apparatus. How much NADH my muscles could deliver. How many complexes I had. How fast they could turn over. How much oxygen my blood could bring to them.

And here is the part I did not know at fifteen and which is the reason Chapter 19 exists. That ceiling is not fixed.

Every one of those components can be built. More mitochondria, more complexes, more capacity. The training I was doing was, without my knowing it, a signal to build more of this machinery.

The ceiling I was hitting was the ceiling I had built by that point. Not the one I was stuck with.

## Chapter 11. Sparks and Signals

When I was competing, the advice was simple and everyone repeated it.

Hard training produces free radicals. Free radicals cause damage. Therefore take antioxidants, and take plenty of them. Vitamin C. Vitamin E. Whatever was in the tub that year.

It made perfect sense. Damage is bad. Antioxidants fight damage. Take more antioxidants.

In 2009, a group led by Michael Ristow published a study that took that reasoning apart.

They put healthy men through a four-week exercise program. Half of them also took vitamin C and vitamin E in the doses that were then routinely recommended to athletes.

The men who exercised without supplements improved their insulin sensitivity, as you would expect. Exercise does that.

The men who exercised **and** took the antioxidants did not show that improvement. And the molecular markers of the body's own antioxidant defense system, the genes that build your internal protection, went up in the unsupplemented group and were blunted in the supplemented one. *(Ristow and colleagues, 2009.)*

Read that again, because it inverts the whole model.

The antioxidants did not enhance the benefit of exercise. They appear to have interfered with it. And they did it by removing the very signal the body uses to decide it needs to get stronger.

This chapter is about why.

### Where the sparks come from

Go back to the chain from Chapter 10.

Electrons pass from complex to complex, and the handoffs are good but not perfect. Occasionally an electron slips off early and lands directly on an oxygen molecule.

Oxygen with an extra electron is called superoxide, and it is reactive. It is the first of a family of molecules collectively called reactive oxygen species.

Superoxide is converted by an enzyme called superoxide dismutase into hydrogen peroxide. Hydrogen peroxide is more stable, which is useful and, as you will see, essential to the whole story. From there, other enzymes, catalase and the glutathione system among them, convert it to plain water.

So your cells have a layered handling system: a chain that makes reactive oxygen, an enzyme that converts it, and more enzymes that dispose of it. *(Evidence: well established.)*

Now the crucial question. Why did your cells not evolve to simply produce less of it?

### Because it is a message

&#91;image: Figure 11.1. Two fates for the same molecule. In a small, brief amount, hydrogen peroxide acts as a signal that switches on the cell's own defense and repair machinery. In a large, sustained amount, it overwhelms the handling system and starts reacting with whatever is nearby.\]

Here is what the last thirty years of research has established, and it is one of the bigger reversals in modern biology.

**Hydrogen peroxide is a signaling molecule.**

It works by reacting, reversibly, with sulfur-containing amino acids on certain proteins. That reaction changes the protein's shape, which changes what it does, and it can be undone. It is an on-off switch, and the switch is thrown by the redox state of the cell.

This means your cells can detect, in real time, how hard they are working and how much strain their mitochondria are under, and can change gene expression in response.

One of the best-characterized examples is a protein called Nrf2. Under ordinary conditions it is held inactive. When oxidative signaling rises, Nrf2 is released, travels to the nucleus, and switches on a whole battery of genes: antioxidant enzymes, detoxification systems, repair machinery.

So the reactive oxygen produced by hard work is the trigger for building better protection against reactive oxygen. *(Evidence: well established. Helmut Sies and Dean Jones's framing of the redox code is the standard reference.)*

Now the Ristow result makes sense. Flood the system with antioxidants and you erase the message before it can be read. The cell never learns it needs to adapt.

### The shape of everything

This brings us to a curve that I think is the single most useful picture in this entire book.

&#91;image: Figure 11.2. Hormesis. A small, recoverable stress produces an adaptive response and leaves the system better off. The same stress, sustained or oversized, does the opposite.\]

The technical name is hormesis. A stress that is small enough, and brief enough, that the body can recover from it triggers an adaptive response that leaves you better off than before. The same stress, sustained or too large, simply causes harm.

Applied to reactive oxygen, this is called mitohormesis.

But look at the x-axis of that figure, because the curve is not only about reactive oxygen.

Exercise is on that curve. So is heat. So is cold. So is going without food. So is psychological challenge. Every single intervention in Part VI of this book lives somewhere on that shape.

Which means the question is never "is this good for me." The question is always **how much, how often, and can I recover from it.**

And it means two opposite failures are possible, which is why I drew both ends. A sedentary, temperature-controlled, never-hungry, never-challenged life sits at the left end of that curve, where nothing is asking the body to adapt. Overtraining, chronic inflammation and burnout sit at the right end. Both are failures. They are not opposites to be balanced against each other. They are two ways of falling off the same curve.

*(Evidence: hormesis is well established as a phenomenon across many stressors. Mitohormesis specifically is strong and still developing. The precise position and width of the useful range are different for every person and every stressor, and nobody can give you a number.)*

### The overcharged mitochondrion, revisited

Chapter 3 made a claim I can now explain properly.

Reactive oxygen production is not constant. It depends heavily on the state of the system, and the worst state is a familiar one.

When membrane potential is high and ATP demand is low, the chain backs up. Electrons sit on the complexes longer, waiting. And an electron sitting on a protein in an oxygen-rich environment has time to slip off.

Worse, under certain conditions electrons can be pushed **backwards** up the chain into Complex I, a phenomenon called reverse electron transport, which is a particularly potent generator of superoxide. It requires exactly the combination we have been describing: a highly reduced pool of carriers and a high membrane potential. *(Evidence: well established.)*

So the dangerous condition is not hard work. It is **fuel without demand**.

A person who eats constantly and moves rarely is running their mitochondria in precisely the state that produces the most reactive oxygen for the least benefit. High charge, low flow, electrons backing up.

That is Chapter 17 in a sentence, and now you know the mechanism.

### What the big antioxidant trials found

If the simple model were right, giving people antioxidants should improve health. It was tested at scale, and the results are sobering.

Two large trials of beta-carotene supplementation in people at high risk of lung cancer found **more** lung cancer in the supplemented groups, not less. One of them was stopped early.

The SELECT trial, testing vitamin E and selenium for prostate cancer prevention in more than thirty thousand men, found a statistically significant **increase** in prostate cancer in the vitamin E group.

Meta-analyses of antioxidant supplement trials have generally failed to show mortality benefit, and some have suggested harm at high doses. *(Evidence: well established. These are large, well-conducted trials with unwelcome results.)*

I want to be careful about what this does and does not mean.

It does not mean antioxidants are bad. It does not mean vitamin E is poison. It does not mean you should avoid colorful vegetables, which are associated with good outcomes in essentially every population ever studied.

What it means is that **isolated antioxidant compounds at high doses are a different intervention from eating plants**, and that a system built to use oxidative signaling does not necessarily benefit from having that signaling suppressed.

The simple story, free radicals bad and antioxidants good, was wrong. Not because free radicals cannot cause damage, but because the body is not trying to eliminate them. It is trying to regulate them.

Which is Chapter 3's argument again, arriving from a completely different direction. Not maximum. Regulated.

### Where this leaves practice

I am not going to give you protocols here. That is Part VI. But I want to name what changes once you understand this chapter.

The goal is not to minimize oxidative stress. The goal is to produce it in the right pattern: real demand, followed by real recovery, repeatedly.

That is what training is. It is also, as you will see, what heat exposure, cold exposure and fasting windows are. Every one of them is a controlled stress with a recovery period attached, and the recovery period is not the part where nothing happens. The recovery period is where the adaptation gets built.

And it reframes something people get wrong constantly. If you are training hard and never recovering, you are not being disciplined. You are on the wrong side of the curve, and you will get worse, not better.

I wish I had understood that at twenty.

## Chapter 12. Mitochondria as Environmental Sensors

Over five years I went back and forth between San Diego and Abu Dhabi, and I also spent time in Europe and in Brazil.

Different latitudes. Different day lengths. Different heat. Different food. Different altitudes. Different water.

My DNA did not change across any of those moves. Not one letter of it.

And yet my body was clearly different in each place. Different sleep, different appetite, different heat tolerance, different capacity for work. Some of that was training and some was acclimatization, and all of it was my biology behaving differently in response to where I was standing.

Which raises the question this chapter is built around.

If the instructions do not change, what is reading the environment and deciding what to do about it?

Douglas Wallace, one of the founders of mitochondrial genetics, has spent decades arguing that the answer is substantially the mitochondria. That they are not merely power plants but the interface between the outside world and the inside of the cell. Between environment and biology.

I find that framework persuasive, and I want to show you why, and also be clear about where it is established and where it is still an argument.

### Not permanent objects

Start with something that surprises most people.

The mitochondria in your cells right now are not the ones you had a few weeks ago.

&#91;image: Figure 12.1. The mitochondrial life cycle. Units are built, merged, split, tested and either kept or destroyed. The population is continuously turned over.\]

They are built, merged, split, tested, and destroyed continuously. The whole population is a flow rather than a fixed inventory.

**Building.** When a cell needs more capacity, it makes more. The central switch is a protein called PGC-1α, which turns on the program for building new mitochondria. And one of its main activators is AMPK, an enzyme that monitors the ratio of AMP to ATP and detects when energy is running low.

Stop on that, because it is the mechanism behind half of Part VI. **The signal to build more mitochondria is energy being spent.** Not energy being supplied. Spent.

**Merging.** Mitochondria fuse with each other, mixing their contents. A mitochondrion that is struggling can be rescued by fusing with healthy neighbors and sharing their proteins and their DNA. It is a dilution strategy.

**Splitting.** Fission does the opposite. It isolates the portion that cannot be rescued into a separate unit, so that the damage can be dealt with without taking the whole network down.

This constant fusion and fission is called mitochondrial dynamics, and the balance between them shifts with the state of the cell. *(Evidence: all well established.)*

### And then the test

So the cell has isolated a suspect unit. How does it decide whether that unit is worth keeping?

It cannot open it up and look inside. It needs a test it can run from the outside, quickly, on every unit, continuously.

Here is the test it uses, and it is the payoff of everything since Chapter 2.

&#91;image: Figure 12.2. The quality control mechanism. A protein called PINK1 is continuously imported into healthy mitochondria and destroyed. Import requires membrane potential. When the voltage collapses, PINK1 accumulates on the outer surface and marks the unit for destruction.\]

A protein called PINK1 is constantly being made and constantly arriving at mitochondria. In a healthy mitochondrion, PINK1 is imported across the inner membrane and immediately chopped up and disposed of. It never accumulates.

Here is the crucial detail. **That import requires membrane potential.** The voltage is what drives the protein across.

So when a mitochondrion loses its voltage, import fails. PINK1 cannot get in. It builds up on the outer surface instead.

And PINK1 sitting on the outside is the alarm. It recruits a second protein, Parkin, which tags the mitochondrion for destruction, and the cell's recycling machinery wraps it up and breaks it down for parts. The process is called mitophagy. *(Evidence: well established. Worked out largely by Richard Youle's group and others.)*

I want to make sure the significance of this lands.

The cell has no way to inspect a mitochondrion from the outside. So it does not try. **It asks the mitochondrion for a voltage, and treats the ability to hold one as proof of life.**

Every chapter since Chapter 2 now reads differently. Membrane potential is not only stored energy. It is a credential. It is the thing a mitochondrion presents in order to be allowed to continue existing.

And if you want one sentence for why Chapter 3 insisted that regulated voltage matters more than maximum voltage, it is this: the body already agrees. It built its entire quality control system around the ability to hold a voltage, not around the size of it.

### When the whole cell has to go

There is a version of this at a larger scale.

If damage is extensive enough that the cell itself is beyond saving, mitochondria do something remarkable. They release cytochrome c, the same small electron carrier we met in Chapter 10, out into the body of the cell.

Outside the mitochondrion, cytochrome c is not an electron carrier. It is a death signal. It assembles a protein complex that activates a cascade of enzymes which dismantle the cell in an orderly, controlled way.

This is apoptosis, and it is not an accident or a failure. It is a decision. The cell takes itself apart neatly, packaging its contents so that neighboring cells can clear them without triggering inflammation. Compare that to necrosis, where a cell bursts and spills its contents, which does trigger inflammation. *(Evidence: well established.)*

So the same molecule that carries electrons inside the mitochondrion is the signal that ends the cell when it is released. Position is everything. And once again, the structure holding that molecule in place is a membrane.

### The sensing framework

Now I can state Wallace's argument properly.

Mitochondria sit at the junction of nearly every environmental input that matters. Consider what they are simultaneously exposed to and responsive to.

**Food.** Not just how much, but what kind. Fat, carbohydrate and protein enter the system at different points and produce different ratios of NADH to FADH2, which changes how the chain runs.

**Oxygen.** Availability directly determines whether the chain can move electrons at all. Altitude changes this.

**Temperature.** Cold activates uncoupling and heat production. Heat changes membrane properties and protein folding.

**Demand.** How much ATP the cell is being asked for, moment to moment.

**Time.** Through the NAD+ and clock loop of Chapter 6, mitochondrial capacity itself is scheduled across the day.

**Damage signals.** Reactive oxygen from Chapter 11 feeds back into gene expression.

And they do not merely receive all this. They talk back. Mitochondria signal to the nucleus, through reactive oxygen, through metabolites, through calcium, changing which genes get expressed. The technical term is retrograde signaling.

So the loop is: environment changes, mitochondria detect and respond, mitochondria signal the nucleus, gene expression changes, the cell behaves differently.

That is the thread I promised in Chapter 1. **Environment, to biological information, to cellular charge, to signaling, to function.** The mitochondrion is where the first two steps meet. *(Evidence: the individual mechanisms are well established. Wallace's synthesis of them into a unifying framework for health and disease is strong but still developing, and it is a framework rather than a proven law.)*

### The DNA you got only from your mother

One more feature, because it is unusual and it matters.

Mitochondria carry their own DNA, a small circular genome with thirty-seven genes, a leftover from their bacterial ancestry. Everything else they need is encoded in the nucleus and imported.

Three things about this genome are worth knowing.

**It is inherited only from your mother.** Sperm mitochondria are eliminated after fertilization. Your mitochondrial DNA traces an unbroken maternal line.

**There are many copies per cell**, often hundreds or thousands. Which means a cell can carry a mixture of normal and mutated copies, a state called heteroplasmy. Below a certain proportion of damaged copies, the cell functions normally. Above it, function fails. There is a threshold.

**It sits close to the action.** Mitochondrial DNA is located right next to the electron transport chain, which is the cell's main source of reactive oxygen, and it lacks the elaborate protein packaging that protects nuclear DNA.

*(Evidence: well established.)*

Wallace and others have also argued that mitochondrial lineages differ in how efficiently they couple energy production to ATP versus heat, and that this may reflect ancestral adaptation to different climates. It is an interesting idea with some supporting data and considerable dispute. *(Evidence: emerging and contested. I would not build a health recommendation on it today.)*

### What this means for a person

Let me bring this down to ground level, because the practical implication is significant and it is not obvious.

If mitochondria were fixed equipment, your options would be limited to protecting what you have.

They are not fixed. They are a population under continuous turnover, and the rate and direction of that turnover responds to signals you generate.

The signal to build more is **energy demand**.

The signal to clear out the failing ones is a working quality control system, which depends on the cell being able to run the voltage test and act on it.

And both of those processes have a schedule, because they are tied to the clock through the NAD+ loop.

So the quality of your mitochondrial population is not a fixed inheritance. It is an ongoing result of demand, recovery, and timing.

That is genuinely hopeful, and it is not a motivational statement. It is a description of a mechanism.

# Part IV: Materials of the Battery

## Chapter 13. DHA: The Membrane Molecule

Every chapter of this book so far has depended on membranes.

The plasma membrane that holds your cells' voltage. The inner mitochondrial membrane that holds the proton gradient. The membrane whose voltage PINK1 uses as a credential. The membrane whose channels open to let a nerve fire.

I have been treating them all as though they were one generic material. A sheet of fat that keeps charge apart.

They are not generic. Membranes are built from a mixture of fats, and what goes into that mixture changes how the membrane behaves. Which means it changes how fast a protein sitting in it can move, how easily the membrane bends, and how the whole thing responds to being pushed.

And one particular fat turns up, over and over, in exactly the tissues where speed matters most.

### Shape, not calories

DHA stands for docosahexaenoic acid. Twenty-two carbon atoms in a chain, with six double bonds along it.

That sounds like chemical trivia. It is actually the whole point, because every double bond is a kink.

&#91;image: Figure 13.1. Straight saturated fatty acids pack tightly against each other, producing a stiffer membrane. DHA, with six double bonds, cannot pack. It sweeps and flexes, producing a far more fluid membrane.\]

A saturated fat has no double bonds. It is a straight chain, and straight chains stack against each other neatly, like pencils in a box. The result is a tightly packed, relatively stiff membrane.

DHA cannot do that. With six kinks in it, the molecule is constantly changing shape, sweeping through space, unable to settle against its neighbors. The result is a membrane that is loose, mobile, and easily deformed.

Now remember what lives in membranes. Proteins. Ion channels. Pumps. Receptors. Every one of them works by **changing shape**, and every one of them is embedded in this material.

A protein sitting in a stiff, tightly packed membrane is working against resistance. The same protein in a fluid, DHA-rich membrane changes shape faster and more easily. *(Evidence: well established.)*

This is not a nutrition claim. It is physics.

### Where the body puts it

And this is where it gets interesting, because DHA is not distributed evenly. The body concentrates it, dramatically, in specific places.

**The retina.** In the light-sensing membranes of your photoreceptor cells, DHA makes up a very large share of the fatty acids present. In rod outer segments it is the dominant fatty acid.

Why there? Because of what happens in those membranes. A protein called rhodopsin absorbs a photon and must change shape essentially instantly to start the signal. In a DHA-rich membrane it can. Studies in animals deprived of DHA show measurably impaired retinal function. *(Evidence: well established.)*

**The brain.** DHA is a major structural fatty acid of gray matter, and it is concentrated particularly at synapses, the junctions where one neuron passes a signal to the next. Brain DHA is accumulated heavily in late pregnancy and early infancy. *(Evidence: well established.)*

**The sperm tail.** Another tissue where a membrane has to flex rapidly and repeatedly.

Notice the pattern. Retina, synapse, sperm tail. Every one of them a place where a membrane protein has to change shape very fast, very often.

DHA is not a general-purpose nutrient the body sprinkles around. It is a specialized material, placed where speed is the requirement.

### The mitochondrial membrane, honestly

Here I need to be more careful than most books in this genre are, because there is a claim in circulation that goes further than the evidence.

The inner mitochondrial membrane has its own signature fat, and it is not DHA. It is cardiolipin, an unusual phospholipid with four tails instead of the usual two.

Cardiolipin is remarkable in its own right. It is found almost exclusively in the inner mitochondrial membrane. It is required for the electron transport complexes of Chapter 10 to function properly. It shapes the deep folds of the cristae. And it binds cytochrome c, holding it in place until the moment of release that begins apoptosis. *(Evidence: well established.)*

What cardiolipin is made of varies by tissue. In heart muscle, the dominant fatty acid in cardiolipin is linoleic acid, which is an omega-6, not DHA. In brain and retinal tissue, cardiolipin contains more DHA.

So the honest statement is this. **Membrane fatty acid composition matters enormously to mitochondrial function, and DHA is part of that picture in some tissues.** The stronger claim, that DHA is the key fat of the mitochondrial membrane generally, is not supported. I have heard it made and I am not going to repeat it.

### The number you can actually measure

Most nutrition advice cannot be checked. This one can.

&#91;image: Figure 13.2. The Omega-3 Index: EPA plus DHA as a percentage of the fatty acids in red blood cell membranes. It measures what your membranes are actually built from.\]

The Omega-3 Index, developed by William Harris and Clemens von Schacky, measures EPA and DHA as a percentage of the fatty acids in your red blood cell membranes. Because red cells turn over slowly, it reflects your intake over months rather than what you ate yesterday.

The categories they proposed: below four percent is the highest risk group, four to eight percent is intermediate, above eight percent is desirable.

Most people eating a typical modern Western diet land somewhere around four to five percent. Populations eating substantial amounts of oily fish routinely test above eight.

I want to be precise about what those categories are. They are derived from **observational** data associating the index with cardiovascular outcomes. They describe an association. They are not proof that moving your number moves your risk. *(Evidence: the measurement is well established and reliable. The risk categories are observational.)*

That said, this is why the Omega-3 Index is a required test in the Human Battery Project rather than an optional one. It is one of the few numbers in this entire field that tells you something structural about your own cells, responds to what you do, and can be re-measured to see whether you changed it.

### Where the supplement evidence actually sits

Given everything above, the obvious conclusion is to take fish oil. The trial evidence is more complicated than the mechanism suggests, and I am going to walk through it the same way I walked through vitamin D.

**Correcting a deficiency is different from supplementing an adequate intake.** If your index is at three percent, you are structurally short of a material your retina and brain are built from. That is a different situation from someone at seven percent taking more.

**The large trials have been mixed.** Several major randomized trials of omega-3 supplementation have failed to show benefit on their primary cardiovascular endpoints, including the omega-3 arm of VITAL. One large trial using a high dose of purified EPA did show benefit, but its results have been debated because of questions about the placebo used. Another high-dose trial was stopped for futility. *(Evidence: well established that the trials disagree. Genuinely unresolved.)*

**There is a signal of harm worth knowing.** Several of these trials found an increased rate of atrial fibrillation in the omega-3 groups. That finding has been consistent enough across trials to be taken seriously. It is a reason to be thoughtful about very high doses rather than assuming more is better. *(Evidence: strong and consistent across multiple trials.)*

Which is the hormesis curve from Chapter 11, appearing again in a place people do not expect it.

My own position: **measure the index, and use food first.** Oily fish, shellfish, and eggs and meat from animals raised on what they are supposed to eat. If the number is low and food is not moving it, supplementation is reasonable, and there is no evidence supporting heroic doses.

### The bigger ideas, clearly labeled

There are two larger claims built on DHA that I want to present properly, because they are interesting and because they are not established.

**The membrane pacemaker hypothesis.** Proposed by Anthony Hulbert and Paul Else, this observes that across species, animals with more polyunsaturated membranes tend to have faster metabolic rates, and animals with more saturated, harder-to-oxidize membranes tend to live longer. The idea is that membrane composition sets metabolic pace.

It is a genuinely elegant idea with real comparative data behind it. It is also contested, the correlations are not uniform, and the direction of causation is unclear. *(Evidence: emerging. Worth following.)*

**Michael Crawford's brain evolution thesis.** Crawford has argued for decades that DHA was the limiting factor in the evolution of the large human brain, and that early humans could only have obtained enough of it from coastal and freshwater food webs. On this account, the shoreline, not the savannah, made us what we are.

I find it compelling and I have read Crawford carefully. It also remains a hypothesis. The archaeological and isotopic evidence is argued about, and alternative accounts of brain expansion exist. *(Evidence: hypothesis. Presented here as one, and Crawford himself has always argued it as a case to be tested.)*

And one more, which I will name and then set down.

There are claims in circulation that DHA is a quantum molecule, that its electronic structure permits electron tunneling or converts photons into electrical current in some special way. I have read that material closely. I want to separate what is true in it from what is being smuggled in alongside.

Here is the part that is not an argument. Everything is a quantum system at the molecular level. Every bond in your body is quantum mechanical, including the ones in table salt. Calling DHA a quantum molecule does not name a mechanism. It applies a word that is true of everything and therefore distinguishes nothing.

Here is the part that is real, and it is better than the version it gets dressed up as. Those six double bonds give DHA a conformational flexibility that almost nothing else in your membranes has. It changes packing, fluidity, thickness, how proteins sit and move inside the bilayer, and how lipid domains behave. That is not mysticism. That is measured membrane biophysics, and this chapter has spent several pages on it.

So here is how I frame DHA, and this framing is mine.

**DHA is a membrane physics molecule. Its job is to set the physical environment that receptors, ion channels and electron transfer proteins have to work inside.**

And that licenses a question I find genuinely interesting, which I state as a hypothesis and not as a finding.

**If DHA sets the physical environment around membrane proteins, and if the proteins this book cares most about are the ones that detect photons, move electrons and hold voltage, then the tissues that hoard DHA hardest should be the tissues where that physical environment matters most.**

Look at where it concentrates. Your retinal photoreceptors, which are in the business of catching light. Your brain, which is in the business of electrical signaling. Those are not random tissues. They are the two most photon-dependent and voltage-dependent organs you own.

I am not going to overclaim that. A pattern is not a mechanism, and there are other reasons a flexible lipid would be useful in a membrane that has to bend, turn over fast and pack a great deal of protein. I will also note that this is exactly where I stopped earlier in the chapter, when I declined the popular claim that cardiolipin is DHA-rich in every tissue, because it is not. But the pattern is the reason I think of the membrane as part of the machinery rather than the bag the machinery sits in.

*(Evidence: DHA's effects on membrane physics are well established. Its enrichment in retina and brain is well established. My reading of why those two facts belong together is a hypothesis of mine. What would settle it: measuring how membrane DHA content changes the function of specific voltage-dependent and photon-dependent proteins in living tissue, with the prediction stated in advance.)*

On the stronger claim, the one I am not making: I know of no experiment showing DHA converting photons into electrical current in a human membrane. If that evidence arrives I will be the first to be delighted by it. It has not arrived, and I am not going to build a chapter on a floor that is not there yet.

I am interested in this territory. I am not going to tell you it is established, because it is not.

### What this chapter actually asks of you

Strip out the speculation and a solid, practical core remains.

Your membranes are built from what you eat. That is not a metaphor. The fatty acids in your diet physically become the material of the membranes that hold your cells' voltage.

A specific fat is concentrated in your fastest-signaling tissues, and most people are short of it.

And unlike almost everything else in nutrition, **you can measure your own number, change it, and measure it again.**

That is why it is in the protocol.

## Chapter 14. Water

This is the chapter I have rewritten more times than any other.

Water is where the field I have spent thirteen years reading in is at its most interesting and at its least disciplined. There is genuinely fascinating established science here that almost nobody knows about. There are serious, unconventional researchers whose work deserves a hearing. And there is a market full of devices sold on claims that have no evidence behind them whatsoever.

All three are frequently discussed in the same sentence, by the same people, in the same tone.

So I am going to do something different in this chapter. I am going to sort them, in front of you, and tell you exactly which rung each claim sits on.

If you only take one thing from this book about how to evaluate a health claim, let it be the habit this chapter is demonstrating.

### Start with what is not in dispute

Before anything contested, I want you to see how remarkable the established picture already is.

&#91;image: Figure 14.1. Four things water is already known to do in the body, none of which are controversial, and none of which resemble the advice most people have received about drinking more.\]

**Water dresses every ion.** A sodium ion in your body is never a bare sodium ion. It is surrounded by a shell of water molecules, oriented by its charge, moving with it. That shell determines how big it effectively is, how fast it moves, and whether it fits through a given channel.

Which means the ion gradients from Chapter 2, the ones your entire membrane voltage rests on, are really gradients of hydrated ions. Water is not the background to that system. It is part of the machinery. *(Evidence: well established.)*

**Water carries protons in a way nothing else does.** This one is my favorite and it connects directly to Chapter 10.

A proton moving through water does not swim across like a boat. Instead, it attaches to one water molecule, which passes a different proton to the next molecule, which passes one to the next, down a chain of hydrogen bonds. The charge travels far faster than any individual molecule could.

It is called the Grotthuss mechanism, and it means the proton gradient your mitochondria spend most of their energy building is moved through a medium with special properties for exactly that job. *(Evidence: well established.)*

**Cell volume is a signal.** A cell that takes on a little water and swells slightly interprets that as an instruction to build. A cell that loses water and shrinks interprets it as an instruction to break things down. Dieter Häussinger and colleagues established this in liver tissue: hydration state is read as a metabolic instruction. *(Evidence: strong.)*

So hydration is not merely about having enough fluid in the system. It is an input that changes what your cells decide to do.

**You manufacture water.** At the end of the chain in Chapter 10, oxygen accepts electrons and protons and the product is water. Your mitochondria make a few hundred milliliters a day. Some desert animals meet nearly all their water needs this way. *(Evidence: well established.)*

And the body has dedicated protein channels for moving water across membranes, discovered by Peter Agre, who received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2003 for it. The cell does not let water diffuse where it likes. It controls it.

None of that is fringe. All of it is more interesting than the advice most people have been given.

### Now the ladder

Here is where I am going to be unusually explicit, because this is the territory where books like mine usually lose the argument.

&#91;image: Figure 14.2. The water claims sorted by how much evidence stands behind each one, from what is resolved inside the machinery down to what is sold in a bottle. They are not the same kind of statement and this book will not present them as though they were.\]

Let me walk down it.

### Rung one: the four things nobody disputes

That is the section you just read. Water dresses every ion, carries protons in a way nothing else does, reports cell volume as a signal, and is manufactured by your own mitochondria. None of it is contested, and none of it is what the bottled-water industry is selling. It is the floor the rest of the ladder stands on.

### Rung two: water near a surface is genuinely different

This rung is where the serious version of the argument lives, and it is more solid than most skeptics realize.

Water molecules that sit within one to three molecular layers of a surface do not behave like bulk water. Their movement is slower, their hydrogen bonding is reorganized, and their properties are measurably different. This has been demonstrated with neutron scattering, nuclear magnetic resonance and terahertz spectroscopy. It is not controversial. *(Evidence: strong and well measured.)*

Now add a fact about cells that people consistently underestimate.

The inside of a cell is not a dilute solution. It is densely packed with proteins, membranes, filaments and structures. The distances between surfaces are small. Which means that a substantial fraction of the water inside a cell is sitting close enough to something to be in that altered state.

So the statement "a meaningful proportion of the water in your cells is not behaving like water in a glass" is a reasonable one. *(Evidence: strong.)*

That is the legitimate kernel. Hold onto it, because the next rung is the one almost nobody in this argument talks about, and it is the firmest ground in the chapter. It is not in a beaker. It is inside your mitochondria.

### Rung three: inside the machines, ordered water is part of the mechanism

Almost all of the public argument about structured water happens in glassware. Gels, dyes, tubes, exclusion distances, arguments about how far ordering can possibly propagate. That is where the fight is, and we get to it on the next rung.

While that fight has been going on, structural biology quietly settled a different version of the question, and it is the version that actually matters for this book.

In 2020, Daniel Grba and Judy Hirst published a 2.7 angstrom structure of complex I, a 42-subunit machine, from the yeast Yarrowia lipolytica, in Nature Structural and Molecular Biology. At that resolution you stop seeing only protein. You start seeing the water. They resolved 275 structured water molecules inside the enzyme. And those waters were not scenery. In their own words, the water molecules "connect mechanistically crucial elements and constitute proton-translocation pathways through the membrane."

Sit with that sentence for a second, because it is the one this chapter has been walking toward.

The proton pathway through the first complex of your electron transport chain is partly built out of water. Not water as the solvent the machine happens to be floating in. Water as a working part of the machine.

And 275 is a floor, not a ceiling. Cryo-electron microscopy can only resolve the water molecules that are held still enough to show up as density. Every water in that enzyme that is merely somewhat ordered is invisible in that number. *(Evidence: well established. This is a published structure, not an interpretation of one.)*

**Now the strange part about complex I.** Look at what this machine has to accomplish. The chemistry happens at one end, where NADH hands over its electrons and ubiquinone gets reduced. The proton pumping happens somewhere else entirely, out in the membrane subunits. Ville Kaila, who has spent years on this problem, puts the distance plainly: the reaction at the active site "triggers proton pumping across the membrane domain of complex I, in subunits up to 200 Å away from the active site."

Two hundred angstroms is an enormous distance in protein terms. Nothing physically travels it. No electron makes that trip, and no proton is carried that far by a hand. The coupling is electrostatic, and it is relayed.

Relayed through what?

In 2020, Michael Röpke, Patricia Saura, Daniel Riepl, Maximilian Pöverlein and Kaila published simulations of mammalian complex I in the Journal of the American Chemical Society under a title that does not leave much room for interpretation: "Functional Water Wires Catalyze Long-Range Proton Pumping in the Mammalian Respiratory Complex I." The membrane arm hydrates and dehydrates as the enzyme cycles. When the water wires form, protons can move along them. When the wires break, the protons cannot. The water is not the medium the switching happens in. The water is the switch. *(Evidence: strong. Simulation built on high resolution structures, consistent across several independent groups, but still simulation.)*

**And the same thing is true at the far end of the chain.** Florian Blanc and Gerhard Hummer simulated the rotor of a mitochondrial ATP synthase and published it in PNAS in 2024. Water fills the access and exit half channels all the way to the interface where the rotating ring meets the stator. Proton transfer there, they write, happens "via water wires in a Grotthuss-type mechanism," and it "is contingent upon every involved water molecule adopting a favorable orientation."

Every involved water molecule adopting a favorable orientation. That is a sentence about molecular ordering, in PNAS, about the machine that makes your ATP.

### Drawing the line precisely

This is the exact place where good science gets turned into bad marketing, so I am going to draw the line with more care than I have drawn any other line in this book.

Here are two sentences. They sound similar. They are not.

**Sentence one: structured water receives the energy released by electrons and uses it to run biochemical reactions.** That is not an established mechanism. It is not what any of the papers above say. It is not what I am claiming. If you have heard some version of it from a wellness stage, including from people I otherwise respect, it is running ahead of the evidence.

**Sentence two: the energy of electron transfer is converted into an electrochemical potential, and ordered, interfacial water networks participate directly in the proton transfer and energy transduction that does the converting.** That is strongly connected to published mitochondrial biophysics. That is the structure and the simulations above.

The difference between those two sentences is the difference between a book you can defend and a book you cannot.

So here is the chain as I understand it, stated the long way, with nothing skipped:

&#91;image: Figure 14.3. Where water actually sits in the chain from food to biochemical work. It is a working part of the proton machinery, not a reservoir of stored energy, and the difference between those two statements is the difference between a defensible book and an indefensible one.\]

Food becomes reducing equivalents, NADH and FADH2. Those hand electrons into the transport chain. Electron transfer releases redox energy. That energy drives proton pumping. Proton pumping builds a membrane electric field and a proton gradient, which together are the battery. Organized, interfacial water networks are part of the physical machinery that moves those protons. The gradient drives ATP synthase. ATP does biochemical work, including running the pumps that hold the plasma membrane voltage you read about in Chapter 2.

Water is in that chain. It is in it as plumbing and as a moving part, not as a reservoir of stored light waiting to be spent.

I find the real version more impressive than the marketed one. The marketed version asks you to believe in a mysterious energy store. The real version says that the most important energy converting machine in your body has water molecules built into its proton pathway, and that whether those molecules are lined up correctly determines whether the machine works at all.

### How long does water remember?

Everything I have said so far about water is spatial. Where the molecules sit, whether they are ordered, whether they form a pathway. Underneath it sits a second question that is more interesting and, as it turns out, more answerable. Not where. For how long.

Here is the honest way to ask it.

Take any molecular property of water you like. The orientation of a molecule, the direction of its dipole, which partner it is hydrogen bonded to. Call that property A. Then ask how well the value of A now predicts the value of A at some later time. Physicists write that as a correlation function:

C(t) = \[⟨A(0)A(t)⟩ minus ⟨A⟩²\] divided by \[⟨A²⟩ minus ⟨A⟩²\]

At time zero, C equals one, because the system is perfectly correlated with itself. As it forgets, C falls toward zero. How long that takes is the correlation time, and that number is what anyone actually means, or ought to mean, by memory.

That definition does three things at once. It makes the question measurable. It strips out the mysticism. And it lets me say exactly what I am not claiming.

**What bulk water does.** In ordinary liquid water, hydrogen bonds break and reform on the order of a picosecond, and molecular reorientation takes a few picoseconds. A picosecond is a millionth of a millionth of a second. Nothing biological happens on that timescale. So the popular claim, that a glass of water carries information about something dissolved in it hours or days ago, fails on the most basic physical grounds. The correlation is gone before you have set the glass down. *(Evidence: well established.)*

That is why I declined water memory earlier, and it is why the refusal is a matter of physics rather than taste.

**But the decay is not a simple exponential, and that is where this gets interesting.**

If forgetting were simple, C(t) would fall off as exp(minus t over tau) and there would be nothing more to say. It does not. In 1996, Alenka Luzar and David Chandler published work in *Nature* showing that hydrogen bond kinetics in water carry a long-time tail that follows a power law rather than an exponential. The paper has been cited more than two thousand times.

The reason is almost embarrassingly simple once you see it. When a hydrogen bond breaks, the two partners do not vanish. They diffuse. And two molecules that have drifted apart can drift back together and re-bond. So the chance that a pair is still associated at time t is not governed only by how long one bond lasts. It is governed by how likely the partners are to find each other again.

**And here is the part that matters for this book.**

In 2025, a group publishing in *Nature Communications* generalized that result and measured it directly, in simulation and by tracking single particles under a microscope. They call it binding memory. The long-time tail follows a power law, and the exponent is set by the dimensionality of the space the molecules are diffusing in. In their words, the scaling exponents "unequivocally align with minus D over 2."

C(t) proportional to t raised to the power of minus D over 2

In three dimensions that is t to the minus three halves. In two dimensions, t to the minus one. In one dimension, t to the minus one half.

Read those three numbers again slowly, because they are nowhere near each other. Lower the dimensionality and the correlation decays dramatically more slowly. The same water, constrained to fewer directions, remembers longer. And the reason is not exotic. In a confined space, a molecule that wanders off has fewer ways to get lost and a far better chance of coming back.

&#91;image: Figure 14.4. The same water, constrained to fewer directions, remembers longer. Correlation decays as t to the power of minus D over 2, where D is the dimensionality of the space the molecules diffuse in. A plain exponential is gone almost at once. Bulk water in three dimensions falls away fast. Water confined to a sheet against a membrane, or to a single-file wire inside a protein channel, holds correlation far longer.\]

Now look at where biology puts its water.

A water wire threading a proton channel in complex I is not three-dimensional. It is a line. The hydration layer pressed against a membrane is not three-dimensional. It is a sheet. The 275 waters resolved inside that 2.7 angstrom structure are not free to go wherever they like.

**Biology systematically confines water into lower dimensions. By that same equation, confinement is precisely where correlation should last longest.**

That is my hypothesis, and it is the first one in this book I have been able to write as an equation rather than a hunch. *(Evidence: the power law and its dimensional dependence are established. That biology's confined geometries produce functionally significant correlation times is mine, and untested.)*

### The number that keeps this honest

Before that runs away with anyone, including me.

Fabio Sterpone, Guillaume Stirnemann and Damien Laage measured how much water actually slows down next to a protein. For eighty percent of the hydration layer, the slowdown factor is about two to three.

Not a thousand. Not a hundred. Two to three. The strongly retarded water is a small minority sitting in specific confined pockets, not a halo of transformed liquid surrounding everything.

So the honest version is not that biological water is a different substance. It is that most of it is only slightly slower than ordinary water, and that a small, structurally specific fraction, inside channels and against particular surfaces, is where anything interesting could be happening.

That narrows the claim a great deal, and it makes it better. The interesting water is not the water in you. It is the water inside the machines.

### The Biological Water Correlation Hypothesis

Stated so that someone can break it:

**Biological water is an active component of bioenergetics rather than the solvent that bioenergetics happens in. Confinement, interfaces, membranes, ions and electric fields alter its molecular dynamics. Those changes are described by correlation functions with finite, measurable relaxation times, and never by persistent memory. The hypothesis is that in the specific low-dimensional geometries biology builds, water wires, channel interiors, and the sheet against a membrane, correlation persists long enough to influence proton transfer, and therefore energy conversion.**

Notice what that sentence does not say. It does not say water stores information. It does not say water remembers substances. It does not say anything survives longer than the physics allows. It says the geometry sets the timescale, and asks whether the timescale is long enough to matter.

**The missing experiment, and it is a specific number.** Take the binding autocorrelation function and measure it for the waters inside a respiratory complex's proton channel. If a water wire in complex I behaves as a one-dimensional system, the tail should decay as t to the minus one half. If it behaves like bulk water, it will decay as t to the minus three halves, and my hypothesis has nothing left in it.

That is a simulation anyone with the structure and the compute could run this year. It predicts a specific exponent. It can fail cleanly. I would like to see it either way.

**And the principle, which I will state carefully because the first half is defensible now and the second half is not yet.** Water is not merely the medium surrounding the battery. At specific molecular interfaces, in specific structures we can name, water is part of the circuit. Whether that is true generally, across biology rather than inside particular enzymes, is exactly what the experiment above would start to answer.

That is the firmest ground in this chapter. Now the ladder starts to shake.

### Rung four: Pollack's exclusion zone

Gerald Pollack, a bioengineer at the University of Washington, has made an observation that is real and reproducible.

Put a hydrophilic material, most famously a polymer called Nafion, into water containing suspended microspheres, and a zone forms next to the surface from which the microspheres are excluded. It can be seen under a microscope. It can extend a long way, hundreds of microns, which is enormously larger than a few molecular layers. Pollack reports that this zone is electrically negative relative to the water beyond it, and that it grows when illuminated with infrared light.

Pollack's interpretation is that this is a distinct phase of water, ordered into sheets, with a separated charge, effectively a battery built by light. He has extended the idea to biology, proposing that cells use this to do work, and that this is a fundamental and overlooked feature of living systems.

Here is my honest assessment.

**What supports it.** The exclusion phenomenon is observable and has been reproduced. Something is happening at that interface. The measurements of charge separation and infrared response have been published.

**What challenges it.** Most physical chemists do not accept the interpretation. There are conventional explanations for exclusion zones involving known electrokinetic and colloidal effects, diffusiophoresis, and chemical gradients from the material itself, that do not require a new phase of water. The distances involved are difficult to reconcile with what is known about how far molecular ordering can propagate. And independent replication demonstrating the specific interpretation, rather than the observation, has been limited.

**What remains unknown.** Whether anything resembling a large exclusion zone exists inside living cells, as opposed to next to an artificial polymer in a dish.

**What would settle it.** Direct measurement inside living tissue, with a pre-specified prediction that differs from what conventional physical chemistry would forecast, replicated by a laboratory with no stake in the outcome.

*(Evidence: hypothesis, contested. The observation is real. The interpretation is disputed and should not be presented as established.)*

I have read Pollack's work carefully and I think it deserves to be tested rather than dismissed. I also am not going to build a health protocol on it, and if you have seen me quoted elsewhere placing more weight on it than this, treat this chapter as the correction.

### Rung five: Ling's challenge

Gilbert Ling spent his career arguing that the standard model of the cell is wrong.

His association-induction hypothesis held that cell water is largely ordered on protein surfaces, that potassium is held inside cells by adsorption onto those proteins rather than by pumping, and that the membrane pump model could not account for the energy budget.

I want to give Ling his due, because his central question was a good one. He asked how a cell could possibly afford to pump that much, and he was asking it before the answer was fully known.

And then the answer arrived. Jens Skou found the sodium-potassium pump in 1957. Its structure has been solved. Its mechanism is understood. It can be isolated, measured, and inhibited, and when you inhibit it the gradient collapses, exactly as the pump model predicts.

So the strong form of Ling's hypothesis is refuted. Not unfashionable. Refuted.

What survives is the weaker and more interesting question he raised: how much of cell water is genuinely bulk-like, and how much of cellular organization depends on water structure rather than on membrane machinery? That question is still open, and it is the same question as rung two.

*(Evidence: minority view, with the strong form refuted and a weaker version still live.)*

### Rung six: the products

There is a market of devices and products promising to structure, energize, restructure or otherwise improve your drinking water.

I am aware of no controlled human evidence that any of them produce a health benefit.

And there is a physical problem that any such claim has to answer. Whatever structure exists in liquid water at room temperature reorganizes on a timescale of picoseconds. Water does not hold a shape the way a solid does. So a device that claims to impart lasting structure to water you then drink is claiming something that needs an explanation nobody has provided.

*(Evidence: none that I am aware of. I would change this paragraph if a well-designed trial showed otherwise.)*

I take no commissions on any product in this book, and this is the sort of paragraph that makes that worth saying.

### So what does this leave you with

A reasonable person reading this chapter might feel let down. I spent it taking things away.

Here is what I think is actually left, and I think it is more than it sounds.

**Hydration status is a real metabolic input**, not just a comfort issue, because cell volume is read as a signal.

**Minerals matter**, because it is the dissolved ions that carry the charge in every process in this book. Water without electrolytes does not do the job that water with them does. This is why the program specifies mineral-rich water and not simply more water.

**Your mitochondria are a water source**, which is one more reason metabolic health and hydration are less separate than they look.

**And the interface between water and the surfaces inside your cells is real, measurable science** that most people have never heard of, and is where I would look if I were a researcher choosing a problem.

That is a chapter's worth. It is just not the chapter the market would prefer me to write.

### The question I am leaving open

There is one claim in this chapter I have neither made nor refuted, and it is worth being exact about which one, because the difference between those two is the whole subject of this book.

The claim is that structured water receives the energy released by electrons, holds it, and later spends it on biochemical work. Water as a battery rather than as wiring.

I do not make that claim. There is no evidence for it, and I am not going to state something as true because it would tidy up my model.

But unevidenced and impossible are different words, and I am using the first one on purpose.

**What makes it hard.** The energy would have to be held somewhere. The correlation work earlier in this chapter says bulk water forgets a molecular configuration in picoseconds, and that a protein's hydration shell slows that by a factor of two or three. Storage on a timescale useful to biochemistry needs something orders of magnitude longer than either figure, and nothing anyone has measured provides it.

**What would open it.** A measurement showing that water at a confined biological site holds correlation far longer than the hydration-shell figure, together with a demonstrated route by which that correlation does work rather than merely persisting. The first half is the experiment I already named, the binding autocorrelation exponent inside a proton channel. So this is not an idle possibility. It is a testable one waiting on an instrument.

Until both halves exist, the honest position is the one I am taking. Not claimed, and not refuted either. If I wrote it off, I would be doing the thing I criticize other people for, which is treating an absence of evidence as a finding.

### What comes next

Membranes and water are the materials. The next chapter is about where the materials come from.

It is also the chapter where I tell you about an elk, a thirteen-year-old with a knife, and the first real biology lesson I ever had.

## Chapter 15. Food Is Stored Sunlight

I was about thirteen the first time I understood where food really comes from.

My father, my two brothers and I had gone on an elk hunt, and instead of handing the animal off to a processor, we brought the whole elk back to the ranch. All of it. It was the first time our family had ever butchered an animal ourselves, and my father turned it into a classroom.

He walked my brothers and me through it step by step. But he did not stop at the cuts. As we worked, he gave us an anatomy lesson on every part of the animal. This muscle. What it did while the elk was alive. What it becomes on a plate. The top sirloin. The tenderloin. Every piece had a job long before it ever had a name at a butcher counter.

I did not realize it then, but that was my first real biology lesson.

Then my father showed us a way of cooking he had learned from the Argentinian tradition. We dug into the ground, laid down hot coals, buried the meat and set vegetables on top. The earth did the cooking.

Here is what I understand now that I could not have put into words at thirteen.

That elk was stored sunlight.

### The chain, literally

&#91;image: Figure 15.1. The path from a photon to your mitochondria. At every step it is the same energy, handed along, carried by electrons.\]

Start at the sun. A photon leaves it and arrives here eight minutes later.

It strikes a molecule of chlorophyll in a leaf and knocks an electron into a higher energy state. The plant catches that excited electron and uses its energy to build sugar out of carbon dioxide and water.

That is what a sugar molecule is. It is carbon, hydrogen and oxygen arranged so that its electrons sit in a high-energy configuration. Solar energy, locked into chemical bonds.

The elk ate the grass and rebuilt that stored energy into its own muscle and fat. We ate the elk.

And in our mitochondria, the electron transport chain from Chapter 10 stripped those electrons back off and let them fall, step by step, down to oxygen, capturing the released energy along the way.

Same electrons. Same energy. Handed from the sun to the grass to the elk to us. *(Evidence: well established.)*

So when I say food is stored sunlight, I am not being poetic. I am describing where the electrons came from.

And notice what that means for Part II of this book. Sunlight reaches you by three different routes. It reaches your eyes as information, setting your clock. It reaches your skin directly, where it runs a small amount of chemistry on the spot. And it reaches your mitochondria as electrons, on a plate, having taken a long detour through a plant and possibly an animal. Only the third route is a serious energy supply, and that is the one everybody calls eating.

The first is a message. The third is the fuel. They arrive by different paths and they do different jobs, and confusing the two is where a great deal of bad writing on this subject comes from.

### Then it became my business

Years later, that lesson from the ranch turned into a business.

In 2009 I became a partner in an upscale restaurant in Seattle. A few years after that I opened my own in Bellevue, and over the years that followed I opened several more.

Every one of them was built on the way I grew up eating. Farm to table. We designed menus around what was growing that season, sourced from farms within a couple of hundred miles. When the season changed, the menu changed with it. We did not decide what we wanted and then find a way to get it. We asked what the land was giving us right now, and built from there.

The dish I was proudest of, at 99 Park, was halibut with squash and berries.

There is nothing clever about that plate. It is three things that arrive in the Pacific Northwest at roughly the same time, cooked simply and put next to each other. That is the entire idea. It tasted the way it did because it was a snapshot of one particular stretch of the year in one particular place.

You could build that dish in February. You would be flying in three lies to do it, and it would not taste like anything.

My customers came for the flavor. What I was actually selling them was a relationship with the season.

Some of the reasons that matters are well established. Produce picked ripe and eaten soon after harvest generally retains more of its delicate nutrients. Vitamin C in particular degrades steadily during long transport and storage. *(Evidence: well established.)*

Other reasons belong to my working model, and I am about to be very clear about which is which.

### The plate

First, the practical structure, because the program specifies it.

&#91;image: Figure 15.2. The macronutrient split used in the Human Battery Project, and an honest statement of what kind of claim it is.\]

Roughly forty percent of calories from protein, forty percent from fat, twenty percent from carbohydrate.

**Protein is structure.** Every pump, channel, enzyme and receptor in this book is a protein. Your muscle is protein. And it is the most satiating of the three macronutrients, which matters for a reason that has nothing to do with willpower.

**Fat is material and slow fuel.** Chapter 13 was entirely about what your membranes are built from. Fat is also the substrate for steroid hormones, and it is a fuel the body can burn at a steady rate over long periods.

**Carbohydrate is fast fuel.** It is what you want for brief, hard effort, and it is what refills muscle glycogen. It is useful in the right amount at the right time, and Chapter 17 is about what happens when it arrives constantly instead.

And vegetables are present at every meal without being the bulk of the plate.

Now the part that a lot of nutrition books skip.

**This split is a design decision, not a proven optimum.** I arrived at it by reasoning from the physiology in this book and from what I have seen work. When you look at the actual trial literature comparing macronutrient ratios head to head, the differences between reasonable approaches are generally modest, and adherence, meaning whether people can actually live this way, predicts outcomes better than the ratio does. *(Evidence: my working model. The trial literature does not establish an optimal ratio.)*

I also want to flag honestly where this sits relative to mainstream guidance. Recommending that vegetables not be the bulk of the plate runs against most dietary guidelines, and the observational evidence associating higher vegetable and fruit intake with better outcomes is large and consistent. My position is that nutrient density and the structural materials come first, and that vegetables belong at every meal. But I am not going to pretend I am in agreement with the guidelines when I am not, or that the evidence is on my side when it is mixed.

### The seasonal argument, labeled

Now the more speculative claim, which is genuinely mine.

Here is the reasoning chain.

**Step one, established.** Food is stored sunlight, and the electrons in your food came from a plant capturing photons.

**Step two, strong but developing.** Mitochondria act as environmental sensors, as Chapter 12 described, reading the conditions around the cell and adjusting.

**Step three, my working model.** If mitochondria read the environment, then food may be part of what they read. Summer food and winter food differ in composition. Summer brings fruit, rich in carbohydrate. Winter historically brought fat and protein and stored roots. Those are not just different calories. They are different electron-carrying substrates, entering metabolism at different points, and producing different ratios of NADH to FADH2 into the chain.

**Step four, hypothesis.** If those differences carry seasonal information, then eating strawberries in January is not simply nutritionally neutral. It is sending a summer signal into a winter body, at a time when the light signal, the temperature signal and the day length signal are all saying something else.

I find that argument compelling. Let me be equally clear about its status.

Steps one and two are solid. Step three is a reasonable inference that has not been directly tested in humans. Step four is a hypothesis, and I am not aware of a single controlled human trial testing whether out-of-season eating produces measurable harm.

### The version I can actually defend

I want to kill one version of this argument before it gets loose, because it is the version that sounds best said out loud and it is the version I am least able to support.

The bad version goes like this. The electrons in a tropical papaya carry summer information. When they arrive in a January body, that information conflicts with the winter signal, and the conflict causes electrons to leak.

I cannot make that claim, and I am not going to.

Electrons are indistinguishable elementary particles. That is not a turn of phrase, it is one of the strictest statements in physics. Swap any two electrons in the universe and no measurement anywhere can detect that anything happened. There is no property of an electron that records where it was captured, which plant captured it, or how long the day was when it happened. If you ever hear me say that a summer electron carries a summer code, I am wrong, and I would rather put that in writing now than be quoted on it later.

For a while I treated that sentence as the end of the argument. It is not. It is the end of one argument, about one object.

Electrons do not arrive in your mitochondria as naked electrons. They arrive inside molecules, held in chemical bonds, in particular oxidation states, with particular reduction potentials, inside particular metabolites that meet particular enzymes. A papaya does not hand you a bucket of electrons. It hands you sucrose and fructose and citrate and ascorbate and carotenoids and several hundred phenolic compounds, and every one of those is a specific arrangement of matter that the growing environment helped build.

So the sentence I actually want in this book is narrower, and more useful, than the one I started with.

**Electrons are indistinguishable. Electron environments are not.**

Food does not need to write a latitude onto an electron in order to carry environmental information. That information, if it is there at all, lives in the molecular and redox architecture that biological electron flow emerges from. Which raises the obvious question, and it is the question this chapter now has to answer honestly: how much of that architecture survives the trip to the inner mitochondrial membrane?

The honest version of the idea does not live in the electron. It lives one level up, in the substrate profile and in the state of the body receiving it. And at that level there is something real to argue about.

Here is the case in four pieces, with the strength of each stated.

**One. Humans really do register the season.** In 1994, Maija-Liisa Laakso and colleagues followed salivary melatonin, cortisol and body temperature across three seasons in nine healthy volunteers living at 60 degrees north. Their finding: "The melatonin and cortisol rhythms delayed significantly (about 1 hr) in midwinter compared with summer and autumn." Body temperature did not shift, which they attributed to socially fixed schedules rather than photoperiod. Nine people is a small study and 1994 is a long time ago, and I am not going to dress it up. But the pathway it implies, from latitude to day length to retina to clock to hormone timing, is exactly the pathway Part II spent four chapters building. *(Evidence: the pathway is well established. The size of the seasonal shift in any individual person is not.)*

**Two. Human metabolism and immunity actually vary across the year.** In 2024, Jeffrey Gassen, Summer Mengelkoch and George Slavich analyzed 2,118 people from the MIDUS cohort and found seasonal variation across a long list of immune and metabolic markers. The pro-inflammatory proteins IL-6, IL-8 and TNF-alpha generally peaked when days were shorter. Hemoglobin A1c climbed from late autumn through spring. Triglycerides ran higher in summer and autumn. HDL fell steadily across the year. Glucose, insulin, LDL, CRP and ICAM-1 showed no seasonal pattern at all, which is worth saying because it is the kind of detail that gets left out when a study is used as ammunition. And the finding I keep coming back to: for 18 of 19 markers, the relationship between stress and the biomarker itself changed with the season, in size and sometimes in direction. *(Evidence: strong. Large sample, observational.)*

That second leg matters more than it looks, and here is why. It means the biochemical environment that receives your dinner is not the same environment in January that it was in July. Same food, different body.

**Three. Food supplies the reducing equivalents, and their electrons enter the chain.** Carbohydrate, fat and protein metabolism generates NADH and FADH2, those carriers hand electrons into the respiratory complexes, and the redox reactions build the proton-motive force that makes ATP. That is Chapter 10, and it is established biochemistry, not a position of mine.

**Four. Electron leak depends on the state of the mitochondrion, not simply on the amount of fuel.** Electrons can leave the intended path and reduce oxygen prematurely, generating superoxide, with complexes I and III as important sites. How much of that happens depends on the redox state of the carriers, on the proton-motive force, and on how coupled the system is. That is Chapter 11, and it is also established. It is not a fixed percentage of electrons falling off a conveyor belt, whatever you may have read.

Now put the four together, and notice that you get a chain that never once requires an electron to remember anything.

### What a volt actually buys

Before I can argue about food, I have to be honest about the thermodynamics, because the thermodynamics is where my own idea gets into the most trouble.

The usable energy in an electron transfer is set by how far the electron falls, and the relationship is exact:

```latex
\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = -nF\Delta E^{\circ\prime}
```

Here n is the number of electrons transferred, F is Faraday's constant at 96,485 joules per mole per volt, and the delta E term is the difference in reduction potential between where the electron starts and where it lands.

Put the real numbers in. The NAD+ and NADH couple sits at about minus 0.32 volts. The oxygen and water couple sits at about plus 0.82 volts. The span is 1.14 volts, and for the two electrons that NADH carries, that comes to roughly 220 kilojoules per mole. That span is the entire energy budget of aerobic life. Every chapter in this book that talks about charge is talking about how a cell spends it.

Now the problem for any claim that food carries energy quality.

**Those potentials are properties of the carriers, not of the meal.** Carbohydrate, fat and protein are all dismantled down to a small common set of intermediates, and what leaves that machinery is NADH and FADH2, the same two molecules whatever you ate. A polyphenol that oxidizes at plus 0.25 volts on a carbon electrode is not donating electrons to complex I. It has no thermodynamic route into the proton-motive force at all. You can measure that polyphenol's voltage precisely, and the number will be real, and it will not be an input to your mitochondria.

One refinement keeps this from being a flat refutation. The standard potential of the NADH couple is fixed. The actual potential follows the Nernst equation, and depends on the real ratio of NADH to NAD+ inside the compartment, which a meal genuinely does move. But it moves it by tens of millivolts, not by the volt-scale differences that an electrochemical reading of a food reports.

*(Evidence: established. The thermodynamic relation and the standard potentials are textbook physical chemistry. The convergence of macronutrients onto common carriers is established biochemistry. Neither is a position of mine.)*

### What season actually changes in a plant

If the season is doing anything, then, it is not doing it to the electron. It is doing it to the plant.

That part is not in doubt. Growing conditions change plant chemistry, reliably and measurably. Photon flux, day length, spectrum, ultraviolet exposure, temperature, water availability and stress all change what a plant synthesizes, and the compounds they move most are exactly the redox-active ones: polyphenols, flavonoids, anthocyanins, carotenoids, ascorbate, glutathione.

What I had wrong was the direction.

In 2013 Gene Lester and colleagues grew spinach through a subarctic summer and through a subtropical winter and measured the leaves. Summer production raised dry matter, oxidized ascorbate, tocopherols and total phenols. It also lowered reduced ascorbate, alpha carotene, folate and overall antioxidant capacity. Same leaf, same experiment, different compounds moving in opposite directions.

It gets worse for the simple story. Simona Proietti and colleagues moved spinach to ten degrees and watched ascorbate climb about 41 percent in seven days. Cold, not summer sun. In field kale, moderate and then strong frost raised dry weight, soluble sugars, ascorbate, phenolics, anthocyanins and antioxidant activity. Cold again.

The effect is not even consistent within a crop. Mark Lefsrud and colleagues grew kale and spinach in the same controlled chamber and found maximum lutein and beta carotene at thirty degrees for kale and at ten degrees for spinach. Two leafy greens, one chamber, carotenoid optima twenty degrees apart. Lee and Oh gave two kale cultivars the same four-degree treatment and got 15 percent more total phenolics in one and 16 percent less in the other.

Then drought. Medina-Lozano and colleagues ran seven lettuce genotypes over two years and found vitamin C fell with water stress in every single one, while anthocyanins rose. One stressor, two antioxidants, opposite signs.

So the conclusion, stated plainly, is the opposite of what the seasonal eating world usually says:

**Season changes the redox and molecular environment of food. Season does not turn a single antioxidant dial up in summer and down in winter.** Anybody who tells you summer produce is simply more powerful is describing a dial that does not exist.

*(Evidence: established that growing environment alters plant redox chemistry, including in chamber studies with genetics, soil and nutrition held constant and only light or temperature manipulated. Established that the direction is compound specific, cultivar specific, and frequently opposite within the same leaf.)*

### The part nobody in this argument wants to talk about

There is a confound sitting in the middle of every claim about out-of-season food, including mine, and it is larger than the effect everybody is arguing over.

Fernando Vallejo and colleagues took fresh broccoli, wrapped it, held it seven days at one degree to simulate commercial transport, then three days at fifteen degrees to simulate sitting in a shop. Ten days, ordinary handling, nothing abusive. Glucosinolates fell by 71 to 80 percent. Flavonoids fell by about 60 percent. Caffeoyl-quinic acid derivatives fell by 73 to 74 percent.

Read those numbers against the seasonal differences above. The supply chain does more damage in ten days than the season does across an entire growing cycle.

Which means that when someone compares in-season local produce against out-of-season imported produce and finds a chemical difference, they have not measured the season. They have measured the season plus the truck plus the warehouse plus the shelf, and the truck is the biggest term in that sum. I think that is a good argument for eating local and fresh. I do not think it is an argument that summer electrons are different, and I am not going to let the strength of the first claim smuggle in the second.

*(Evidence: established, and it is the most important methodological caveat in this chapter.)*

### You can actually measure this, and the measurement is real

There is an entire analytical field doing what this chapter has been circling. It puts food on an electrode and reads its electron-donating behavior directly. Cyclic voltammetry, differential pulse voltammetry and square wave voltammetry sweep a voltage across a sample and record the current that flows when something in it gives up an electron.

Two numbers come out. The potential at which a peak appears tells you how easily that molecule surrenders an electron. The size of the peak tells you how much of it is there. Quality and quantity, separately. Shlomo Chevion and colleagues established that two-parameter readout in 2000, and it is what makes the word fingerprint defensible rather than decorative.

The numbers are specific. A catechol group, two hydroxyls sitting next to each other on a ring, oxidizes at about plus 0.2 to 0.3 volts against a silver and silver chloride electrode. An isolated or meta hydroxyl needs plus 0.65 to 0.85 volts. Catechin, which contains both arrangements, shows two peaks, at 0.22 and 0.72 volts. Quercetin shows four, spread from roughly 0.15 to 0.80 volts. Where the hydroxyls sit on the molecule sets the voltage. That is chemistry, not metaphor.

Does the fingerprint carry provenance? Partly. Voltammetric sensor arrays have discriminated wine varietals at better than 90 percent accuracy. Electrochemical fingerprints of maple species have separated the same species sampled in different seasons. An electrochemical platform has sorted coffees from thirteen origins at about 92 percent.

And then the result that keeps me honest. Ervin and Kariuki compared conventionally, organically and locally grown carrot, tomato, rhubarb, raspberry, strawberry and bell pepper by differential pulse voltammetry, and titled the paper for what they found: minimal impact of cultivation conditions. The fingerprint tracks what the plant is, and what physiological state it is in. It does not reliably track how a farmer grew it.

So two things are true at once, and I want both of them in this book. Food has a real, reproducible electrochemical signature that carries genuine information about the plant. And that signature is not a thermodynamic input to your mitochondria. It is a provenance signal and a bioenergetic red herring at the same time, and most people arguing about this only ever notice one of those.

*(Evidence: established that the measurement is real and reproducible and that the peak potentials track molecular structure. Emerging that it discriminates season and geographic origin, resting on a small number of papers, mostly in leaves and beverages rather than whole foods.)*

### What actually survives the funnel

If most of the chemistry is dismantled on the way in, what is left? Something, and it is more interesting than voltage.

&#91;image: Figure 15.3. What survives the trip from the field to the membrane. The season builds several hundred redox-active molecules in the plant, spread across oxidation potentials from about 0.15 to 0.85 volts. Digestion and central metabolism take nearly all of that apart and hand the respiratory chain two carriers, NADH and FADH2, whatever the food was. The whole energy budget is the 1.14 volt span from the NAD+ couple to oxygen, about 220 kilojoules per mole. Four things go round that funnel rather than through it, and none of them is a voltage: the ratio of the two carriers, the actual NADH to NAD+ ratio, lipid species arriving intact, and the catabolites your microbes make.\]

**The ratio of the two carriers changes with the fuel.** Oxidizing glucose produces roughly five NADH for every FADH2. Oxidizing fatty acids shifts that ratio to about two to one. Those two carriers enter the respiratory chain at different points, so changing their ratio changes how reduced the coenzyme Q pool becomes. An over-reduced Q pool, with a high proton-motive force behind it, is the condition that pushes electrons backwards into complex I, and reverse electron transport at complex I is one of the largest sources of mitochondrial reactive oxygen there is. Filippo Scialò and colleagues set this out in 2017, and Chouchani and colleagues had already shown in 2014 that it is physiologically consequential rather than a laboratory curiosity.

That is the real channel. Not the voltage of a polyphenol. The stoichiometry of what your cells are burning.

**The actual redox ratio moves.** Genetically encoded biosensors can now read the cytosolic NADH to NAD+ ratio inside living cells, and it shifts with which substrate is supplied and differs between cell types given the same one.

**Lipid species arrive intact.** Fatty acids are not reduced to a common currency the way sugars are. What you eat becomes what your membranes are built from, which is the whole argument of Chapter 13.

**And the gut rewrites the rest.** Most polyphenols never reach circulation as themselves. They arrive in the colon, are catabolized by the microbiome, and what circulates are microbial phenolic catabolites and conjugates, in the range of 0.1 to 10 micromolar, differing widely between people according to which microbes they happen to carry.

So food composition does reach mitochondria. It reaches them as substrate stoichiometry, as lipid species, and as microbial metabolites. It does not reach them as a voltage.

*(Evidence: established for the carrier ratio, for reverse electron transport, and for the lipid route. Established for microbial catabolism, with one correction to the older framing: the parent compounds are poorly absorbed, but total systemic exposure to polyphenol-derived material can be far higher than "poorly bioavailable" suggests, because the microbial products are absorbed.)*

### The Seasonal Redox-Mismatch Hypothesis

Here is the hypothesis, stated so that someone could go and break it.

&#91;image: Figure 15.4. Two streams of environmental information arriving at the same mitochondrion. For most of evolutionary history they were correlated. The hypothesis is about what happens when they are not, and the dashed box at the bottom is the version of the argument this book refuses to make.\]

**Mitochondria integrate two separate streams of environmental information. One stream is photoperiod and circadian signaling, which sets the organism's metabolic state. The other is nutrient-derived substrate, which sets cellular redox and electron supply. For almost all of evolutionary history those two streams were correlated, because what was available to eat was a consequence of the same season that set the light. Modern food systems uncouple them, by supplying foods year round that historically signaled a particular season or a particular latitude. The hypothesis is that chronic mismatch between the photoperiod-driven metabolic state and the nutrient-driven substrate flux alters mitochondrial redox balance, membrane potential, electron leak and reactive oxygen signaling, and that in susceptible people this promotes inflammatory signaling.**

It needs a name, because an idea without one cannot be argued about properly. I am calling it **the Seasonal Redox-Mismatch Hypothesis**. And I am going to tell you right now which word in that name I trust least, because it is the word redox. I will come back to that at the end of the chapter, after I have given you the version that can fail.

Read steps three and four above with that in mind. When I said seasonal food carries a signal, I meant the substrate package, not the particle. A tropical fruit delivers a specific combination of sugars, fiber, water, micronutrients and phytochemicals, which changes insulin signaling, the NADH to NAD+ ratio, which fuel gets oxidized and therefore the redox state of the mitochondrion. Meanwhile the light environment is setting what that mitochondrion is prepared for. Those are two real, measurable inputs that can agree or disagree.

That is a smaller claim than the one I started with. It is also one that can be tested, which the original could not be.

**The prediction, unproven, and stated so it can fail.** An organism kept under a winter photoperiod and fed a substrate pattern mimicking summer or tropical availability will show a different mitochondrial redox state, a different rate of reactive oxygen production, or different inflammatory signaling than the same diet delivered under a summer photoperiod, with calories and macronutrients held identical.

**The experiment.** Two by two. Winter photoperiod against summer photoperiod, crossed with a seasonally matched food pattern against a tropical one, calories and macronutrients controlled so that the only variable is the shape of the substrate and the length of the day. Then measure the things this book has spent twelve chapters describing: mitochondrial membrane potential, the NADH to NAD+ ratio, flavin redox state, oxygen consumption, superoxide production at complexes I and III, proton leak, ATP output, circulating insulin and glucose, CRP and cytokines, and clock gene expression in peripheral tissue.

**And the food has to be built, not bought.** This is where every version of this experiment I have seen falls down. The summer food and the winter food came from a shop, which means they differed in transport time at least as much as in growing conditions, and the section above showed which of those is the bigger term. So the real experiment starts a season earlier, in a growth chamber. Genetically identical plants. Same soil, same nutrients, same water, same ripeness at harvest, same hours from harvest to plate. Only photoperiod, photon flux, spectrum and temperature are manipulated. Then characterize what you grew before anybody eats it: metabolomics, lipidomics, isotope ratio analysis, cyclic voltammetry, redox potential, carbohydrate and organic acid profiles. Now you have two foods that differ in the way the hypothesis says matters, and in no other way.

The number that decides it is not the main effect of the food and not the main effect of the light. It is the interaction term. Everything else is decoration.

If the tropical substrate pattern raised mitochondrial reactive oxygen only under the winter photoperiod, the hypothesis has support. If it made no difference, the hypothesis is wrong, or it needs to be rebuilt around a different mechanism. Either result is worth more than the version of this argument I have heard on podcasts, which cannot fail because it was never specified well enough to fail.

### The strongest case against my own hypothesis

I made myself look for evidence against this as hard as I looked for evidence for it. Here is what I found, in roughly descending order of how much it hurts.

**The redox limb has probably already failed.** In 2006 Lotito and Frei asked why plasma antioxidant capacity rises after people eat flavonoid-rich food, and concluded that the rise is not caused by the flavonoids at all. It is urate, produced by the body's own handling of the fructose that arrived with the fruit. The measurable redox signal after eating a plant turns out to be an endogenous response to a sugar load. Whatever the plant's redox chemistry was doing, that is not it.

Underneath that sits the bioavailability problem. Manach and colleagues reviewed 97 human studies and found total polyphenol metabolites in plasma running from zero to about 4 micromolar after a 50 milligram intake, circulating as glucuronidated, sulfated and methylated conjugates rather than as the compounds that were measured in the plant. The molecule on the electrode is not the molecule in the blood.

**The antioxidant story has been tested at scale and lost.** The Cochrane review of antioxidant supplements covers 78 trials and 296,707 participants and reports no benefit for prevention, with beta carotene and vitamin E appearing to increase mortality. Ristow and colleagues showed in 2009 that antioxidant supplementation blocked the improvement in insulin sensitivity that exercise otherwise produces. And the USDA withdrew its own ORAC database of food antioxidant values in May 2012, on the grounds that the values had no demonstrated biological significance after the food was eaten. If my hypothesis needs more antioxidant capacity in the food to mean a better redox state in the person, that step has been tested repeatedly and it does not hold.

**The cleanest test of my interaction found no interaction.** In 2015 Ross and colleagues took F344 rats, the strain used in this work precisely because it is photoresponsive, crossed photoperiod against a high-fat diet, and tested for the interaction explicitly. Long days raised lean mass on ordinary chow but not on the high-fat diet, and there was no interaction at the thyroid hormone genes that carry the photoperiodic signal. Their conclusion was that photoperiod and diet act through independent mechanisms. Togo and colleagues then found the whole photoperiod and diet phenomenon in one rat strain and not in another of the same species, which is a warning about how far any of this travels.

**Humans may not be photoperiodic in the way this argument needs.** Bronson reviewed the question in 2004 and concluded that the evidence humans respond seasonally to day length is inconsistent, proposing that some people are photoresponsive and others simply are not, which would explain why nobody can replicate anybody. A 2024 review of photoperiod and metabolic health notes that direct human studies are very rare, and raises the possibility that because humans are diurnal and laboratory rodents are nocturnal, photoperiod effects on energy metabolism could run in the opposite direction in us. Not weaker. Opposite.

There is a counterweight worth stating. Dopico and colleagues found more than 4,000 protein-coding transcripts in human blood and fat with seasonal expression profiles, and the patterns invert between Europe and Oceania. That is about as clean a demonstration of human seasonality as exists. But seasonality is not photoperiodism. Temperature, infection, crowding and diet all change with the season too, and that study does not separate them.

**And somebody has already found the interaction I was looking for, using a mechanism that is not mine.** In 2025 Levine and colleagues reported in *Science* that dietary unsaturated fat changes phosphorylation of the clock protein PERIOD2 at serine 662, that this phosphorylation is necessary and sufficient for shifting daily activity rhythms to entrain to a seasonal light cycle, and that cutting dietary unsaturated fat left mice unable to adapt to a simulated summer photoperiod. That is a real food composition by photoperiod interaction, with a molecular mechanism and a genetic test behind it.

It is also not my mechanism. It runs through polyunsaturated fat, oxylipins and a kinase acting on a clock protein. It does not pass through mitochondrial redox at any point. I could cite that paper as support for the shape of my idea, and it would be dishonest to cite it as support for the content.

**So here is the amended hypothesis, and it is narrower than the one I stated a few pages ago.**

The mismatch is worth proposing and worth testing, but it belongs around substrate composition, lipid species and microbial metabolites meeting a photoperiod-set physiological state, not around redox capacity delivered by food. Mitochondrial redox, if it is involved at all, sits downstream of the carrier ratio, not downstream of the antioxidant content of a papaya. The name I gave this points directly at the part most likely to be wrong. I am leaving the name in the book with that warning attached rather than quietly swapping it for something safer, because in ten years I want a reader to be able to check which half of it survived.

*(Evidence: the case against the redox limb is established. The absence of any human trial of seasonal against out-of-season food with metabolic outcomes is a fact, not an interpretation. The interaction itself is emerging, demonstrated in mice through a clock mechanism, and contradicted in rats under a clean factorial design.)*

### One more variable, and a caution attached to it

There is a physical property of food-derived hydrogen that does differ by source, and it is closer to what people are reaching for when they talk about food carrying information. Hydrogen comes in isotopes, and the abundance of deuterium varies with geography, climate, plant type and metabolic pathway. Because proton transfer sits at the center of mitochondrial bioenergetics, as Chapter 14 argued at some length, it is chemically reasonable to ask whether isotope composition could shift reaction kinetics through kinetic isotope effects. A 2025 review in *Discover Food* by Répás and colleagues surveys the biological effects of deuterium present in food.

I am including it because it is a real physical property, unlike electron memory, and because leaving it out would be avoiding the strongest form of the question. But I am attaching three warnings to it. The deuterium-depletion field has a commercial side, and some of its most prominent advocates sell products into it. The review I have cited is not in a leading journal. And nothing in that literature establishes that eating tropical fruit at a northern latitude causes inflammation in a human being. *(Evidence: hypothesis. I would put this a rung below the mismatch hypothesis above, and I would want independent work before I moved it.)*

**What would settle it:** the two by two study I described above. Calories and macronutrients held constant, photoperiod crossed with substrate pattern, mitochondrial and inflammatory markers as outcomes. As far as I know that study has not been done. I would very much like to see it, and I am telling you in advance that I would accept a null result.

*(Evidence: steps one and two are established and strong. Steps three and four are my working model and a hypothesis. Nobody should present this as settled, including me.)*

### What I am confident about

Strip out the speculation and there is still a straightforward case for eating locally and seasonally, and it does not require any of the above.

Food picked ripe and eaten soon generally has more in it. Food grown nearby was picked closer to ripeness because it did not have to survive a long journey. Seasonal food is usually cheaper and better, which matters for whether you actually keep doing it. And eating seasonally builds variety across the year automatically, without anyone having to plan it.

That is a good enough argument on its own.

The seasonal signaling idea is a possible additional reason, and I hold it as a hypothesis worth testing rather than a reason you should feel bad about a winter tomato.

### Back to the fire pit

I think often about that day at the ranch.

What my father was doing, without using any of this language, was closing a loop. Here is the animal. Here is what each part did while it was alive. Here is how it becomes food. Here is how we cook it in the ground.

No step of that was abstract. No step of it came in a package with a number printed on the back.

Most people, including me for large parts of my life, eat without any of that connection. The food simply appears. Where it came from, what season it belongs to, what was involved in producing it, and what its electrons are actually carrying are all invisible.

I do not think you need to butcher an elk to eat well. But I do think that knowing what food actually is changes how you treat it.

It is sunlight. It arrived here eight minutes after it left, and then it took a much longer road to reach your plate.

# Part V: Why We Drain

## Chapter 16. Out of Rhythm

For about eight years I was the chief executive of a company in Abu Dhabi.

For most of those years I also had businesses in the United States, and from 2009 onward I was opening restaurants in the Pacific Northwest. Abu Dhabi and Seattle are eleven time zones apart. Someone was always awake somewhere, and it was frequently me.

I thought I was managing it well. I was young, I was fit, I had always been an athlete, and I treated my capacity to function on strange hours as an asset. Plenty of people I worked with did the same. It was practically a credential.

What I understand now is that I was not managing it. I was running a years-long experiment in circadian disruption on myself, and the results of that experiment do not show up as a bad night. They show up slowly, in numbers, years later.

This chapter is about what that actually does.

### The failure is not what people think

Here is the mistake almost everyone makes when they think about circadian health.

They picture the clock slowing down, or getting weak, or stopping. Something breaking.

That is not what happens.

&#91;image: Figure 16.1. Internal desynchrony. In the upper row, every tissue clock keeps the same time. In the lower row, every clock is still working perfectly. They simply no longer agree with each other.\]

Look at the bottom row of that figure and notice what is true of it.

Every one of those clocks is running. None are broken. None have stopped. Each organ is doing its job competently.

And the system is failing anyway, because the brain thinks it is morning while the liver has been told, by a late meal, that it is something else entirely.

**This is internal desynchrony, and it is the central failure mode of Part V of this book.**

It is a genuinely different concept from being tired, and it explains something that otherwise looks strange. Someone can sleep eight hours, eat reasonable food, exercise regularly, and still have bloodwork that drifts the wrong way. Every input looks fine. The inputs are arriving at the wrong times relative to each other.

### How fast it happens

I want to show you the study that convinced me this is a real and fast-acting problem rather than a slow, hypothetical one.

In 2009, Frank Scheer, Steven Shea and colleagues put healthy adults through a laboratory protocol designed to force circadian misalignment. Participants lived on a twenty-eight-hour day, which means their behavioral schedule steadily rotated out of phase with their internal clocks. Food, sleep and activity were all controlled.

In ten days, the misaligned participants showed higher glucose after meals despite higher insulin, meaning their insulin was working less effectively. Their cortisol rhythm reversed. Blood pressure rose. Leptin, the hormone that signals satiety, fell.

And three of eight participants reached postprandial glucose levels that would be classed as prediabetic. *(Scheer and colleagues, 2009. Evidence: well established as a controlled experimental finding.)*

Healthy young adults. Ten days. Nothing wrong with the food.

That study is the reason I treat circadian disruption as a metabolic problem rather than a comfort problem, and it is the experimental backbone of this chapter.

### The jet lag most people give themselves

You do not need a research protocol or a job in Abu Dhabi to produce misalignment. Most people manufacture a version of it every week.

&#91;image: Figure 16.2. Social jet lag. Sleep timing shifts later on free days and back again for the working week, moving the midpoint of the night by an hour or more, repeatedly.\]

Till Roenneberg named this social jet lag. He defines it precisely, as the difference between the midpoint of your sleep on free days and on work days.

You stay up later on Friday and Saturday. You sleep later on Saturday and Sunday. Then Sunday night you try to fall asleep at your weekday time, and cannot, because your clock has already begun shifting. Monday morning you get up with an alarm, in the dark, on the wrong internal time.

You flew nowhere. Your clock does not care about the reason.

Larger social jet lag is associated in population studies with higher body mass index, worse metabolic markers, more smoking and more caffeine use. The associations are consistent. Whether social jet lag causes those outcomes or travels with a cluster of other habits is less settled, and the honest reading is that some of both is likely. *(Evidence: the phenomenon is well established. The associations are consistent. Causation is not proven.)*

What makes it worth naming is that most people have no idea they are doing it, because it does not feel like disruption. It feels like a weekend.

### The two failures that produce it

Part II gave you the mechanism. Here it is assembled.

**The days are too dim.** A clock anchored by bright morning light, receiving a few hundred lux indoors instead of tens of thousands outdoors, gets a weak anchoring signal. The strength of the entraining signal determines how firmly the clock is held.

**The nights are too bright.** Evening light suppresses melatonin, delays the start of biological night, and pushes the clock later. Chapter 8's figure showed an ordinary living room doing this.

Each one alone would be a problem. Together they work in the same direction: a weak pull toward the correct time in the morning, and a strong push in the wrong direction at night.

**And then food arrives at the wrong hour.** Chapter 5 showed that the liver and gut take their timing cue substantially from feeding, not light. So a late meal is not simply extra calories. It is a time signal, sent to a different set of clocks than the one your eyes are talking to.

Which is exactly how you produce the bottom row of Figure 16.1.

### What it does to the repair schedule

Now connect it to Chapter 6, because this is the part that most health writing does not reach.

NAMPT, the bottleneck enzyme for rebuilding NAD+, is under clock control. So NAD+ levels rise and fall daily. Sirtuins, which run repair and metabolic regulation, depend on NAD+ and follow it.

Disrupt the clock and you disrupt that rhythm. In animals with damaged clock genes, the NAD+ rhythm flattens.

So circadian disruption is not only a sleep problem. It is potentially a **repair scheduling** problem. The window in which the repair machinery is best supplied gets blurred.

I want to be careful here. The clock-to-NAD+ link is well established in mice and much less directly demonstrated in humans. What I am confident of is the direction of the reasoning. What I cannot show you is a human trial quantifying it. *(Evidence: strong in animal models, inferred in humans.)*

### Not everyone is built the same

One thing I want to say clearly, because it is often missed and because it makes people feel like failures.

Chronotype is real. Some people are genuinely, biologically wired to run early and some to run late. It has a heritable component, it shifts across the lifespan, and it is not a character trait.

Teenagers run late as a matter of developmental biology, which is why early school start times are, biologically speaking, a scheduled experiment in circadian misalignment on adolescents.

The problem for a late chronotype is not that running late is unhealthy. The problem is that a society built around early start times forces late types into chronic misalignment, every working day, for decades. *(Evidence: well established.)*

So when I get to the practical chapters, the goal is not to make everyone a morning person. It is regularity and a strong signal, at whatever phase your biology actually sits.

### The one-hour experiment we run on everybody

There is a strange natural experiment that happens twice a year in much of the world, and it is worth knowing about.

When clocks shift forward in spring, several studies have reported a small increase in heart attacks in the following days, with a corresponding decrease after the autumn shift back. The effects are modest and not every analysis finds them, but multiple independent datasets have pointed the same way. *(Evidence: strong but the effect size is small and estimates vary.)*

One hour. A shift so small that most people do not consciously notice it.

If a single one-hour shift is detectable in population health statistics, it tells you something about how much weight the timing system carries. And it should make you think carefully about what a habitual two-hour weekend shift is doing over decades.

### What I would tell myself in 2010

I want to close this chapter honestly rather than with a lesson.

During those years, I did not feel damaged. I felt capable. I could function on strange schedules, across time zones, on short sleep, and I took some pride in that. If you had shown me this chapter then, I think I would have agreed with the science and assumed it applied to other people.

The thing about internal desynchrony is that it does not announce itself. There is no symptom called "my liver clock is four hours behind my brain." What there is instead is a gradual drift in the numbers, a slow change in how recovery feels, and an explanation that arrives years later.

I got the explanation later than I would have liked. That is most of why I wrote this book.

## Chapter 17. Overfed, Idle, Inflamed

There is a sentence people say to me constantly, in some form or another.

*I have no energy.*

And for most of my career I heard that the way almost everyone hears it. As a shortage. Something is missing. The tank is low. Find what is depleted and replace it.

The physiology in this book says something different, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

Most people in the modern world are not running out of fuel. They are drowning in it.

The battery is not empty. It is jammed.

### The chain, step by step

&#91;image: Figure 17.1. What constant fuel with no demand actually does, traced through the machinery from Part III.\]

Let me walk it, because every step is something we have already built.

**Fuel keeps arriving.** Eating begins shortly after waking and continues until shortly before bed. Something arrives every ninety minutes or two hours. Electrons enter the system continuously.

**Nothing is spent.** Very little movement. ATP demand stays low, all day.

**The motor stalls.** Protons cannot flow back through ATP synthase because there is no call for ATP. Membrane potential climbs and stays high. This is Chapter 3's overcharged mitochondrion, and Chapter 10's dammed river with nothing drawing from the reservoir.

**Electrons back up.** With the chain unable to pump against a full gradient, NADH accumulates relative to NAD+. The gauge from Chapter 6 now reads congested. Researchers call this reductive stress, which sounds like the opposite of a problem and is not.

**Reactive oxygen rises.** Electrons sitting on the chain in an oxygen-rich environment slip off onto oxygen. Chapter 11 explained exactly this condition: high potential, low demand, maximum leak.

**The cell defends itself.** And here is the step that reframes everything.

### Insulin resistance as a defense

The standard story is that insulin resistance is a malfunction. The cell has stopped listening. Something has gone wrong with the receptor.

There is a better reading, and it has real mechanistic support.

If a cell is already overwhelmed with fuel it cannot process, what is the sensible response to a hormone arriving to say **take in more glucose**?

Refuse it.

Insulin resistance, on this account, is not a failure. It is a cell turning down the delivery because its loading dock is full. Work by Hoehn and colleagues argued explicitly that insulin resistance functions as a cellular antioxidant defense mechanism, protecting the cell from the consequences of further nutrient loading. *(Evidence: strong and developing. The protective framing is well supported mechanistically and is not the only interpretation in the field.)*

That reframing matters enormously for how you think about the problem.

If insulin resistance is a broken receptor, the fix is a drug that makes the cell listen again.

If insulin resistance is a cell refusing a delivery it cannot handle, then forcing it to accept more is not obviously the goal, and the actual fix is to reduce the delivery or increase the demand.

I am not telling anyone to stop a medication. I am telling you what the mechanism suggests about the direction of the solution.

### The fuel has to go somewhere

When muscle and liver cells stop accepting glucose, and fat cells reach their capacity to store safely, the fuel does not disappear. It goes into tissues that were never designed to store it.

Fat accumulates in the liver, in muscle, around organs. This is ectopic fat, and it is a far better predictor of metabolic trouble than overall body weight is.

Roy Taylor's work introduced a useful idea here: the **personal fat threshold**. Each person has an individual limit to how much fat they can store safely in fat tissue. Below it, they are metabolically fine at whatever weight they are. Above it, fat spills into the liver and pancreas and the trouble starts.

This explains something that otherwise looks contradictory: why some people at a high body weight have clean metabolic markers, and why some people at a normal weight develop type 2 diabetes. They have different thresholds. *(Evidence: strong and developing. The concept is well supported and the exact threshold cannot be measured directly in an individual.)*

### Can you still switch fuels?

There is a capacity that gets quietly lost in all of this, and it may be the most useful single way to think about metabolic health.

&#91;image: Figure 17.2. Metabolic flexibility. A healthy system moves between burning fat and burning carbohydrate across the day. A jammed system gets stuck, and the long fat-burning stretch never arrives.\]

A healthy metabolism switches. After a meal it burns mostly carbohydrate. Between meals and overnight it switches to fat. The switching itself is the sign of health.

This is called metabolic flexibility, and impaired switching is a well-established feature of obesity and type 2 diabetes. A metabolically inflexible person stays stuck on carbohydrate, unable to move cleanly into fat oxidation even when fasted. *(Evidence: well established.)*

Look at the lower panel of that figure and notice what is missing. There is no long stretch anywhere in that day where fat is the dominant fuel. Not overnight, because the last meal was late. Not in the morning, because food arrives on waking.

The switch is never thrown, so the machinery for throwing it gets worse.

And this is why Chapter 20 is about eating windows rather than about what to eat. The gap is the intervention.

### Sitting still is its own input

One more thing about the demand side, because it surprises people.

Prolonged sitting is associated with worse metabolic outcomes **independently of whether a person exercises**. Someone who trains for an hour and then sits for the remaining fifteen waking hours is not in the same metabolic state as someone who moves frequently through the day.

The mechanism is plausible from everything in this book. An hour of high demand is one hour. What the rest of the day does to the ratio of fuel arriving to fuel spent is a separate question, and the answer is spread across the whole day. *(Evidence: the association is well established; the independent causal contribution is strong but harder to isolate.)*

Chapter 19 comes back to this. The short version is that exercise and movement are not the same intervention.

### And then the fire starts

Now the third word in this chapter's title.

Damaged and overloaded mitochondria leak their contents. And mitochondrial DNA, released into the cell or the bloodstream, is a problem, because it still looks bacterial.

Your immune system evolved to recognize bacterial DNA as a sign of infection. It cannot tell the difference between DNA from an invading bacterium and DNA from your own mitochondria, because they share an ancestry. So mitochondrial DNA outside a mitochondrion triggers an immune response.

This is one of the routes by which metabolic overload becomes inflammation. Sensing systems including the NLRP3 inflammasome are activated, inflammatory signaling rises, and the process continues because the source of the signal has not gone away. *(Evidence: well established mechanistically.)*

Researchers call the resulting state inflammaging: a low-grade, chronic, unresolving inflammatory tone that rises with age and predicts a wide range of outcomes.

And the reason it matters for this book specifically is Chapter 6. Immune activation and DNA damage both consume NAD+, and CD38, a major NAD+ consumer, rises with inflammation and with age.

So chronic inflammation does not simply cause damage. **It competes for the molecule your repair enzymes need.** The fire burns the water supply.

### The stress part

I want to include chronic psychological stress here rather than in a chapter of its own, because mechanistically it belongs in the same place.

The cortisol rhythm from Chapter 5 is supposed to be a curve: high in the morning, low at night. Under chronic stress, that curve flattens. The morning peak blunts and the evening trough rises.

A flattened cortisol rhythm is associated with worse outcomes across a range of conditions. And notice what kind of failure it is: not too much cortisol or too little, but a **loss of the daily shape**. Which is Chapter 3's argument about dynamic range, arriving in yet another system.

Martin Picard and Bruce McEwen have developed the concept of mitochondrial allostatic load: the idea that the cumulative burden of chronic stress is carried, in part, by mitochondria, and that they are both a target of stress and a mediator of its effects. *(Evidence: strong and developing. The framework is well argued and still being tested.)*

This is why I do not treat stress as a separate topic from metabolic health. In this model they are the same system, stressed from different directions.

### What this chapter is not saying

I want to be careful about a few things, because this territory attracts overstatement.

I am not saying carbohydrate is the problem. I am saying fuel without demand is the problem, and that applies to any fuel.

I am not saying obesity is simply a matter of willpower or timing. Body weight regulation is genuinely complex, involves appetite biology that is not under conscious control, and is influenced by factors from genetics to medication to sleep.

I am not saying insulin resistance is good. I am saying it is protective in origin, which tells you something useful about how to reverse it.

And I am not telling anyone to stop treatment for a diagnosed condition based on a chapter of a book.

What I am saying is that the mechanism runs in a specific direction, and that direction points at demand and timing rather than at finding something to add.

# Part VI: Recharging

## Chapter 18. Restore the Rhythm

I promised in Chapter 1 that I would not hand you a list of biohacks, and I meant it.

So here is how Part VI works. Every recommendation comes attached to the specific failure it is aimed at. If you understand the failure, the recommendation becomes obvious, and more importantly, you can adapt it to your own life without me.

That matters, because your life is not my life. You may work nights. You may live at a latitude where the sun barely clears the horizon in January. You may have small children who have opinions about your sleep schedule.

What does not change is the machinery.

### The day, laid out

&#91;image: Figure 18.1. Four moments in a day, each aimed at a specific piece of the machinery from Part II.\]

### Morning light: the anchor

**What to do.** Get outside within roughly the first hour of waking. Ten to thirty minutes on a bright day, longer when it is overcast. No sunglasses. Not through a window.

**The failure it targets.** A clock that runs slightly longer than twenty-four hours and drifts later every day unless something pulls it back, receiving only a few hundred lux indoors when it needs thousands.

**Why the details matter.**

*Why early.* Light in the morning pulls the clock earlier. The same light late at night pushes it later. This is the phase response curve from Chapter 4, and it is the single reason timing beats duration here. *(Evidence: well established.)*

*Why outside.* Chapter 4's figure. A bright kitchen is a few hundred lux. An overcast morning outdoors is thousands. The difference is not marginal.

*Why no sunglasses.* The melanopsin cells are counting photons arriving at your retina. Sunglasses are designed to reduce exactly that.

*Why not through a window.* Glass cuts intensity, and it removes the UVB entirely, as Chapter 7 showed.

**If you cannot.** If you genuinely cannot get outside in the morning, a bright light box of the kind used for seasonal affective disorder is the established substitute, used shortly after waking. It is not as good as daylight. It is much better than a lit kitchen.

### Daylight through the day: the contrast

**What to do.** Get outside repeatedly, even briefly. Work near a window. Take a call walking. Eat a meal outdoors when the weather allows.

**The failure it targets.** Flattened contrast. Chapter 8's figure showed a modern day where the difference between noon and midnight is smaller than the difference between two rooms.

This one gets less attention than morning light and I think it deserves more. The clock is not taking a single reading at seven in the morning and then ignoring the rest of the day. Daytime light exposure strengthens the whole signal and appears to make people more resistant to the disruptive effect of light at night. *(Evidence: well established.)*

### The last two hours: letting the night start

**What to do.** Drop the light. Use low, warm lamps rather than overhead lighting. Red or incandescent bulbs in the rooms you use at night. Dim your screens and hold them further away.

**The failure it targets.** Melatonin onset cannot begin while the light is up, and an ordinary lit room shortens the biological night by around ninety minutes.

**Why warm light.** Not because red is magic. Because the melanopsin cells peak near 480 nanometers, in the blue, and are far less responsive to long wavelengths. Warm light is, to the cell that reports to your clock, much closer to darkness. *(Evidence: well established in principle.)*

One practical note that is worth more than any gadget: **the overhead light is the problem.** Ceiling lights put light straight into your eyes from above, which is exactly the geometry of a midday sky. A lamp at waist height does much less.

And on blue-blocking glasses, honestly: the mechanism is sound, small trials have shown effects on melatonin and sleep, and the evidence base is thinner than the marketing. They are a reasonable tool. They are not a substitute for turning the lights down, and if you wear them while sitting under a bright ceiling light you have solved part of a problem you could have solved entirely.

### The night: protecting the shift

**What to do.** Make the room as dark as you can. Cover or remove standby lights. Keep the room cool. A warm bath or shower before bed helps, for the reason Chapter 9 gave: you are using warmth to cool faster. And go to bed and get up at close to the same times, including on weekends.

**The failure it targets.** Light during sleep suppresses melatonin further. A warm room blocks the core temperature fall that sleep onset requires. And an irregular schedule is social jet lag, manufactured at home.

I want to put particular weight on **regularity**, because it is the least glamorous item in this chapter and possibly the most important. Chapter 9's finding was that consistency of sleep timing predicted mortality more strongly than duration did. *(Evidence: strong observational finding.)*

The practical version is not perfection. It is narrowing the range. If your bedtime currently varies by three hours across a week, getting it to within an hour is a large change, and it costs nothing.

### Every recommendation and its target

&#91;image: Figure 18.2. Each practice in this chapter, the failure it addresses, where the mechanism was explained, and how strong the evidence is.\]

### If you work nights

I am not going to pretend there is a clean answer here, and I am suspicious of anyone who offers one.

If you work permanent nights, the goal is to pick a phase and hold it, rather than flipping back and forth on days off. Bright light during your working night, real darkness for your daytime sleep including blackout coverings, and consistent meal timing relative to your own schedule.

If you work rotating shifts, that is the hardest case in this entire book, because the system is being asked to re-entrain repeatedly and never completes the process.

What I will not do is tell you that some combination of supplements makes night work equivalent to day work. The evidence does not support that. What the rest of this book offers a shift worker is everything else: the food timing, the movement, the light you can control, and the fact that improving four of the six inputs is better than improving none.

### Grounding, honestly

Grounding, sometimes called earthing, means direct skin contact with the earth. Bare feet on grass or soil.

I do it. I grew up doing it without a name for it. And I am going to label it correctly.

There are small published studies reporting changes in inflammatory markers, blood viscosity, sleep and cortisol rhythm. They are mostly small, mostly short, and difficult to blind, since people know whether they are barefoot on the ground.

My honest position: the evidence is not strong enough for me to place it beside morning light, and it is not so absent that I am going to mock it. **What would move it:** larger trials with a credible sham condition and pre-registered outcomes.

In the meantime, the reason I still recommend spending time barefoot outdoors is not primarily electrical. It is that doing so means you are outside, in daylight, moving, and usually not looking at a screen. Those are four established interventions arriving together.

If grounding also turns out to do something on its own, that would be a bonus. *(Evidence: hypothesis.)*

### The honest minimum

If you take nothing else from this chapter, take these three, in order of how much they are supported.

**Get outside in the morning.** Every day. Ten minutes is a real intervention.

**Turn the lights down for the last two hours.** Not off. Down and warm.

**Go to bed at roughly the same time.** Including Saturday.

Those three cost nothing, require no equipment, and are aimed at the three best-established failures in Part V. Everything else in this chapter is refinement.

## Chapter 19. Restore the Demand

I have owned Jiu-Jitsu studios, and the thing I watched happen there over and over is the reason I believe what is in this chapter.

Someone walks in who has not trained in years. First few weeks, they are wrecked. They cannot hold position, they cannot breathe, everything hurts.

Three months later they are a different person. Not just better at Jiu-Jitsu. Different. They stand differently. They sleep differently. They talk differently about their own capacity.

Nothing was added to them. No supplement, no treatment, nothing supplied from outside.

The only thing that changed is that their body was repeatedly asked for more than it could comfortably give, and then allowed to recover.

That is the entire content of this chapter, and the physiology behind it is now something you can follow precisely.

### Spending is the signal to build

Go back to Chapter 12.

The switch that turns on mitochondrial building is PGC-1α. One of its main activators is AMPK, which is an energy sensor. It detects the ratio of AMP to ATP, which is to say **it detects that energy has been spent.**

This is worth stating as plainly as I can, because it inverts how most people think about energy.

Your body does not build capacity when you supply it with more fuel. It builds capacity when you run short.

The demand comes first. The machinery follows.

Endurance training is the best-characterized stimulus for mitochondrial biogenesis that we know of. Not a supplement, not a food, not a device. Repeated demand. *(Evidence: well established.)*

And the same activity raises NAD+ levels in muscle and increases the expression of the enzymes that rebuild it. So exercise is also, mechanically, one of the better-supported ways to support the spine of this book. *(Evidence: strong.)*

### The number that outperforms almost everything

If I could force one measurement into every annual physical, it would be this one.

&#91;image: Figure 19.1. Three comparisons from a study of 122,007 adults followed after a treadmill test. Being in the lowest fitness group carried roughly five times the risk of death of the fittest group.\]

Cardiorespiratory fitness, usually expressed as VO2max, is a measure of how much oxygen your body can use at maximum effort. Given everything in Part III, you now know what that number is actually measuring: how fast your mitochondria can move electrons to oxygen, and how well your heart and lungs can supply them.

The study followed 122,007 adults who had taken an exercise treadmill test. Being in the lowest fitness group, the bottom quarter, carried an adjusted hazard ratio for death from any cause of 5.04 compared with the elite group.

Five times the risk. And the authors were explicit about something else: they found **no upper limit of benefit**. The fittest people had the lowest mortality, with no point at which more fitness stopped helping.

Two things in that finding matter, and they pull in slightly different directions.

**The largest single step is out of the bottom.** The gap between the lowest group and everyone else dwarfs every other gap in the data. If you are currently sedentary, the most valuable thing in this entire book is the first step off that bottom rung, and it does not require becoming an athlete.

**And there was no ceiling.** Even at the top, more was better. Whatever you may have read about excessive endurance exercise, this dataset did not find a point where fitness itself stopped paying.

Two honest caveats.

First, this is observational. Fitter people differ in many ways, and reverse causation is a real concern: illness reduces fitness as well as the other way around. Good analyses adjust for this, and adjustment is never complete. *(Evidence: the association is well established and consistent across cohorts. Causation is strongly implied by trial evidence on intermediate outcomes, but a randomized trial with mortality as the endpoint does not exist.)*

Second, the figure shows the three comparisons the authors actually published, with their confidence intervals. You will see other group-by-group numbers from this study quoted elsewhere. Several of them are not in the paper, and I have left them out rather than reconstruct them.

### Five kinds of demand

Exercise is not one thing, and treating it as one thing is why people plateau.

&#91;image: Figure 19.2. Five different kinds of demand, each aimed at a different part of the machinery, plus the one that is not optional.\]

**Move often.** This is the one most people skip, and Chapter 17 explained why it matters independently. Breaking up sitting, walking after meals, standing and moving through the working day. Studies interrupting prolonged sitting with short walking breaks show improved glucose handling after meals. This is not exercise. It is keeping fuel moving. *(Evidence: well established.)*

**Build a base.** Easy aerobic work, at an intensity you could hold a conversation through, for the majority of your training hours. This is the most reliable stimulus for mitochondrial building, and the reason it has to be easy is that it has to be sustainable in volume. *(Evidence: well established.)*

**Go hard, briefly.** Short, genuinely hard efforts raise the ceiling. They are potent and they cost a lot of recovery, which is exactly why they should be a small fraction of the week. This is Chapter 11's curve: the most powerful stimulus is also the easiest one to overdose. *(Evidence: well established.)*

**Get strong.** Resistance work against real load. Two sessions a week is enough to change the trajectory.

The case for strength is stronger than most people realize. Muscle is the largest site for glucose disposal in the body, so more of it means more places to put fuel, which speaks directly to Chapter 17. Grip strength, a crude proxy for overall strength, predicts mortality in large international cohorts. And the loss of muscle with age is one of the better predictors of losing independence. *(Evidence: well established.)*

There is also a signaling dimension. Working muscle releases signaling molecules into the bloodstream that affect other tissues, including the brain. The general finding that muscle is an endocrine organ is well established; claims about specific named molecules vary in how well supported they are, and some are considerably less settled than popular coverage suggests. *(Evidence: the general principle is well established; individual myokine claims vary.)*

**Breathe well.** Nasal breathing, slow rate, longer exhales.

The established mechanisms here are more interesting than the mystical framing this topic sometimes attracts. Nasal breathing filters, warms and humidifies air, and your paranasal sinuses produce nitric oxide, which is carried into the lungs. Slow breathing at around six breaths per minute reliably increases heart rate variability, which is Chapter 3's range marker. And carbon dioxide is not simply waste: it is what causes hemoglobin to release oxygen into tissue, which is why breathing harder does not always mean delivering more. *(Evidence: the mechanisms are well established. Specific breathing protocols vary widely in how well they have been tested.)*

### The sixth, which is not optional

Recovery is not the absence of training. It is where the adaptation gets built.

Chapter 11's curve is the whole argument. The stress creates the signal. The recovery period is when the cell responds to that signal by building more capacity. Remove the recovery and you have the signal with no response, repeated, which is the right-hand side of the curve.

I have watched a great many athletes get worse by training harder, and I have done it myself. Overtraining does not feel like weakness. It feels like discipline. That is what makes it dangerous.

The practical markers that something is wrong: resting heart rate drifting up, heart rate variability drifting down, sleep getting worse rather than better, performance falling while effort rises, persistent low mood.

If those are appearing, the answer is not more.

### What I would actually tell someone starting

I promised no biohacks, so here is the honest hierarchy, in order of evidence strength per unit of effort.

**If you are sedentary, walk.** Daily, outdoors, ideally in the morning so it doubles as Chapter 18. Figure 19.1 says the largest available gain in this entire book is the first step off the bottom.

**Add resistance work twice a week.** Anything that genuinely loads the muscle. It does not require a gym.

**Then build aerobic volume.** Easy, conversational, boring.

**Then add hard efforts, sparingly.**

**And protect the recovery throughout**, which mostly means the sleep from Chapter 18.

That sequence is deliberately unglamorous. It also reflects what the evidence actually supports, rather than what is interesting to write about.

### The thing I saw in the studio

I want to come back to those students, because there is a part of it that the physiology only half explains.

What changed in three months was not only their mitochondrial density. It was that they had direct, repeated evidence that they could become capable of something they were not capable of before.

I am not going to dress that up as a mechanism, because I cannot measure it. But I have watched it happen enough times to say that the demand is doing something to a person beyond what shows up on a test.

The body builds what it is asked for. So does the rest of it.

## Chapter 20. The Empty Window

This chapter is about the intervention that costs nothing, requires nothing, and is the hardest one in the book to actually do.

A gap.

Not a food. Not a supplement. A period in each day when nothing arrives.

And I want to be careful with this chapter in a way I have not needed to be with the others, because fasting is a subject where enthusiasm regularly outruns the evidence, and where the advice can genuinely harm some people. I will get to both.

### Why a gap, mechanically

Go back to Chapter 17. The failure was fuel arriving continuously with no demand, producing a system that never discharges, never switches fuels, and sits at high charge with electrons backing up.

Eating windows attack that failure directly, and not by reducing calories.

A gap does three things that eating less, spread across the same number of hours, does not.

**It forces the switch.** Metabolic flexibility, from Chapter 17, is the ability to move between burning carbohydrate and burning fat. That switch only gets exercised if there is a period when carbohydrate is not arriving.

**It lets the gauge reset.** The NAD+ to NADH ratio from Chapter 6 reads congested when fuel is constant. A gap lets it recover.

**And it gives the clock a clean signal.** Chapter 5 showed that peripheral clocks take their timing cue substantially from feeding. A defined eating window is a clear daily time signal to the liver and gut. Eating from waking to bedtime is not.

That third one is why this chapter sits in the same book as Chapter 18 rather than in a diet book.

### What the hours actually do

&#91;image: Figure 20.1. What happens across a fasting window, with an honest statement about which parts are established in humans and which are not.\]

For the first several hours after a meal you are digesting and storing. Insulin is elevated and glucose is the dominant fuel.

Over the first several hours, liver glycogen begins to be drawn down and the body begins shifting toward fat. By twelve to twenty-four hours it is substantially depleted.

Between roughly twelve and sixteen hours the switch becomes clear. Fat oxidation rises and ketones become measurable in the blood. This is the metabolic switch, and it is well documented. *(Evidence: well established.)*

Growth hormone also rises during fasting, which is part of why extended fasting does not strip muscle as fast as a simple calculation would suggest. *(Evidence: well established.)*

### The autophagy claim, and why I will not give you an hour

You have probably seen a specific number. Autophagy switches on at sixteen hours, or eighteen, or twenty-four.

I am not going to give you one, and here is why.

Autophagy is the cell's recycling process, and it is genuinely important. Yoshinori Ohsumi received the Nobel Prize in 2016 for working out its mechanisms. Fasting induces it robustly in animals, and the animal evidence is substantial.

The problem is measurement. Autophagy is extremely difficult to assess in a living human. You cannot biopsy someone's brain, and the markers available in accessible tissue are indirect and disputed. Different tissues almost certainly run on different timelines.

So those specific hour marks come overwhelmingly from animal work, and they have been repeated so often that they now circulate as though they were human measurements. They are not.

What I am confident of: fasting induces autophagy in animals, the mechanism is conserved, and it is reasonable to think something similar happens in people. What I cannot tell you honestly is at what hour, in which tissue, in you. *(Evidence: well established in animals; the human timeline is not established.)*

This is exactly the sort of claim I would have repeated without checking ten years ago.

### Where you put the window matters

Here is the finding that most people do not know, and it is the one that connects this chapter back to Part II.

&#91;image: Figure 20.2. The same ten-hour eating window, placed early or late in the day. The length is identical. What it does is not.\]

Most popular fasting advice treats an eight- or ten-hour window as a single thing. It is not. Where you place it changes the result.

Studies of early time-restricted eating, where the window is shifted toward the morning and eating finishes in the afternoon or early evening, have generally produced better metabolic outcomes than late windows of the same length. In one well-controlled trial, an early window improved insulin sensitivity and blood pressure even without weight loss. *(Sutton and colleagues, 2018.)*

Meanwhile, trials of late windows have been less impressive. One widely reported trial of a noon-to-eight window found no significant weight advantage over three meals a day. *(Lowe and colleagues, 2020.)*

And work directly comparing late eating to early eating, with calories and content held constant, has found late eating associated with increased hunger, reduced energy expenditure, and changes in fat tissue gene expression. *(Vujović and colleagues, 2022.)*

The explanation follows from Chapter 5. Insulin sensitivity has a daily rhythm. Your body handles the same meal differently at eight in the morning than at ten at night. A late window is not just a window. It is a late time signal, sent to clocks that should be winding down.

**So skipping breakfast and eating late is a very different intervention from eating early and stopping in the afternoon, even when both give you the same number of fasting hours.** *(Evidence: strong and still developing. The human trial literature is real but not enormous, and results vary with study design.)*

### The honest state of the human trials

I want to give you the overall picture rather than cherry-picking.

Time-restricted eating in humans has produced **modest** results overall. Some trials show metabolic improvements independent of weight loss, which is the interesting finding. Many show benefits that largely track with people eating less. Several show little.

The animal evidence is considerably stronger than the human evidence. Mice on time-restricted feeding show striking protection against metabolic disease at identical calorie intake, and those findings are robust and replicated. Humans are not mice, they eat on a different circadian scale, and they can be more difficult to control.

My reading: **the mechanism is sound, the human effect sizes are more modest than the enthusiasm suggests, and window placement is probably part of why trials disagree.** *(Evidence: strong in animals, mixed in humans.)*

### Where this is not for everyone

I want this section to be as prominent as the rest of the chapter, because the enthusiasm around fasting has outpaced the caution.

**Do not do this if you have a history of disordered eating.** Structured restriction can reactivate patterns that took a long time to settle, and a book cannot assess that risk for you. This one matters more than any benefit described above.

**Do not do this without medical supervision if you take medication affecting blood glucose**, particularly insulin or sulfonylureas. The risk of hypoglycemia is real and serious.

**Not in pregnancy or while breastfeeding.**

**Not for children or adolescents.**

**Caution if you are underweight**, frail, elderly, or losing muscle. And if you are older, fasting without resistance training risks losing muscle you cannot easily rebuild. Chapter 19 is not optional alongside this chapter.

**Watch your sleep.** If fasting is disrupting your sleep, shorten the window or move it earlier. Sleep is better established than fasting is.

If you have a medical condition or take medication, this is a conversation with your doctor before it is an experiment.

### What I actually do and recommend

For most healthy adults, the version of this chapter I would defend is unremarkable.

**Finish eating two to three hours before bed.** This is the single highest-value change in this chapter for most people, and it costs nothing. It removes a late time signal, allows core temperature to fall, and starts the gap.

**Aim for a gap of twelve to fourteen hours overnight.** For most people this happens naturally once late eating stops. Dinner at seven and breakfast at eight is thirteen hours and requires no special effort.

**Put the eating window earlier rather than later** where your life allows it.

**Longer fasts are optional, not foundational.** They may have additional effects. They also carry more risk and require more care, and the twelve-to-fourteen-hour overnight gap captures most of what is well supported.

**Do not fast to compensate for eating badly.** A gap does not undo a day of food that never should have arrived. That is not how the mechanism works.

### The unglamorous truth

I have written a chapter that argues against most of the fasting content you will encounter.

The extended fast is more interesting to talk about. The exact autophagy hour is more satisfying than "we cannot measure it in humans." The dramatic protocol makes better content than "stop eating after dinner."

But the intervention with the best combination of evidence, safety and sustainability is a twelve to fourteen hour overnight gap, placed earlier rather than later, done most days.

That is it. That is the chapter.

It is the kind of recommendation nobody can sell you anything for, which is one of the reasons I trust it.

# Part VII: The Project

## Chapter 21. The 90-Day Human Battery Project

I am participant number one.

I do my own Day 0 bloodwork. I follow the protocol. I do my own Day 90 retest, alongside everyone else. My numbers go in the same column as theirs.

I want to say why that matters to me, and it is not humility.

If I am going to ask people to pay attention to a model of human physiology that includes several claims I have labeled as my own working model, the least I can do is put myself in the position of being wrong in public. If the numbers do not move, I will know before you do, and I will have to say so.

That is the difference between a program and a protocol. A program sells you an outcome. A protocol runs an experiment and reports the result.

### What we are measuring, and what we are not

Let me deal with the obvious problem first, because it is the one a serious reader will have been holding since Chapter 2.

This entire book is built on membrane potential. And **there is no blood test for membrane potential.** There is no wearable that reads it. Nothing in this protocol measures the thing the book is about.

I am not going to pretend otherwise.

What we can do is measure things that shift when the underlying system shifts. Indirect markers, and they sort into two kinds: five dimensions the Score is built from, and a set of markers that belongs to no dimension at all and still matters.

&#91;image: Figure 21.1. What the panel measures, and what it does not. Five scored dimensions make the Human Battery Score, and it is complete across them. A second set of markers is clinically meaningful, returned to you and flagged when out of range, and belongs to no dimension. Three dimensions of the model sit below, reached by nothing in this panel, and their absence does not make a score incomplete.\]

**FLOW** asks how fast electrons move, and whether your fuel can switch. Respiratory exchange ratio and oxygen consumption measure it most directly. Where a metabolic cart is not available, the glucose regulation panel stands in: fasting insulin, which is the single most informative and most under-ordered test in this group because it rises years before glucose does; HbA1c for the longer view; and the triglyceride-to-HDL ratio, a crude but useful insulin-resistance proxy. That is Chapter 17.

**CAPACITY** asks how much reserve sits above your resting demand. A VO2max estimate, which is Figure 19.1. Grip strength, which predicts mortality. Sit-to-stand, walking speed, balance. These are the cheapest and most predictive measurements in the whole protocol and almost nobody takes them. That is Chapter 19.

**TIMING** asks whether your clock and your tissues still agree. Logged sleep and wake times, the regularity of them, and when you eat. Heart rate variability and heart rate recovery, which are Chapter 3's dynamic range made measurable. That is Chapters 5, 9 and 16.

**STRUCTURE** asks what your membranes are built from. The Omega-3 Index, which is the only test in this entire panel that measures a structural material rather than a concentration. That is Chapter 13, and it is required rather than optional.

**ENVIRONMENT** asks what is actually arriving, and when. Morning light timing and duration. First meal and last meal. These are logged rather than drawn from a vein, they cost nothing, and they are the only dimension you can change today rather than in ninety days. That is Chapters 4, 7 and 18.

**And then the markers that belong to no dimension at all.** hs-CRP for inflammation. GGT, an underappreciated marker of oxidative and metabolic stress as well as liver load. Uric acid. Homocysteine. Body composition. Vitamin D.

These are clinically meaningful, they are returned to you, and they are flagged when they sit outside a reference range. They do not enter the Score. I want to be explicit about why, because folding everything into one number is exactly the temptation this book argues against. **A marker earns a place in a dimension by measuring that dimension, not by being important.** hs-CRP tells you something real about inflammation and nothing specific about flow, capacity, timing, structure or environment.

Vitamin D is the clearest case, and the one I got wrong first. It is tempting to score it under ENVIRONMENT as a proxy for sunlight. It is not one. The protocol recommends D3, so what serum vitamin D measures is your supplementation. And Chapter 7 argues that the vitamin D trials underperformed precisely because they tested a molecule instead of the sunlight that makes it. Using it as a light proxy would repeat, inside my own scoring, the error I spent a whole chapter describing.

**Three more dimensions exist in this model and are not here.** Charge, redox and leak are in the Coda, and they carry no number because nothing in this panel reaches them. Your Score is complete across what can be measured today. It is not incomplete because the model is larger than the instruments.

Across the five dimensions and the referable set it comes to around thirty measurements.

### Why those five

The grouping is not arbitrary. It maps onto the verbs from Chapter 1.

FLOW is charge and convert. CAPACITY is what that conversion can produce on demand. TIMING is repeat. STRUCTURE is the material the other four run on. ENVIRONMENT is what the system is handed in the first place.

Protect and repair get no dimension of their own, and that is deliberate rather than an oversight. Their markers are the referable ones. They tell you something is wrong without telling you which part of the battery it is, and a number that cannot say where the problem is has no business inside a score that claims to.

And there is one more reason I like this structure. It is very hard to move all five in the same direction by accident, or by gaming a single number. Someone can lose weight and get worse. Someone can drop their cholesterol and lose muscle. Five dimensions moving together is a harder and more meaningful thing than any one of them moving alone.

### Ninety days, and why not thirty

&#91;image: Figure 21.2. The structure of the ninety days, the six pillars, the four entry tiers, and why the timescale is what it is.\]

The timescale is set by biology, not by marketing.

**HbA1c** reflects roughly two to three months of average blood glucose, because it measures glycation of hemoglobin in red blood cells. Retest it at thirty days and you are largely re-reading your old life.

**Red blood cells** turn over across roughly four months, which is also what makes the Omega-3 Index a stable measure rather than a snapshot of yesterday's dinner.

**Mitochondrial adaptation** takes weeks of repeated demand.

**And habits** survive one enthusiastic month. Ninety days tells you whether they survived the second and the third, which is the only version that matters.

### Four tiers, because you are not me

The protocol has four entry levels: Beginner, Intermediate, Advanced and Pro. You enter based on how you already live, not on how motivated you feel on the first day.

I think this is one of the more important design decisions in the whole thing, and it comes directly out of Chapter 11.

Hormesis says the useful stress is the one you can recover from. That dose is different for a person who already trains six days a week than for a person who has not exercised in a decade. Giving them the same protocol means giving at least one of them the wrong dose.

And people can move up mid-program. If your Day 45 markers say you have adapted, the protocol should get harder. That is the curve working correctly.

### The six pillars

Morning sunlight. Mineral-rich water. Movement. Food timing. Sleep. Darkness at night.

Every one of them has a chapter behind it, and by now you can say what each is aimed at without looking it up. That was the point of writing the book this way.

Notice what is not on that list. No supplement is a pillar. Nothing you have to buy from anyone is a pillar. I take no commission on anything I recommend in this book, and that was easier to arrange than it sounds, because the things with the best evidence do not have a sales channel.

### Running an honest experiment on yourself

This is the part that separates a self-experiment from a story you tell yourself afterwards, and I want to teach it properly.

**Take the baseline before you change anything.** Not after a good week. The temptation to start living better and then measure is enormous, and it destroys the comparison.

**Change several things at once, and accept what that costs you.** The program changes six inputs together, because that is what an actual life change looks like. The price is that you will not know which one did it. That is a real limitation and I am naming it rather than hiding it.

**Expect regression to the mean.** If your baseline was measured on an unusually bad day, your retest will look better whatever you did. This is the single most common way people fool themselves, and it is why extreme starting values should be interpreted cautiously.

**Watch for seasonality.** A Day 0 in January and a Day 90 in April include a change of season. Vitamin D alone will move. So will activity, mood and daylight exposure.

**Track adherence, not just outcomes.** If your markers did not move, the first question is whether you actually did the protocol. A daily log makes that answerable rather than a matter of memory.

**And know that you cannot blind yourself.** You know you are doing this. Expectation affects subjective measures, which is exactly why the panel is weighted toward objective ones.

An n-of-1 experiment cannot prove that an intervention works. What it can tell you is what happened in you, under conditions you recorded, which is considerably more than most people ever know about their own biology.

### The score, and what it is not

The five scored dimensions feed into a single number, the Human Battery Score.

I want to be blunt about what that number is.

It is a **communication tool**. It exists because thirty markers is too many for a person to hold in their head, and because a direction of travel is easier to act on than a spreadsheet.

It is not a diagnosis. It is not a measure of your worth, your discipline or your biological age. It is not comparable between people in any meaningful way, because the inputs are weighted by my judgment and not by a validated algorithm.

**The only number that matters is the change in your own score between Day 0 and Day 90.**

And I want to say something about composite scores generally, because they have a real failure mode. Combining many measurements into one can hide the important movement. A score can improve while one component gets meaningfully worse. That is why the protocol reports the components alongside the score, and why the components are what a clinician should look at.

If your score goes up and your hs-CRP doubles, the score is wrong and the CRP is right.

### What this could become

The reason I built this as software rather than as a PDF is that a protocol run by one person is an anecdote, and a protocol run by thousands of people with structured Day 0 and Day 90 data is something else.

Not a clinical trial. There is no control group, no randomization, no blinding, and I am not going to call it something it is not.

But a large, structured, longitudinal dataset of people making defined changes and measuring the same panel before and after is genuinely useful. It can tell you which markers move and which do not. It can show where the tiers are wrong. It can generate the hypotheses that a real trial would then test.

And it can tell me where this book is wrong, which is the part I care about most.

If the seasonal eating idea from Chapter 15 produces nothing, I want to know. If the grounding from Chapter 18 shows no signal, I want to know that too. I have labeled every one of those claims so that they can be checked.

### The question, answered

I opened this book with a question and I owe you the answer.

**What determines the electrical state of a human cell, and what allows the body to maintain the gradients necessary for organized biological function?**

Here is the answer, assembled from everything in these pages.

The electrical state of a cell is set by what its membranes are built from, what fuel arrives and when, how much demand is placed on it, whether its mitochondria are being built and cleared on schedule, and whether the whole system knows what time it is.

And the body maintains those gradients by spending energy continuously to hold a difference against a universe that is constantly erasing it, on a schedule set by the sun, using materials supplied by food, with repair that runs at night and capacity that grows only in response to demand.

Which means the practical answer is not one thing. It is a system, and the inputs are light, darkness, water, food, timing, movement, temperature and recovery.

You picked up this book thinking something reasonable. *My body needs energy.*

Here is the thing I hope you are thinking now.

**My biology depends on maintaining precisely regulated gradients of energy and information, synchronized with my environment, so that my cells can produce energy, communicate, repair themselves, and remain organized.**

That is a harder sentence. It is also the difference between chasing symptoms and understanding the system you live inside.

## Coda. The Human Battery, Stated in Full

### Five currencies, not one

The conventional story of cellular energy has one currency in it. Food becomes ATP. Everything else is detail.

I think that story is not wrong so much as it is a single frame from a longer film. The model I work from has five physical currencies moving through the system, and they interact.

&#91;image: Figure C.1. The five physical currencies this model runs on, and the chapters where each one was built. The conventional account tracks only the second of them.\]

**Photons.** Light arrives, and it does two different jobs. It carries timing information through the melanopsin pathway to the clock, which is Chapters 4 through 6. And it does a small amount of direct chemistry in your skin through chromophores and opsins, which is Chapter 7. It is not fuel. It is information plus a little chemistry, and both halves matter.

**Electrons.** Food supplies reducing equivalents, NADH and FADH2 hand electrons into the respiratory chain, and the controlled fall of those electrons toward oxygen is what releases the energy that everything else spends. Chapters 10 and 15.

**Protons and water.** The energy released by electron transfer is converted into an electrochemical gradient by moving protons, and ordered interfacial water is part of the physical machinery that moves them. Chapters 10 and 14.

**Fields.** The gradient is not a metaphor. It is 150 to 180 millivolts across a membrane five nanometers thick, which is a field strength of roughly thirty million volts per meter, and a second battery at the cell surface holding tens of millivolts. Chapter 2.

**Membrane structure.** None of the above happens in free solution. It happens inside a lipid bilayer whose composition determines how the proteins embedded in it can move, fold and function. Chapter 13.

The reason I list five rather than one is that the failures I see in practice are rarely failures of fuel supply. Almost nobody walks into my office short of calories. They walk in with the timing wrong, or the membranes wrong, or the demand signal missing, and their fuel supply is the one thing that is fine.

### Oxygen, which is the other half of the battery

I have to add something to that list, because leaving it out would be a real omission and it is the kind of omission that makes a model look mystical when it is not.

A battery does not work because one terminal exists. It works because there is a difference between two terminals.

Food gives you the negative end. The NAD+ and NADH couple sits at roughly minus 0.32 volts. Oxygen gives you the positive end. The oxygen and water couple sits at roughly plus 0.82 volts. The gap between those two numbers, a little over one volt, is the entire energy budget of aerobic life. Everything in Part III happens inside that gap.

This is why I stopped saying that we need oxygen for energy, which is true in the way that saying a river needs a sea is true. Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor. It is the drain the whole river runs toward, and it is a good drain specifically because oxygen is unusually greedy for electrons.

And here is the part that ties it to Chapter 11. The property that makes oxygen an excellent electron acceptor is the same property that makes it dangerous. Give it one electron instead of four and you get superoxide. The thing that powers you and the thing that damages you are not two different features of oxygen. They are the same feature, used well or used badly. *(Evidence: well established. These are standard bioenergetic redox potentials.)*

I find that clarifying rather than alarming. It means the goal was never to eliminate oxidative stress. The goal is to run the drain properly.

### Why "battery" is not a metaphor

I want to defend the title of this book on physical grounds, briefly, because the word battery is doing real work rather than decorative work.

A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator. Put charge on one side and you get a voltage across the gap. That is a physical definition, not an analogy.

Your cell membrane is a thin insulating sheet with conducting salt solution on both sides. It has a measurable capacitance, close to one microfarad per square centimeter, and that number is remarkably consistent across cell types, because it is set by the thickness and the dielectric properties of a lipid bilayer rather than by anything biological.

So when I say the cell holds charge, I am not reaching for a comparison. I am describing a capacitor with a measured value. Chapter 2 gave you the voltages. This is the reason those voltages are possible at all.

And notice what follows, because this is where Chapter 13 stops being about nutrition and becomes about electronics. The insulator in that capacitor is made of lipid. Change the lipid composition and you change the physical properties of the insulator. Membrane fats are not only structural. They are part of the electrical specification. *(Evidence: membrane capacitance is well established and textbook. That dietary fat composition meaningfully changes the electrical behavior of human membranes in a health-relevant way is my extension of it, and it sits with the other hypotheses.)*

### Temperature is information too

Mitochondria do not only make ATP. They deliberately waste some of the gradient as heat, which is what proton leak is, and in brown fat there is a protein whose entire job is to do that on purpose.

For a long time I filed that under inefficiency. I no longer do. Temperature changes membrane fluidity, which changes everything in Chapter 13. It changes enzyme kinetics, which changes every rate in Part III. And your core body temperature rhythm is one of the main signals the clock uses to keep peripheral tissues in step, which is Chapter 5. Heat is not the waste product of the system. It is one of the system's outputs, and one of its inputs.

There is also a genuinely contested finding here that I am going to mention rather than hide. In 2018, Dominique Chrétien and colleagues published work in *PLoS Biology* arguing that mitochondria are physiologically maintained at close to 50 degrees Celsius, far above body temperature. The paper was published alongside a commentary asking, essentially, whether that could possibly be right, and the measurement method has been argued about ever since. I have no settled opinion on it. I include it because if it is even approximately true, a great deal of what we assume about enzyme kinetics inside the mitochondrion is calibrated to the wrong temperature. *(Evidence: contested. Published in a serious journal, disputed on methodological grounds, unresolved at the time of writing.)*

### You do not have batteries. You have a grid.

One more correction to the picture I have been drawing, and it is a correction to my own framing.

I have been writing as though a cell contains a population of individual mitochondria, each one its own small battery. That is the picture most people carry and it is the picture the word battery encourages.

It is wrong, or at least badly incomplete. Mitochondria continuously fuse together and divide apart. They form connected networks, share contents, and reorganize the network in response to conditions. When a section is damaged, fission isolates it and the disposal machinery from Chapter 12 removes that piece rather than the whole.

So the right picture is a power grid, not a shelf of batteries. And grids have properties that individual batteries do not: connectivity, redundancy, the ability to route around a failure, and the ability to isolate one before it spreads.

I think network structure is plausibly another dimension of what I have been calling battery health, and unlike most of the speculative material in this Coda, it is already measurable by imaging in a laboratory. *(Evidence: mitochondrial fusion, fission and quality control are well established. That network topology will prove to be a useful clinical dimension of health is my hypothesis.)*

### A readout we do not have yet

The last piece is not a mechanism. It is an instrument I wish existed.

Oxidative reactions in living tissue emit light. Very little of it, far below anything you could see, but real and measurable with sensitive detectors. The physics is not controversial: excited carbonyls and singlet oxygen relaxing to their ground states emit photons, and Michal Cifra and Pavel Pospíšil laid out the mechanisms in a 2014 review. In 2017, Rosilene Burgos and colleagues showed in *Scientific Reports* that this emission tracks oxidative metabolism in cells, and drops when the enzyme driving the oxidative burst is inhibited.

Now, the claim I am not making. There is a substantial literature proposing that cells use these photons to communicate with each other. I have read a good deal of it. I do not think the evidence supports it, and I am not going to lean on it.

The question I actually care about is much more boring and much more useful. **If oxidative metabolism emits light, and the amount of light tracks the redox state, could that emission become a noninvasive readout of the thing this entire book is about?**

Every marker in Chapter 21 requires a needle. A redox measurement you could take by holding still in a dark box would change what a book like this can ask people to do. I have no idea whether it will work at the level of a whole person. I would like someone to find out. *(Evidence: the emission is real and measured. Its use as a clinical readout is unproven. Its use as a communication channel between cells is, in my reading, not supported.)*

### The currency I have not yet discussed: spin

There is a third thing an electron has, and the book has not mentioned it once.

Biochemistry draws electrons as small balls moving down a chain. NADH to complex I to coenzyme Q to complex III to cytochrome c to complex IV. That picture tracks two properties: where the electron is, and how much energy it has.

An electron also has spin. And in certain reactions, when a pair of radicals is created together, the two unpaired electrons start out with their spins correlated. Whether that pair goes on to react one way or another can depend on how those spins evolve, and spin evolution can be influenced by magnetic fields far too weak to do anything through ordinary energetics. This is not fringe physics. It is spin chemistry, and it has been studied for decades.

**The strongest biological case for it is in birds.** The radical-pair mechanism is the leading explanation for the light-dependent magnetic compass that night-migratory songbirds navigate by. Peter Hore and Henrik Mouritsen laid out the case in the *Annual Review of Biophysics* in 2016. In 2021, Jingjing Xu and colleagues published work in *Nature* showing that cryptochrome 4 from the European robin is magnetically sensitive in a test tube, and more so than cryptochrome 4 from chicken and pigeon, two birds that do not migrate. The proposed mechanism runs through four successive flavin and tryptophan radical pairs. *(Evidence: the physics is established. The bird biology is strong and still developing.)*

**And here is the problem with importing that into humans, which I am going to state rather than skip.** The bird compass runs on cryptochrome, and cryptochrome does that job by binding a flavin cofactor and being photoreduced. In 2017, Roger Kutta and colleagues published a paper in *Scientific Reports* titled, unhelpfully for anyone hoping otherwise, "Vertebrate Cryptochromes are Vestigial Flavoproteins." Their finding is that the Type II cryptochromes, the class mammals have, lack the structural features to hold the flavin securely and are predominantly cofactor-free. Whatever human cryptochrome is doing in your clock, it is probably not running a light-dependent magnetic compass. There is also a serious physical objection to the mechanism in general: Nathan Babcock and Daniel Kattnig showed in 2020 that dipolar coupling between the two electrons works against the sensitivity the mechanism requires.

So if I stopped there, the honest conclusion would be that spin chemistry is real, birds probably use it, and humans probably do not have the receptor for it.

**But there is a second route, and it does not need cryptochrome at all.** Mitochondria are full of flavins. Flavin mononucleotide sits in complex I. Flavin adenine dinucleotide sits in complex II. Reduced flavoenzymes activating molecular oxygen is exactly the reaction that produces superoxide, which is Chapter 11. And that reaction forms spin-correlated radical pairs, because that is what flavin and oxygen chemistry does.

In 2016, Robert Usselman and colleagues published in *Scientific Reports* an experiment in live human endothelial cells. Using a 50 microtesla static field with a 20 microtesla oscillating field at 1.4 megahertz, tuned to Zeeman resonance, they shifted the ratio of superoxide to hydrogen peroxide the cells produced, and the oxygen consumption and glycolysis rates changed with it. Their interpretation is coherent singlet to triplet mixing at the point where reactive oxygen is formed.

I want to be precise about what that does and does not show, because this is the exact spot where this kind of finding gets turned into a product.

**It does show** that spin-dependent chemistry can reach cellular bioenergetics, in human cells, at field strengths in the range of the Earth's own field. That is not nothing. It is the first experimental bridge I know of between quantum spin dynamics and the machinery this entire book is about.

**It does not show** that the Earth's ambient magnetic field is controlling your mitochondria. The effect required an oscillating field tuned to a resonance. Ambient geomagnetism is not that. Anyone selling you a device on the strength of this paper has skipped the part where the resonance condition was applied deliberately.

There is one more piece, and it is very recent. In 2026, a group including Douglas Wallace, whose work on mitochondrial genetics runs through the background of this whole book, published a preprint describing mtMagLOV2: a genetically engineered flavoprotein, installed into the mitochondrial matrix, whose magnetically sensitive fluorescence reports the redox state of mitochondrial flavins. They used it to distinguish cancer cells, which lean on glycolysis, from cardiomyocytes, which lean on oxidative phosphorylation.

Notice what that paper actually demonstrates, because it cuts both ways. Spin chemistry can absolutely be coupled to mitochondrial bioenergetics and read out optically. And they had to engineer the protein and put it there. It was not already doing the job. *(Evidence: emerging. A preprint, not yet peer reviewed, and I am flagging it as such.)*

**So here is my position on spin, stated as mine.** I think the question of whether spin-dependent chemistry influences mitochondrial signaling under ordinary physiological conditions is one of the most interesting open questions in this field, and I think it is a real question rather than a mystical one, because there is a specific molecular mechanism and there are published experiments on both sides. I do not think it is established. I would be careful with anyone who tells you it is, in either direction. *(Evidence: hypothesis. What would settle it: whether spin effects on mitochondrial reactive oxygen persist at ambient field strengths without an applied resonance, in intact tissue, with the prediction stated in advance.)*

### What health is, in this model

Chapter 3 argued that health is not maximum voltage. It is tissue-appropriate voltage, the ability to change it on demand, and coordination between neighboring cells. Coherence, not maximum. That is coherence across space.

Everything since has been quietly adding a second axis. The clock chapters are about coherence across time. The light chapters are about whether the timing signal arriving matches the metabolic state it arrives into. Chapter 15 is about whether the food signal and the light signal agree. Chapter 17 is about what happens when fuel arrives and demand does not.

So here is the fuller statement, and it is the thesis of the book.

**Health emerges when environmental timing, photon exposure, nutrient-derived reducing equivalents, membrane architecture, proton and water dynamics, mitochondrial membrane potential and redox signaling stay appropriately coordinated in space and in time. Disease, in this model, is not primarily a shortage of energy. It is a loss of coordination between the parts of a system that must agree in order to work.**

I find that a more useful frame than the one I was trained in, for three reasons.

It explains why people with no shortage of calories are exhausted. It explains why interventions that add energy to the system so often fail, while interventions that restore timing so often work. And it makes the same prediction across conditions that are usually treated as unrelated, which is the kind of thing a framework is supposed to do.

It is still my framework. *(Evidence: the individual mechanisms are established. The unifying claim is mine, and it has not been tested as a unified theory. Hold it the way I do: seriously, and provisionally.)*

That is a straightforward study. It needs no new physics, no new instruments, and no belief in anything unusual. It needs a cohort and somebody willing to run the numbers, including the possibility that the answer is no.

### The frontier version, labeled as such

And now the version I would defend in a room full of people who disagree with me.

**The extreme form of the Human Battery hypothesis is that photon timing, electron transfer, spin-dependent chemistry, membrane lipid physics, interfacial water and electromagnetic fields together form a coupled biological information system, and that mitochondria are one of the principal interfaces where those physical signals are converted into biochemical state.**

Parts of that sentence are established. Parts have experimental support and uncertain physiological importance. The complete coupled system is a hypothesis and nothing more, and I am telling you that in the same paragraph where I state it, rather than three chapters later in a footnote.

What I will not do is soften it into something safe. I think it is right. ### Human Battery Theory, version one

If this is going to be a model rather than a collection of interesting ideas, it needs to be stated as one. Here are the principles it rests on. I have numbered them so that anyone who wants to argue with me can say which number.

**One.** Life is a non-equilibrium system. It holds its order by converting energy continuously, and when the conversion stops the order goes, quickly. *(Established.)*

**Two.** The conversion is electrical before it is chemical. Energy from food is first stored as charge held apart across a membrane. ATP is what that charge gets spent into, not where it lives. *(Established.)*

**Three.** You run two batteries, not one. The inner mitochondrial membrane and the plasma membrane, and they are coupled, because the second is maintained by pumps the first pays for. *(Established.)*

**Four.** Membrane voltage is not only power. It is a credential. Cells use it to decide which parts are alive, which are damaged, and which get dismantled. *(Established.)*

**Five.** Health is tissue-appropriate voltage and the ability to change it on demand, not maximum voltage. A cell held rigidly at a perfect number is not healthy, it is unresponsive. *(Mine.)*

**Six.** The whole system runs on a schedule, and light sets the schedule. Repair, disposal and metabolic switching are timed events, not continuous background processes. *(Established.)*

**Seven.** Environmental information and metabolic substrate are two separate inputs. For nearly all of evolutionary history they arrived together. They now frequently do not. *(Mine.)*

**Eight.** The physical environment is as much a part of the machine as the chemistry: the lipid the proteins sit inside, the ordered water protons cross, the temperature everything runs at. *(Partly established, partly mine.)*

**Nine.** Reactive oxygen is a signal before it is damage. Its location, timing and magnitude matter more than its mere presence, and a system with none of it cannot adapt. *(Established, and increasingly mainstream.)*

**Ten.** Disease, in this model, is loss of coordination rather than shortage of energy. Fix the coordination and the energy usually takes care of itself. *(Mine, and it is the whole book.)*

## Testing the Thesis: A Critical Evaluation of the Central Energy Model

This section exists because a model that cannot be wrong is not worth holding.

So here I am going to argue against my own book, as well as I can.

### What the thesis actually claims

Stripped to its core:

*Human health depends on the ability to create, regulate and coordinate electrochemical gradients across membranes, on a schedule synchronized to the environment, and most modern chronic dysfunction can be usefully understood as a failure of that regulation, timing or coordination.*

Note the words **usefully understood**. That is deliberate. The thesis is a framework claim, not a causal law, and frameworks are judged by whether they organize evidence and generate testable predictions, not by whether they are true in the way a measurement is true.

### The strongest objections

**Objection one: the framework is unfalsifiable as stated.**

This is the serious one. "Health depends on regulated gradients" is broad enough to accommodate almost any finding. If someone improves, the gradients were regulated. If they do not, they were not.

My answer is that the framework itself is not what should be tested. Its specific predictions should be. Those are listed below, and several of them could fail.

But I concede the objection has force, and a reader should be suspicious of any framework that explains everything.

**Objection two: the metaphor may be doing work the biology does not support.**

Calling the body a battery is useful for explanation. The risk is that the metaphor then starts generating conclusions. "Charge it up" is an intuition that follows from the metaphor and is contradicted by the biology, which is why Chapter 3 exists.

I have tried to police this throughout. I am not certain I have caught every instance.

**Objection three: much of the supporting research is in animals.**

The NAD+ and clock loop, the peripheral clock food-entrainment work, most of the autophagy evidence, the mitohormesis findings, the sleep and reactive oxygen work: heavily mouse, fly and cell culture. Mice are nocturnal, short-lived, and metabolically different from humans in important ways.

The history of translating mouse metabolic findings to humans is not good. This is a real limitation and I have flagged it chapter by chapter.

**Objection four: the interventions are not novel, and they work for boring reasons.**

A skeptic could say: your recommendations are sleep well, exercise, eat reasonably, get sunlight, do not eat late. These are recommended by every mainstream health body. The bioelectric framework is elaborate scaffolding that produces conventional advice.

This objection is largely correct, and I want to say so plainly.

The framework's value, if it has any, is not that it produces different recommendations. It is that it explains **why** they work, links them to each other, and explains **timing**, which conventional advice mostly ignores. If the framework is worth anything, its distinctive contribution is the when, not the what.

**Objection five: confirmation bias in the researchers I favor.**

I have drawn heavily on Wallace, Ling, Pollack, Crawford, Levin and Van Wijk. These are not randomly chosen. They are researchers whose work supports an energetic and bioelectric view of biology.

A fair critic would note that I have not engaged equally deeply with researchers who would reject this framing. That is true. I have tried to represent the mainstream objections honestly in each chapter, and representing an objection is not the same as having spent years inside it.

### What would make me abandon or substantially revise this

Here are specific results that would damage the thesis.

**If circadian interventions consistently failed in well-designed human trials.** If large, well-controlled trials of morning light, consistent sleep timing and earlier eating windows produced no metabolic or health benefit, the timing half of this book would collapse. This is testable and partly being tested now.

**If membrane potential turned out to be purely downstream.** If it were shown that cellular voltage is entirely a consequence of metabolic state with no independent regulatory role, the organizing idea would become a description rather than an explanation.

**If the mitohormesis findings failed to replicate in humans.** If reactive oxygen signaling turned out not to matter for human adaptation, Chapter 11 would need rewriting and Chapter 19's rationale would weaken.

**If the water wire work turned out to be an artifact of the simulations.** The structural finding would stand, because 275 ordered water molecules were resolved in a real enzyme. But the mechanism I lean on in Chapter 14, that proton transfer through those complexes depends on water molecules being correctly arranged, is largely computational at present. If higher-resolution structures and better experiments showed the proton pathways working some other way, the middle of Chapter 14 would need rewriting and the chapter's claim to be the firmest ground in the water argument would collapse.

**If skin photoreception turned out to be biologically trivial.** The opsins are in the skin and that is not in dispute. Whether what they do is large enough to matter at the level of a person's health is not settled. If it went the other way, the second half of Chapter 4 and most of the new material in Chapter 7 would shrink back to a paragraph, and light would go back to being almost entirely about timing.

**If the environmental mismatch experiment came back null.** If animals held under a winter photoperiod and fed a tropical substrate pattern showed no difference in mitochondrial redox state, reactive oxygen production or inflammatory markers against the same diet under a summer photoperiod, the seasonal argument in Chapter 15 loses its mechanism and I would drop it. What survives either way is the ordinary case for eating locally and in season, which never depended on any of this.

**If spin effects disappeared at ambient field strength.** The one published demonstration I lean on in the Coda used an oscillating field tuned to resonance, not the field you are standing in. If spin-dependent effects on mitochondrial reactive oxygen turn out to require that applied resonance and never appear without it, that arrow comes off the diagram. The model loses nothing else, because I have deliberately built nothing on top of it.

**If early versus late eating showed no difference at matched calories.** Chapter 20 rests substantially on this, and it is a clean, testable question.

**If coherence produced no predictions.** If the coherence framing never generates a measurement that outperforms existing markers, it is a poetic description rather than a scientific claim, and I would have to say so.

### Predictions this framework makes

A framework earns its place by predicting things. Here are mine, stated so they can fail.

1. **Timing interventions will outperform matched non-timed interventions.** The same food, the same total sleep, the same exercise, delivered on a consistent schedule versus an erratic one, should produce measurably different outcomes.
2. **Measures of variability will outperform measures of level.** Heart rate variability, glucose variability and sleep regularity should predict outcomes better than resting heart rate, fasting glucose and sleep duration respectively. Some of this is already supported.
3. **Interventions will show inverted-U dose responses, not monotonic ones.** More should not always be better, for exercise, cold, heat, fasting, or light. Where a study finds a straight line, either the dose range was too narrow or the framework is wrong.
4. **Combined interventions will outperform the sum of their parts.** If the system is a loop, fixing light and food timing together should beat the sum of fixing each alone.
5. **Restoring rhythm will outperform raising levels.** Specifically for NAD+: restoring a daily rhythm should outperform a constant supply at the same average level. As far as I know, nobody has tested this. It is one of the two predictions I most want to see tested.

### My honest confidence, stated numerically

Because vague confidence is a way of avoiding accountability:

- That the established physiology in Parts I to IV is accurately represented: **very high.**
- That circadian disruption is a major and under-recognized driver of modern chronic disease: **high.**
- That metabolic overload with low demand operates through the mechanism in Chapter 17: **high.**
- That the coherence framing will prove more useful than existing frameworks: **moderate.**
- That the Seasonal Redox-Mismatch Hypothesis in Chapter 15 will be demonstrated in humans: **low to moderate.**
- That ordered water inside the respiratory complexes will turn out to matter for how well those complexes work in a living person, and not only in a structure or a simulation: **moderate.**
- That skin photoreception will turn out to have effects large enough to change what anyone recommends: **low to moderate.**
- That grounding will show a specific effect beyond being outdoors: **low.**
- That Pollack's exclusion zone will prove biologically important in humans: **low.**
- That spin-dependent chemistry will prove to matter for human mitochondrial signaling at ambient field strength: **low.**
- That the full coupled system in the Coda will be demonstrated as a single system rather than as separate parts: **low.** I state it because I think it is the right shape, not because I expect to see it proved in my lifetime.
- That a composite across the five scored dimensions in the Coda will outperform its best single component: **moderate.** This is the one I would most like someone to test, because it needs no new instruments and it could be answered within a year.

I would rather be on record with those numbers than be vague and unfalsifiable.

## About the Author

Dr. Micah Pittman is a second-generation Gonstead chiropractor and the founder of The Human Battery Project.

He was raised in San Diego, in the city and on a working family ranch outside it, where the family raised their own livestock, grew their own produce, and hunted. Homeschooled, he entered college at fifteen and studied cell biology at UCSD.

Before finishing that degree he was invited to Abu Dhabi to help build a United Arab Emirates Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu team, and in 1996 he placed third at the first Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu World Championship in Rio de Janeiro. He trained under Nelson Monteiro in Del Mar, California, won state and national titles, and has owned Jiu-Jitsu studios. He spent roughly five years in Abu Dhabi across that period, training members of the royal family and teaching Filipino martial arts to the military.

He returned to finish his degree, graduating from UCSD in 2000, and earned his chiropractic degree from Southern California University of Health Sciences in 2005. He has been a chiropractor since then. He served for several years as chief executive of a company in Abu Dhabi, and from 2009 built a series of farm-to-table restaurants in Seattle and Bellevue.

Meeting his wife Shiloh and her daughter Kynlee changed the direction of his work. The thirteen years of study that followed, across mitochondrial biology, circadian science and biophysics, became this book and The Human Battery Project.

He practices at 620 Chiropractic in Lakeway, Texas.

## A Note on This Book

Every claim in these pages has been labeled with how much evidence stands behind it. Where I have gone beyond the evidence, I have said so and called it my working model.

I expect some of it to be wrong. The evidence map and the critical evaluation exist so that when a piece of it fails, you can see exactly which piece, and so can I.

This book is not medical advice. It does not diagnose, treat or replace care from a qualified clinician, and nothing in it should be used to stop or change a prescribed treatment.

Genesis appears twice in these pages, in Chapters 4 and 8. It is there as an observation about an ancient text and nothing more. It is not offered as evidence, it does not appear in the evidence map, and no argument in this book depends on it. If you removed both passages, not a single claim would change. I left them in because I think the ordering in that text is remarkable, and because an idea this old deserves to be recognized as old rather than presented as new.
