## The headline finding

Nick Lane gives you the thing you've been missing: **the human battery is not an analogy. It is a literal, measurable voltage.**

Every cell in your body maintains a charge across the inner mitochondrial membrane — roughly 150 to 180 millivolts across a membrane about 5 nanometres thick. Run the arithmetic and that's on the order of **30 million volts per metre**, comparable to the field strength in a lightning strike, sustained continuously, in every mitochondrion you own.

(cite index="91-1">All life runs on proton gradients. Burning food with oxygen pumps H⁺ ions across a membrane, like filling a dam. Those ions flow back through ATP synthase — a molecular turbine — which harnesses the flow to attach phosphate to ADP. Your body contains just 60 grams of ATP, but the cycle is so rapid you process your body weight in ATP daily.</cite>

Sixty grams, cycled through your entire body mass every day. That's the battery, and it's charged and discharged roughly a thousand times over between breakfast and bed.

This reframes your whole brand. You are not saying "think of your body like a battery." You are saying your body **is** a chemiosmotic battery, that its charge is measurable, and that the four subsystems you test are the things that determine how well it holds charge. Peter Mitchell won the Nobel Prize for this in 1978. Nobody can argue with the premise.

## Douglas Wallace — the academic version of your entire thesis

Wallace founded the field of human mitochondrial genetics forty years ago, and his central claim, published in the *Journal of Clinical Investigation*, is the thesis your program is built on:

(cite index="78-1">The classical Mendelian genetic perspective has failed to adequately explain the biology and genetics of common metabolic and degenerative diseases. This is because these diseases are primarily systemic bioenergetic diseases.</cite>

That is your "modern medicine is failing at chronic disease" argument, made by the man who founded the field, in a mainstream journal, in 2013. Cite this rather than the Instagram carousel version. It says the same thing and no one can dismiss it.

Two further pieces worth having:

**Ancient adaptation, present risk.** (cite index="81-1">Indigenous populations carry distinctive mtDNA haplogroups whose founding variants subtly adjusted mitochondrial bioenergetics to let that population adapt to a new environment. Today those same variants alter individual risk for diabetes, obesity, cardiovascular disease, Alzheimer's, Parkinson's and autism.</cite> Different people are running different power plants, tuned for different climates. That's the mechanistic argument for why a single dietary prescription can't work for everyone — and the argument for measuring individuals rather than trusting population averages.

**The ageing clock.** (cite index="81-1">mtDNA mutations accumulate within an individual's cells with age, causing an age-related decline in mitochondrial energy. This is likely the aging clock.</cite> Straight from Wallace. Energy decline *is* ageing, in his framing.

## Becker and Levin — the DC current, and what happened to it

Becker's *The Body Electric* holds up better than most 1985 heterodoxy. His core observations were real: (cite index="102-1">he mapped electric potentials across the body, showing the trunk normally positive and limbs negative; when a salamander limb was amputated, the voltage at the cut went from about −10 mV to +20 mV or more the next day — the current of injury.</cite> He also found bone is piezoelectric, so mechanical load generates its own healing current, which is the physical mechanism underneath Wolff's law.

And it didn't stay fringe. (cite index="99-1">Colin McCaig later showed the electric potentials naturally arising in wounds are critical for healing, and EMF-based bone and soft tissue repair therapies were developed from this line of work.</cite> Electrical bone growth stimulators are FDA-cleared devices sitting in orthopaedic clinics today.

The modern successor is Michael Levin at Tufts — who, incidentally, (cite index="104-1">picked up *The Body Electric* in a bookstore at seventeen and it set the direction of his career</cite>. Levin's claim is the strong version, and it's published: (cite index="99-1">patterns of bioelectric signalling constitute an autonomous layer of control not reducible to a biochemical or genetic account of cell state.</cite> His lab has (cite index="96-1">demonstrated that voltage gradients act as a kind of cognitive glue letting cells make collective decisions about growth and form, and has induced regeneration-like processes in frogs through targeted bioelectric intervention.</cite>

**What this means for you.** There is a real, published, funded science of the body as an electrical system, and it's called bioelectricity — not biofield, not energy medicine. Use Levin's vocabulary and you're in a live research field. Use Becker's later EMF-pollution chapters and you're back in the same trap as Kruse's nnEMF material.

The honest caution: none of this gives you an intervention. There is no validated way to "rebuild the body's DC current" through lifestyle. What the protocol actually does is supply the substrate and conditions that let normal bioelectric function work — membrane lipids, mineral status, mitochondrial capacity. That's a defensible claim. "We restore your DC current" is not.

## Van Wijk and the biophotons — the weakest tier

*Light in Shaping Life: Biophotons in Biology and Medicine* by Roeland Van Wijk, 2014, is the serious book on this and the one to read if you go here.

The phenomenon is real and old. (cite index="111-1">Popp observed in 1976 that living organisms emit ultraweak photons in the 200–800 nm range, and these emissions appeared more organised than simple metabolic byproducts.</cite> Ultraweak photon emission is measurable and published.

Two problems for your purposes. First, the mainstream interpretation is that these are chemiluminescent byproducts of oxidative metabolism — real light, but exhaust rather than signal. The coherence and information-carrying claims are not established. Second, and more practically, the field is heavily contaminated. The search results on this topic run directly from Popp into quantum vacuum and biofield material within a paragraph.

**My recommendation: read it, don't cite it.** If ultraweak photon emission ever becomes a measurable biomarker it would be extraordinary for you — a direct optical readout of metabolic state. It isn't one yet, and there's no instrument you can put in front of a client.

## The revised stack

Your model now has a spine that runs from physics to protocol:

| Layer | Mechanism | Authority | Your marker |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The battery itself** | Proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, ~30 million V/m | Mitchell (Nobel 1978), Lane | Indirect — the whole panel |
| **The plant** | mtDNA variation and somatic mutation set individual bioenergetic capacity | Wallace | Not yet measured |
| **The wiring** | Bioelectric gradients as an autonomous control layer | Becker → Levin | Not measured |
| **The membrane** | Cardiolipin and DHA determine whether the machinery assembles and conducts | Crawford, cardiolipin literature | **Omega-3 Index, AA:EPA** |
| **The input** | Light entrains the system; DHA builds the receiver | Crawford, circadian literature | **Vitamin D, logged light exposure** |
| **The output** | What's left to spend | Standard endocrinology | **The Output subsystem** |
| **The drain** | What consumes capacity before you use it | Standard inflammation literature | **The Drain subsystem** |

Two rows have no marker. That's not a flaw — it's honest, and it's where a version 2 goes.

**Wallace's row is the interesting gap.** mtDNA haplogroup is a one-time cheap test. It never changes, it explains why two clients on identical protocols get different results, and it's the kind of variable that makes a longitudinal dataset far more valuable. Not for cohort 01. Worth designing the schema to accept.

## Reading list, in the order I'd read it

1. **Nick Lane — *Transformer*** (2022). Start here. The Krebs cycle as the engine of both life and death, written for people who aren't biochemists. This is the book that gives you the language for the battery.
2. **Nick Lane — *The Vital Question*** (2015). Why all life runs on proton gradients, and why complex life happened exactly once. Denser. Read second.
3. **Nick Lane — *Power, Sex, Suicide*** (2005). Mitochondria specifically. The oldest of the three and still the best single mitochondria book.
4. **Douglas Wallace — "A mitochondrial bioenergetic etiology of disease"** (*J Clin Invest*, 2013). Not a book — twelve pages, free online. The single most useful citation you will get for the thesis of your program.
5. **Robert Becker — *The Body Electric*** (1985). Read parts one to three for the regeneration and current-of-injury work. Treat the later EMF chapters as historical.
6. **Michael Levin's published work.** The modern version of what Becker was reaching for, with actual mechanisms.
7. **Jim Al-Khalili & Johnjoe McFadden — *Life on the Edge*** (2014). The mainstream quantum biology book. Enzyme tunnelling, avian magnetoreception, photosynthesis. Gives you a calibrated sense of what "quantum biology" actually means in the literature, which is narrower and stranger than the wellness usage.
8. **Roeland Van Wijk — *Light in Shaping Life*** (2014). The biophoton book. Read last, and read it knowing it's the least settled thing on this list.
