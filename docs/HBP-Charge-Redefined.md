# Charge, Redefined

## Making the first subsystem mean what Nick Lane means

---

## You are right about the biology

Food enters the mitochondrion. Carbon-hydrogen bonds are stripped of their electrons. Those electrons enter the transport chain and their energy is used to pump protons across the inner membrane, building the gradient. The electrons end at complex IV, where they combine with oxygen and protons to make **water**. The carbons come off through the Krebs cycle as **CO₂**.

Food in. Electrons, water, CO₂ out, and a voltage held across a membrane in between.

That is the reaction. And you are right that we can measure it — just not the way a blood panel measures things.

---

## What can and cannot be measured

**Cannot be measured in a living person:** the membrane potential itself. ΔΨm requires isolated mitochondria and a fluorescent dye. Nobody is doing that on a client, and anyone claiming to measure your mitochondrial voltage directly is not.

**Can be measured, directly and cheaply:** the throughput of the reaction. Oxygen going in, CO₂ coming out, and how much work the system can sustain before it falls back on anaerobic metabolism.

That is not a proxy in the weak sense. Oxygen consumption *is* the terminal step of the electron transport chain. If your mitochondria are pumping protons, they are consuming oxygen, and the rate at which they can do it is the ceiling on your oxidative capacity.

---

## The new Charge: two layers

### Layer one — oxidative throughput (the reaction itself)

| Test | What it measures | Why it belongs here |
|---|---|---|
| **VO₂max / cardiorespiratory fitness** | Maximum oxygen your body can take in, transport and use | The integrated ceiling on mitochondrial oxidative capacity. The terminal step of the ETC, measured whole-body. |
| **RER / respiratory exchange ratio** | CO₂ produced ÷ O₂ consumed | Literally the two outputs of the reaction you described. Tells you which fuel is being burned. |
| **Metabolic flexibility** | RER shift, fasted versus fed | Whether the system can switch between fat and carbohydrate — a direct read on mitochondrial adaptability. |
| **Lactate at a fixed workload** | Where oxidative capacity runs out | When the chain cannot keep up, pyruvate goes to lactate instead of into the mitochondrion. That threshold is the functional edge of the system. |

### Layer two — fuel delivery (upstream of the mitochondrion)

Everything currently in Charge — fasting glucose, fasting insulin, HbA1c, HOMA-IR, triglycerides, the triglyceride:HDL ratio.

These are not the charge. They are whether fuel reaches the machinery in a usable form. Insulin resistance means fuel is present and cannot get in. That is a delivery failure, not a capacity failure, and distinguishing the two is exactly the kind of thing your program should be able to do.

**Two people with identical HbA1c can have completely different oxidative capacity.** The panel alone cannot tell them apart. Adding layer one can.

---

## Why this is worth the added cost

**It is the strongest mortality data in the entire program.** Mandsager's 2018 analysis of 122,007 adults at the Cleveland Clinic found cardiorespiratory fitness was the most powerful predictor of survival they measured — the least-fit had roughly five times the death risk of the most fit, and there was no upper limit where the benefit stopped. Blair's earlier work established low fitness as a stronger mortality predictor than smoking, hypertension or high cholesterol. Nothing on your blood panel comes close.

**It is a functional outcome, not a lab value.** Grip strength was already giving you one non-blood measure. This gives you a second and a far better one — and it is the one clients will *feel* change. Someone whose VO₂max moves from 28 to 34 knows it on the stairs.

**It fixes the Lane collision.** Charge now means what Lane means: how much charge this system can generate, measured by the oxygen it consumes doing it. The blood markers become the fuel-delivery layer beneath it, which is what they actually are.

**And it moves in ninety days.** Aerobic capacity responds to training faster than almost anything else on the panel.

---

## The honest caveats

**The big mortality studies mostly measured METs from treadmill protocols, not breath-by-breath VO₂max.** They are closely related but not identical, and the public conversation has blurred them. If you cite the five-times figure, cite it as cardiorespiratory fitness, which is what was measured.

**Consumer wearable VO₂max estimates are not clinical-grade.** A watch estimate has meaningful error and drifts with heart rate variability, temperature and altitude. Useful for tracking an individual over time, not for comparing across people.

**A true test costs money the participant pays.** A lab VO₂max with gas exchange runs roughly $150 to $300. That is a real addition to an already unbundled cost, on top of two blood draws.

---

## The practical version for a 25-person cohort

Three tiers, pick by what people will actually do:

**Minimum, free.** A standardised submaximal step or bike protocol with a heart-rate monitor, done at home under instruction, at day 0 and day 90. Estimated VO₂max plus heart-rate recovery. Not lab-grade, but consistent within a person across two tests, which is what you need for a delta.

**Better, about $200.** A lab test with gas exchange gives you true VO₂max *and* RER, which means you get the CO₂-to-O₂ ratio — the actual output of the reaction — not just the ceiling.

**Best, adds about $250 in equipment shared across the cohort.** Add a handheld lactate meter and take a reading at a fixed workload. That gives you the point where oxidative capacity runs out, which is arguably the most sensitive marker of mitochondrial function available outside a research lab.

I would run the minimum for cohort 01 and offer the lab test as an option. Consistency between the two timepoints matters more than absolute accuracy for what you are trying to show.

---

## The line for the site

> **Charge is not a metaphor for energy. It is the reaction: food in, electrons stripped, protons pumped, oxygen consumed, water and CO₂ out.**
>
> Nobody can measure that voltage directly in a living person. What we measure is the throughput — how much oxygen your system can move, what fuel it burns, and where it runs out. Plus the blood markers that tell us whether fuel is even reaching the machinery.
>
> That last part is what most panels stop at. It is the fuel gauge, not the engine.
