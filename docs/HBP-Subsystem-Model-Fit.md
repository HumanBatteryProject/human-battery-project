# Do the Four Subsystems Actually Fit the Model?

*Charge, Drain, Output, Reserve, tested against Lane, Wallace, Kruse, Crawford, Pollack, Van Wijk.*

---

## Short answer

The four subsystems are a **measurement taxonomy**, not a mechanistic map. They describe what a blood panel can see. The deeper model describes what is physically happening. Those are different things, and three of the six thinkers land cleanly on the four while three do not land on them at all.

That is not a flaw, but pretending otherwise is. Here is the honest mapping.

---

## Where it fits well

**Wallace fits almost perfectly.** His thesis is that common metabolic and degenerative diseases are systemic bioenergetic diseases. Your four subsystems are four ways of asking how much energy the system can make and hold. Drain in particular, inflammatory load as capacity consumed, is Wallace's argument stated as a measurement. He is the closest thing you have to academic cover for the entire structure.

**Crawford fits through Reserve.** DHA as structural material for the receiving apparatus is exactly what Reserve means: what the machinery is built from rather than how it is performing. The Omega-3 Index sitting in Reserve is a direct instantiation of his argument.

**Cardiolipin and membrane composition fit through Reserve too**, for the same reason. Substrate quality determines whether the machinery assembles.

---

## Where the fit is imperfect

**Lane creates a naming collision, and it is a real one.**

In Lane's framework, "charge" means the proton-motive force, the actual voltage across the inner mitochondrial membrane. That is the thing that is literally charged.

In your framework, "Charge" means glucose and insulin handling. In Lane's terms that is not charge at all. It is **fuel delivery**, substrate arriving at the electron transport chain. The charge is what the chain then builds, and you do not measure it, because nobody can measure ΔΨm from a blood draw.

Anyone who has read *Transformer* will notice this within about ten seconds of seeing your panel.

Two ways to handle it, and both are defensible:

- **Own the distinction.** "We cannot measure the voltage directly. Nobody can, outside a lab with isolated mitochondria. What we can measure is how well fuel gets converted into it, which is what Charge means here." That is honest, it is correct, and it demonstrates you know the difference. It is also a good line.
- **Rename it.** "Fuel," "Intake," or "Conversion" would be mechanistically cleaner. But you would lose the battery language in the one place it works hardest, and the mark has four colours already assigned. I would not do this.

I would take the first option and put that sentence in the science panel.

**Wallace's capacity layer is unmeasured.** His mtDNA argument says two people can run identical protocols and get different results because their power plants have different inherent capacity. Nothing in your four subsystems captures that. It is a genuine hole in the model, and it is the honest reason two clients will diverge on the same protocol. Worth naming rather than papering over.

---

## Where it does not fit at all

**Kruse's model is light-first. Your four subsystems have no input category.**

This is the most substantive gap. In Kruse's framework, and in Crawford's, and in the circadian literature generally, light is the primary input to the system. Your four subsystems all measure the *state* of the battery. None of them measure what is going *into* it.

Light appears in your panel only obliquely, through vitamin D in Reserve. Meanwhile light exposure is arguably the single most emphasised element of your protocol.

**Pollack and Ling have no measurable proxy.** Structured water, cell hydration, the association-induction model. None of it produces a marker you can put on a panel. Same for Van Wijk and biophotons: ultraweak photon emission is measurable in a laboratory and there is no instrument you can put in front of a client.

---

## The resolution, and it is already built

The gap is real but the architecture already answers it. You have **two measurement axes**, not one:

| | What it measures | How often | What it maps to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The four subsystems** | The *state* of the battery, charge conversion, drain, output, substrate | Twice, at day 0 and day 90 | Wallace, Crawford, cardiolipin, standard endocrinology |
| **The five behavioural domains** | The *inputs* to the battery, light and sleep timing, movement, connection and purpose, cognitive load, emotional load | Every day | Kruse's light-first ordering, circadian literature, the behavioural epidemiology |

Light is not missing from the model. It lives in the daily log rather than the blood panel, which is correct. Light exposure is an input you control, not a state you measure. The panel tells you where the battery is. The log tells you what you are putting into it.

That also protects the mark. Four spheres, four subsystems. Adding a fifth blood category to hold light would break the logo and would put an input in a state taxonomy, which is the wrong shape anyway.

---

## What I would change

**One thing, and it is small.** State the two-axis structure explicitly on the site. Right now the four subsystems are prominent and the five behavioural domains are buried in the model section. Someone reading carefully will ask where light went, and the answer is good. It should not require asking.

**One sentence to add to the science panel**, because it is the sharpest thing in this analysis:

> The proton gradient itself cannot be measured from a blood draw. Charge here means how well fuel is converted into it, the input side of the reaction, not the voltage. Nobody can measure the voltage in a living human, which is precisely why nobody has been measuring anything.

**And one honest admission worth keeping:** Pollack, Ling and Van Wijk contribute nothing measurable to this program. They shape how the protocol was designed. They do not appear in the score, and they should not be presented as though they do.

---

## The summary line

**The four subsystems measure the battery. The five daily domains measure what you put into it. The deeper physics explains why both matter and is not something a blood test can see.**

That is three sentences, it is true, and it survives a reader who knows the literature better than you do.
