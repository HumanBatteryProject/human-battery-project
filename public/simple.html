The Human Battery

Here is the part nobody told you. You are not like a battery. You are one.

Inside every cell in your body there are tiny power plants. There are hundreds of them in a single cell. Trillions of them in you.

Each one does the same thing. It pushes tiny charged pieces to one side of a wall. That builds up a charge, exactly the way a battery does. Then it lets them rush back through, and it uses that rush to make fuel.

That is real electricity. In you. Right now. While you read this.

A man named Peter Mitchell figured this out. He won the Nobel Prize for it in 1978.

So this is not a nice way of talking about how you feel. It is how your body actually works. Nobody argues about it.

Here is what is strange. You only hold about two ounces of that fuel at a time. Two ounces. But you use it up and make it again all day long. By the time you go to bed, you have made and burned your whole body weight in it.

Your battery gets charged and drained about a thousand times a day.

And in your whole life, nobody has ever checked it.

They checked your blood pressure. They checked your cholesterol. Somebody probably checked your ears. Nobody checked the thing that runs everything else.

That is what we do.

SHOW ME THE SCIENCE

The mechanism is chemiosmosis. The electron transport chain pumps protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, establishing a proton-motive force with an electrical component of roughly 150 to 180 millivolts. Across a membrane approximately 5 nanometres thick, that is a field strength on the order of 30 million volts per metre, comparable to a lightning strike, sustained continuously in every mitochondrion in the body.

ATP synthase harnesses the return flow of protons to phosphorylate ADP. The body contains only about 60 grams of ATP at any moment, but turnover is rapid enough to process roughly body weight in ATP daily.

Peter Mitchell received the 1978 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the chemiosmotic hypothesis. Nick Lane's The Vital Question and Transformer are the accessible accounts. Douglas Wallace's 2013 paper in the Journal of Clinical Investigation makes the case that common metabolic and degenerative diseases are, at root, systemic bioenergetic diseases.

None of this is fringe. What is unusual is not the biology. It is that nobody measures it.

These complaints are not a diagnostic list, and any one of them can have a specific medical cause that needs a physician. What they share is that each maps onto a measurable domain of physiological function rather than a discrete disease process.

Afternoon fatigue, central adiposity and reactive hunger cluster around glucose and insulin handling. Cognitive fog, arthralgia without injury, and new-onset anxiety cluster around inflammatory and oxidative load. Disrupted sleep architecture, cold intolerance, low libido and flattened drive cluster around thyroid and steroid hormone status. Poor recovery, frequent infection and integumentary changes cluster around substrate adequacy: iron, B12, vitamin D, magnesium, protein status and membrane fatty acid composition.

That clustering is the reason the panel is read as four subsystems rather than thirty-three individual results.

Some of these can be caused by a real medical problem. We do not diagnose anything. If a test comes back off, we tell you to go see a doctor.

Four things we check

Think about your phone battery. Four things matter: how fast it charges, how fast it drains, how much power it can give you, and what it is made of. Your body works the same way.

Charge

How much power you can actually make. Food goes in. Oxygen goes in. Water and carbon dioxide come out. That is where your energy comes from. We test how much of it you can do.

Drain

What is using up your energy in the background. Like an app running when you are not looking. This shows up as foggy thinking and aches.

Output

How much power you can actually use. This is the part that decides if you feel like doing things. Low output feels like no drive and bad sleep.

Reserve

What your battery is built out of. If you are short on the raw stuff, nothing else works well no matter how hard you try.

We take blood and check 33 things. Then we sort them into these four groups and give you one score for each. Then a single number for the whole battery.

SHOW ME THE SCIENCE

Charge: fasting glucose, fasting insulin, HbA1c, HOMA-IR, triglycerides, triglyceride:HDL ratio.

Drain: hs-CRP, GGT, ALT, AST, uric acid, homocysteine, white cell count, AA:EPA ratio, kynurenine:tryptophan ratio.

Output: TSH, free T3, free T4, total and free testosterone, DHEA-S, morning cortisol, IGF-1, SHBG.

Reserve: Omega-3 Index, 25-OH vitamin D, ferritin, iron saturation, B12, folate, RBC magnesium, albumin, HDL.

Each marker is scored 0 to 100 against a target band rather than against the laboratory's population reference range. A result at the edge of "normal" scores near 50. That is deliberate, and it is the difference between a screening panel and a capacity measurement. Markers absent from a panel are excluded rather than imputed. Sex-specific bands apply to testosterone, DHEA-S, ferritin and SHBG.

Subsystem scores are averaged within group, then combined with weights of 0.30 Charge, 0.25 Drain, 0.25 Output, 0.20 Reserve. The methodology is versioned so a score computed today remains interpretable years from now.

How it works

You get your blood drawn

Before you start. This is your day one number. You go to a lab near you and pay them directly.

You follow the plan for 90 days

What to eat. When to eat. When to get sunlight. When to sleep. How to move. We give you all of it, one piece at a time.

You check in every day

On your phone. Takes less than a minute. Tap what you ate, what you did, how you slept. If you had a bad day, you say so. Nobody yells at you.

We talk every week

Once with the whole group. Once just you and a coach.

You get your blood drawn again

At day 90. Then we sit down and look at both sets of numbers side by side and see what actually changed.

SHOW ME THE SCIENCE

The 90-day interval is not arbitrary. Red blood cells live roughly 90 to 120 days, which is why HbA1c reflects a three-month glycaemic average and why the Omega-3 Index, measured in red cell membranes, captures most of the change dietary intervention can produce over that window.

Draw conditions are standardised at both timepoints: twelve-hour fast, no alcohol for 48 hours, no training for 24 hours, and the same hour of the morning. Cortisol and testosterone both vary substantially across the morning; a 7am draw compared against an 11am draw produces an apparent change that is an artefact of timing.

The daily log is structured rather than free text. Foods and exercises are recorded against controlled reference lists with stable identifiers, sleep and light as timestamps rather than durations. Free text exists as one optional note field. This is what makes the data analysable later instead of a corpus of sentences.

Adherence is tracked as a percentage rather than a streak, because a broken streak causes people to disengage entirely while a percentage invites improvement.

What we are not going to tell you

We do not know what your numbers will do. Nobody does. That is the whole reason we test twice.

Everyone in a group does the same plan at the same time. So if you feel better, we cannot prove it was the plan and not just having someone pay attention to you for three months. We are not going to pretend otherwise.

What we can do is show you exactly what changed in your blood between day one and day 90. Almost nobody has that. Most people never get told a single number about themselves twice.

Anyone in this business who says they are certain is selling you something. We would rather just measure.

SHOW ME THE SCIENCE

This is a single-arm pre/post cohort with n=30 and no control group. It is exploratory and hypothesis-generating by design. Observed changes cannot be attributed to the intervention alone: coaching attention, seasonal variation, individual circumstance and regression to the mean all contribute, and none of them can be separated without a comparison arm.

A relevant cautionary example: a 2024 randomised trial of the Wim Hof Method in 84 women with high depressive symptoms found roughly 24% improvement in the intervention group, and an equivalent improvement in the active control group receiving warm showers and slow breathing. Both worked. Which means neither was shown to work for the reason claimed.

Results from these cohorts will be reported descriptively, with effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than significance testing on a panel of this size. Any claim beyond "this is what changed in these people" would not survive review, and will not be made.

Whose work this is built on

We did not come up with the science. We put other people's work together and turned it into something you can actually do.

Here is who we learned it from. None of them work with us. Some of them would argue with each other. It is only right to say their names.

How the battery works

Peter Mitchell · Nick Lane · Douglas Wallace

Mitchell figured out that all life runs on tiny electric charges and won the Nobel Prize. Lane wrote the books that explain it. Wallace showed that a lot of long-term illness is really an energy problem.

Fats and cell walls

Michael Crawford · William Harris · Clemens von Schacky

Crawford spent his career on the fat in your eyes and brain. Harris and von Schacky built the blood test that tells you how much of it you have.

Sunlight and sleep

Richard Weller · Pelle Lindqvist · Russell Foster

Weller found that sunlight lowers blood pressure in a way vitamin D does not explain. Lindqvist followed 29,000 women for 20 years and found the ones who avoided the sun did worse. Foster found how light sets your body clock.

Water inside cells

Gilbert Ling · Gerald Pollack

Both argued the water in your cells is more organised than people think. Both are argued with. They changed how we think about the body. They are not part of what we test.

Electricity in the body

Robert Becker · Michael Levin · Colin McCaig

Becker showed a cut generates its own electric current while it heals. Levin at Tufts turned that into a real field of study.

Quantum biology

Jim Al-Khalili · Johnjoe McFadden · Philip Kurian · Clarice Aiello · Gregory Engel · Greg Scholes · Thorsten Ritz · Nigel Scrutton · Alexandra Olaya-Castro · Vlatko Vedral

These are physicists and biologists studying how living things handle light, energy and electrons at the smallest scale there is. Kurian's work is closest to what we are doing.

The tiny structures inside cells

Roger Penrose · Stuart Hameroff · Jiří Pokorný · Jack Tuszynski · Travis Craddock · Anirban Bandyopadhyay · Aristide Dogariu

They study whether the scaffolding inside your cells moves energy and light around. Some of this is settled. Some of it is a long way from settled.

Light coming out of living things

Fritz-Albert Popp · Roeland Van Wijk · Michal Cifra

Living tissue gives off a very faint light. That part is true. What it means is still being worked out.

Ageing, cold and heat

David Sinclair · Wim Hof · Matthijs Kox · Peter Pickkers · Jari Laukkanen

Sinclair on ageing. Hof on cold. Kox and Pickkers ran the study that tested him properly. Laukkanen on saunas. All of it is argued about, and we say so.

What really predicts how long you live

Julianne Holt-Lunstad · Steven Blair · Kyle Mandsager · Darryl Leong

Being connected to people. Being fit. Being strong. This is the plainest evidence in the whole model and it is also the strongest.

And the people who say the others are wrong

Max Tegmark · Arieh Warshel · Matt Kaeberlein · Daniel Elton · Charles Brenner · James Timmons

We read these people too. When they are right, we say they are right. That is the part most people leave out.
