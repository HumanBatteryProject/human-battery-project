What is The Human Battery Project

Your body is made of trillions of tiny batteries.

Low energy. Poor sleep. Brain fog. Stubborn weight. Trouble focusing. Slow to recover. Some of these may be because your batteries are not charging properly, or are losing charge.

A 90-day program. We measure your batteries with a blood panel and fitness tests. You follow a simple daily plan built on light, water, movement, food and sleep. At day 90 we measure again and show you what changed.

We draw blood at day 0 and again at day 90, thirty-three markers both times, through a partner laboratory near you. In between you follow a written protocol covering food, training, light, sleep, cold and heat. You log each day in your own portal. At the end you see both sets of numbers side by side.

Here is why it exists. Most of those markers already show up on an ordinary blood panel. They get reported as a wall of numbers you are told are normal. We read them as four subsystems and score each against a target band instead, because a result sitting at the edge of normal is exactly the person this program is for.

Start with the thing in your hand

You have been holding a battery all day, so you already understand this. Your phone tells you when it is at 20 percent. You have never once known what you are at. A bad cable charges a phone slowly. Your body runs on food and sleep, and light is the signal that times them. Apps you never opened keep running in the background, and in your body that is inflammation, using power all day until you notice you are at 15 percent by two in the afternoon. After a few years a phone says its maximum capacity is 78 percent, so it charges to full and still dies by lunch. Cheap material makes a battery that fades, and yours is built from what you eat. Your phone shows you the number. We are going to show you yours, twice, ninety days apart.

This is not a comparison

It is real electricity, and it is running right now.

The voltage is roughly 150 millivolts across a membrane about five nanometres thick. Work that out and it is near thirty million volts per meter, lightning-strike field strength, held continuously, in every mitochondrion in your body.

You carry only about sixty grams of that fuel at any moment. But you make and burn roughly your own body weight in it every day.

The Human Battery

You are not like a battery. You are trillions of them. Not as a figure of speech but as a matter of physics.

Inside every cell you have hundreds of tiny power plants. Each one pushes charged particles across a membrane to build up a voltage, then lets them rush back through and uses that flow to make fuel. It is a battery, charging and discharging, and there are trillions of them in you right now.

Coherence: they have to stay in step

Trillions of batteries cannot each run on their own terms. A neuron, a muscle cell and an immune cell are built to hold different voltages, and they are all supposed to hold them while staying synchronised with the tissue around them and with the day outside.

That is coherence: cells keeping their proper electrical identity while coordinating their voltage, metabolism, timing and behavior with the tissue around them and with the whole organism.

Lose it and the symptoms arrive long before a standard panel flags anything. Tired at the wrong hours, wired at night, slow to recover. The circadian protocol is how we restore the timing, and the daily log is how we track whether it is holding.

Peter Mitchell worked this out and won the Nobel Prize for it in 1978. It is not a theory and it is not new. It is simply not something anybody measures.

Four things decide how well that battery works: how well you make charge, what drains it in the background, how much you can actually spend, and what the whole thing is built from. Those four are what we test.

Charge is the only one that is not just blood. Food enters the mitochondrion, electrons are stripped from it, protons get pumped across a membrane, and oxygen combines with them at the end to make water. Carbon leaves as CO2. That reaction is what builds the voltage.

Nobody can measure that voltage directly in a living person. What we can measure is the throughput: how much oxygen you move, which fuel you burn, and where the system runs out. Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the strongest independent predictors of all cause mortality in healthy adults, and the association is graded: each increment of fitness carries a measurably lower risk.7

The blood markers sit underneath that. They tell us whether fuel is even reaching the machinery. That is a fuel gauge, not an engine test, and most panels stop there.

Two of the blood markers are worth singling out. The Omega-3 Index is the most responsive marker on the panel. Red cell membranes turn over in about four months, so ninety days captures most of the change a diet can produce. And free testosterone at 450 means something entirely different in a man than in a woman; the scoring knows the difference.

Your body runs on electrochemical gradients

Every cell keeps a voltage across its membranes. Food is taken apart, electrons are passed down a chain, and protons are pumped to one side of a membrane inside the mitochondrion. Letting those protons back through is what makes ATP. That is the mechanism Peter Mitchell proposed in 1961, and it is how the energy in your body is actually made.1

The phone battery is an analogy. The voltage is not. A phone has one cell with one charge you can read off a screen. You have trillions of cells, each holding its own gradient, and no instrument reads them as a single number. When this site says battery, it means the analogy. When it says gradient or membrane potential, it means the physical thing.

Where that machinery runs badly, the consequences are not confined to one organ. Defects in oxidative phosphorylation have been proposed as a common thread under a range of degenerative conditions.3 This is a paradigm rather than a settled finding, which is why it sits in the contested tier and not the established one.

Light sets the clock, and does a little chemistry

Light does two jobs. The larger one is timing. A separate class of cell in the retina carries light to the clock in your hypothalamus, working without rods and without cones, and its job is to tell the clock what time it is.4 That signal sets when everything else runs, and it is what the protocol is built around.

The smaller job is real chemistry in your skin. Ultraviolet light converts a precursor in skin into previtamin D3, which is where your vitamin D comes from.2 The same light releases nitric oxide stored in skin, which widens arteries and lowers blood pressure.8 So light is a real energy input, and a small one.

Your skin absorbs a great deal of solar energy, and almost all of it becomes heat. You have no machinery for turning photons into ATP. Absorbing energy and converting it into biological work are different things, and that difference is why we do not tell you the sun charges you the way a charger fills a phone.

Follow people for long enough and sun exposure habits track with how long they live. In a Swedish cohort followed for two decades, women who avoided the sun had higher all cause mortality than those who did not.9 That is observational. It cannot separate sunlight from everything that travels with it, and it is filed as contested for exactly that reason.

The blood pressure half of that is one small trial, so it is contested rather than settled. The vitamin D half is not: skin photosynthesis has been established since 1980.

Red and near-infrared light come up often here, so it is worth saying where they sit. In the laboratory those wavelengths interact with cytochrome c oxidase in the respiratory chain, and studies report changes in mitochondrial enzyme activity under narrow conditions of wavelength, dose and distance.6 That work is contested, most of it is cell culture or device work rather than sunlight, and it is not part of this program. Nothing on your panel measures it. It is named here because it is the honest edge of the timing claim, not because it is something you will be doing.

What we measure, and why those things

The Omega-3 Index is EPA and DHA as a percentage of the fatty acids in your red cell membranes. It is a defined measurement with a literature behind it, not an index invented here.5 Red cells turn over across about four months, so ninety days captures most of the change diet can produce.

VO2max is on the panel because cardiorespiratory fitness predicts all cause mortality across large pooled populations.7 It is the one measurement here that tests the machinery under load rather than sampling it at rest.

The model, and how sure we are of each part

The program is built on a working theory of how the body captures, stores and spends energy. Some of that theory is settled science. Some is actively argued over. Some is frontier speculation that shaped how we designed the protocol. We tell you which is which.

The quantum floor

Tunnelling in enzymes, spin chemistry in photoreceptors, light-handling networks in the cytoskeleton. Real physics, running at timescales far below anything a ninety-day protocol touches. It is part of why the rest works, not something we can move.

Ninety days, in three phases

Each phase has one job. You do not get the whole protocol on day one, because nobody sustains that. You get the next thing.

Days 1-30 Stop the drain

Remove what is costing you capacity before adding anything that builds it. Sugar and alcohol come out, sleep gets a fixed window, morning light becomes non-negotiable, and you start logging every day. Most people feel the first change here, and it is usually sleep.

Days 31-60 Recharge

Now the inputs. Protein target, real training on a schedule you can hold, cold and heat exposure, and a meal timing window that lines up with your own clock rather than someone else's. The daily log keeps running, and the weekly call is where we adjust it.

Days 61-90 Build capacity

Load goes up, and the work shifts from repair to headroom. Day 90 ends with the second blood draw and a session comparing it against day 0, not to celebrate but to decide what you keep doing for the next ten years.

Questions

What exactly is the Human Battery Project?

A ninety-day measured program. Thirty-three blood markers plus physical capacity testing at day 0, a written protocol in three phases, and the same measurements repeated at day 90 under the same conditions.

Is this medical treatment?

No. The Human Battery Project is a health coaching and research program. It does not diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any condition, and nothing in it replaces care from a licensed physician.

What gets measured?

Thirty-three blood markers across four subsystems, plus VO2max and grip strength. The same panel and the same physical tests at day 0 and day 90.

Why 90 days?

Red cells live about 120 days. HbA1c reflects roughly three months of glycaemia and the Omega-3 Index reflects roughly four months of intake, so ninety days captures most of the change diet can produce in either without requiring a complete turnover.

What is the Battery Score?

A composite index over the four subsystems, each marker scored against a target band rather than the laboratory reference range. It is this program's measurement framework and is not a validated diagnostic instrument.

What happens on Day 0?

Blood draw on a physician-signed requisition at a partner laboratory, VO2max and grip testing, and your baseline Battery Score across all four subsystems.

What happens on Day 90?

The same panel and the same physical tests, repeated under matched conditions, followed by a review comparing day 0 against day 90 marker by marker.
